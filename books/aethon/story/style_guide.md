## Narrative Voice & Tone

Restrained and observational — the prose maintains a deliberate emotional distance that paradoxically creates intimacy. The narrator watches characters from just inside their heads, noting without editorializing. The tone is dry, precise, occasionally wry, but never cold.

> *"He was not a tall man but he stood like one — solidly, without apology, taking up exactly the amount of space he intended to."*
> *"The drawer was closed."*

## Dialogue Style

Dialogue is clipped, asymmetric, and loaded with subtext. Characters rarely complete explanations — they speak in fragments, deflections, and acknowledgements that substitute for full sentences. Verbal tics include the repeated single-word confirmation ("I know," "Fine," "Yes") used as a complete response that carries more than it says. Exchanges often end on the shorter speaker's line, not the longer one's.

> *"Elbow off the table," Maren said. / He put his elbow back on the table, because it hadn't been on the table in the first place..."*
> *"We'll both get in," he said. / It wasn't a question."*

## Scene Description

Sensory detail is selective and functional — smell and sound dominate over visual spectacle. Settings are described through what they *mean* to the character rather than what they look like objectively. Description density is low-to-medium; the author resists accumulation, preferring one precise image over three approximate ones. Emotional states are embedded in environmental observation rather than stated directly.

> *"The porridge was the colour of winter mornings and smelled like honey and something faintly smoky."*
> *"The packed earth gave way to fitted stone. The buildings stopped leaning into each other and stood upright, properly spaced, with the specific confidence of structures that had never needed to make room."*

## Transitions & Connective Technique

Scene breaks use the em-dash-and-space triple rule (`---`) as a hard cut, with no transitional summary. Time jumps are handled by dropping the reader into the next moment mid-action, often with a single orienting sensory detail. Paragraph-to-paragraph transitions frequently work by *echo* — the last word or image of one paragraph is picked up or answered by the first of the next, creating a quiet chain.

> *[End of scene: "Neither of them moved for another minute."] [Start of next beat: "Then Daran stood up and said he was going to be late for dinner..."]*

Within scenes, transitions between interiority and action are unmarked — the prose slides from observation to thought to action without signaling the shift.

## Pacing

The text alternates between long, clause-heavy sentences that accumulate detail and very short declarative sentences (sometimes single words or fragments) that land like full stops. Climaxes are handled with *compression* — the most significant moments get the fewest words. Lulls are given space: ordinary domestic scenes run long, building texture before anything happens. Paragraph length is short-to-medium throughout; walls of text are avoided. The rhythm is: medium, medium, long, short. Short. Medium.

> *"It built. / And then his hand moved."*
> *"He did not look back. / The road was there. They were on it."*

## Diction

Signature constructions include "the specific [noun] of [description]" (used constantly to name unnamed social or sensory phenomena), "the particular [noun/quality] of" (same function), and "the kind of [X] that [Y]" as a definitional move. Figurative language is restrained and almost always grounded in physical sensation or spatial metaphor. Colloquialism is minimal but present in dialogue. The prose favors Anglo-Saxon monosyllables in emotional beats and longer Latinate constructions in analytical passages.

> *"the specific ease of someone who had walked a long way and intended to walk further and had made a private peace with both facts"*
> *"the particular thoroughness of someone who did not get many visitors and was going to make the most of the ones he had"*

## Emotional Expression

Almost entirely externalized — interior monologue is rare and brief. Emotion is rendered through action, physical sensation, spatial behavior (where characters stand, whether they move), and what characters *don't* say. When interiority does appear, it is clipped and analytical rather than lyrical, often framed as filing, categorizing, or deferring.

> *"He put it in the same place he put everything he didn't have language for yet, the place that held all the things he was going to deal with when he had more information."*
> *"The absence was not a thought. It was not a name. It was more like the awareness of a space where a sound had been and was not anymore."*

## Distinctive Habits

**The negative inventory**: the author frequently lists what a character is *not* thinking about, which functions as a way of thinking about it — the denial enacts the presence.
> *"He was not thinking about the exam... He was not thinking about Daran's face... He was not thinking about Ashford."*

**The filed observation**: Aldric's cognition is rendered as a filing system — he "files" things constantly, and this becomes both characterization and a structural device for deferring emotional reckoning.

**The named-but-unnamed social phenomenon**: the prose repeatedly identifies social dynamics with precision while refusing to name them directly, instead describing their mechanics.
> *"Not together — apart from each other, occupying separate positions rather than clustering, because proximity to other commoners did not confer safety and sometimes confirmed a category that was better left unnamed."*

**Ending on the ordinary**: chapters and scenes frequently close by returning to a mundane action (eating, sleeping, walking) after an emotionally significant moment, using the ordinary as a form of containment rather than resolution.

# Writing Methodology Reference (Full Version)

Complete reference material for writing quality. Internalize these principles.

## 1. Anti-AI Pattern Guide

### Emotion
| Bad (AI-like) | Good (Human) | Key |
|---|---|---|
| He felt very angry. | He crushed the teacup in his hand. Scalding water ran through his fingers, but he didn't flinch. | Externalize through action |
| She was very sad and tears fell. | She gripped her phone until her knuckles went white. The chat log blurred. | Body detail replaces label |

### Transitions
| Bad | Good | Key |
|---|---|---|
| Although he was strong, he still lost. | He was strong, sure. But the old bastard across from him fought dirtier. | Colloquial voice |
| However, things were not so simple. | No such luck. | Character thought replaces "however" |
| Therefore, he decided to take action. | He stood up and kicked the chair aside. | Cut causal connectors, show action |

## 2. Six-Step Character Psychology

For every important character action:
1. **Situation**: What's the character facing? What cards do they hold?
2. **Core motivation**: What do they want most? Fear most?
3. **Information boundary**: What do they know? Not know? Misjudge?
4. **Personality filter**: Given the same situation, how would THIS character react?
5. **Behavioral choice**: Based on 1-4, what do they choose?
6. **Emotional expression**: What emotion accompanies this? Body language, expression, tone?

## 3. Supporting Character Design

- Every side character has their own agenda. Protagonist wins by outsmarting smart people.
- Core tag + contrast detail = alive (cold-exterior character secretly feeds strays).
- Establish character through events, not description dumps.
- Different characters speak differently — vocabulary, length, verbal tics.
- In group scenes: never "everyone gasped" — pick 1-2 specific reactions.

## 4. Immersion Pillars

1. **Info delivery**: One line of dialogue can establish identity, status, personality
2. **Concrete/visual**: "The back seat of a taxi stuck in traffic for forty minutes" not "a big city"
3. **Familiarity**: Scenes readers have lived through carry natural immersion
4. **Resonance**: Protagonist's struggle must feel universal — injustice, being underestimated
5. **Desire engine**: Create emotional gap → reader anticipates release → release exceeds expectation
6. **Five senses**: Wet shirt on the back, hospital disinfectant, rain puddles at the bus stop

## 5. Emotional Escalation (Anti-Flowchart)

Fix boring daily scenes by adding fuel:
1. **Add causality**: Coming home → add "debt collector just called" → instant urgency
2. **Progressive escalation**: Stack bad things — scolded → missed bus → phone fell in drain → livestream ended → choked on stale bread. Each layer worse.
3. **Daily serves mainline**: Every quiet scene must plant a hook, advance a relationship, or build contrast.

## 6. Pre-Write Checklist

1. Which outline node does this chapter correspond to?
2. What's the protagonist's optimal move right now?
3. Who starts the conflict and why must they?
4. Do antagonists have clear motives and countermoves?
5. What information does each character have? Any boundary violations?
6. Does the chapter end with a hook?
7. Any flowchart passages? If so, add causality or strong emotion.
8. Does this chapter advance the main plotline?
