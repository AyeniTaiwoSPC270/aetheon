# Chapter 19: What Maret Writes

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Instructor Maret —

Two counts should never disagree, and Instructor Maret has just found three that do.

He has them side by side on the desk in his quarters, the lamp turned low enough to save oil and high enough to read by, which is the only kind of compromise he makes willingly. The ledger, open to a seating-order page of circuit-diagram answers three weeks old. The small notebook, open to four lines in his own hand. And a borrowed intake file he is not, strictly, supposed to have out of the records room overnight.

Three counts. One boy.

He has known since the fourth of September that Vane's numbers do not sit where they are supposed to sit. He has not known, until roughly eleven minutes ago, whether that was Vane, or whether it was the instruments lying to him — cheap steel, bad calibration, the ordinary treachery of measuring tools that get walked past a hundred students a day and touched by none of them gently. An instrument that lies once is a maintenance requisition. An instrument that lies three times, in three different rooms, to three different people, stops being an instrument problem.

He closes the ledger before he lets himself think that in words.

There is a clock on this, and it is not a subtle one. The year-end review compares every recorded answer against every other recorded answer, mechanically, without opinion, the way Maret has designed it to. In eleven weeks the ledger will be read by someone who is not him, and whatever is inside the small notebook will need, by then, to be either an explanation or a decision. Right now it is neither. It is three numbers and a boy who does not know he is being counted.

He goes to Records in the morning, before his first class, because the morning clerk is the one who does not ask why.

The office smells of dust and the particular ink Greyveil buys in bulk because it is cheap and dries wrong on cold days. The morning clerk, a narrow young man with ink on his cuff and the settled patience of someone who has been asked for the wrong file at least once by everyone in the building, looks up without standing.

"Instructor," the clerk says. "Second person this week who's wanted a first-year's full intake record, not just the summary. Are they teaching you all to be curious at the same hour now?"

"Vane," Maret says. "The Ashford commoner. Entrance results, full file, not the summary."

"Summary's usually enough."

"It isn't today."

The clerk goes still for exactly as long as it takes to decide the flat sentence isn't worth arguing with, then disappears between two shelves and comes back with a folder thinner than it should be for someone who passed with distinction. He sets it on the counter and does not let go of it immediately.

"Second," the clerk says again, mostly to himself, doing the sum out loud the way people do when they want someone else to notice a coincidence without having to say the word themselves. "You, and Instructor Bavel, two days back. Before you two, in the eleven years I've kept this counter, exactly once — and that was a discipline matter, not a curiosity."

"Two," Maret says, before he can stop himself.

"I count things for a living, Instructor. It's a very small living, but I'm accurate in it."

"There's a flag on this one," the clerk says. "Just so you're not surprised by it in front of someone else."

"What kind of flag."

"The kind that means somebody already looked, decided it wasn't worth chasing, and signed their name so nobody else has to chase it either." The clerk lets go of the folder. "Happens more than you'd think. Instruments misbehave in that room constantly. Draughty. Old glass."

"Who signed it."

"Assessment Bureau. Not a name I'd recognise, and not one you're getting out of me before my tea." The clerk says this lightly, the way a man says something he has decided in advance to say lightly. "Enjoy your morning, Instructor."

Maret takes the folder to the corridor window rather than his classroom, because the classroom has students in it in forty minutes and this cannot be read in forty minutes with an audience.

The examiner's practical notation is longer than a passing result requires. Twice the length of the boy standing next to Vane's name on the same page, in fact — Maret counts, because counting is the only part of this he still trusts completely. Output climbed past the instrument's stated midpoint twice during a single reading and the examiner recorded both instances rather than the higher of the two, which is not standard practice unless the examiner did not believe her own first number and wanted a second one on file to argue with it. At the bottom, in a different, smaller hand — the flag the clerk warned him about — a note: *Instrument variance consistent with midpoint recalibration error, Q3. No follow-up indicated.* Signed with an initial Maret does not know and a stamp he does. Closed.

He reads it twice. He reads the shape of the number itself a third time, ignoring the sentence built around it, the way he has trained himself to read circuit diagrams — not what a student says the fault is, but where the pressure actually goes.

It goes to the same place it went in his own ledger three weeks ago. It goes to the same place it went in the yard four days ago, in a burst that flattened four paces of sand into a ring while a boy called it ambient discharge to a class that believed him because believing him was easier.

Three counts. One shape.

He had expected, walking into Records this morning, to find either nothing — an ordinary instrument fault, closed for ordinary reasons, a false alarm he could file behind his own two data points and forget — or to find that someone above him already knew and was simply waiting, patiently, for the boy to grow into whatever this was before doing anything about it. He had not expected to find a closed file. A flag raised and then, deliberately, folded back down by someone with the authority to fold it.

That is worse than either of the outcomes he had planned for. An oversight, he could correct quietly. A watching authority, he could report to, or defer to, and either way the decision would stop being his to carry alone. A folded flag means someone already decided this was not worth the trouble of following, and either they were right, and it genuinely is instrument variance three times over in three different rooms four years apart — which insults every year Maret has spent learning to read instruments for a living — or they were wrong, on purpose, for a reason that had nothing to do with Vane and everything to do with something Maret cannot see from a records-room window at seven in the morning.

He returns the folder before the clerk's tea gets cold, because a folder is easier to explain missing for an hour than missing for a day.

Bavel is in the yard when Maret finds him, resetting posts nobody is using yet, in the specific unhurried way of a man who arrived early because arriving early means nobody else is there to watch him do it.

"Instructor," Maret says.

"Instructor," Bavel says back, and does not stop chalking.

"Your report. The discharge, four days ago." Maret keeps his voice at the register he uses for a wrong answer he intends to let stand uncorrected until the year-end review. "Ambient. Shell overload against old sand."

"It's a good yard for it," Bavel says. "Old sand. Told you that myself."

"You wiped your hands twice afterward."

Bavel's chalk stops on the post. He does not turn around immediately, and when he does, his face has arranged itself into precisely nothing, which is, Maret has learned over eleven years of shared staff meetings, the most information Bavel is capable of volunteering without being ordered to.

"Hands get chalky," Bavel says. "It's a chalk yard."

"They were clean the second time."

A pause. Not a long one — Bavel does not deal in long pauses, they are not economical — but long enough that Maret marks it the way he marks a hair-thin thread going still under a student's hand.

"Write what you saw," Bavel says finally. "I wrote what I needed the yard to believe. Those don't have to match, Instructor. Yet."

"And if the year-end review asks why they don't?"

"Then it asks in eleven weeks," Bavel says, and goes back to chalking, "and you'll have had eleven weeks to decide what you actually saw. I've had four days and I still don't know what I saw. Ask me again in ten."

On his way back to the theory wing he passes Vane going the other direction, a satchel over one shoulder, deep in some argument with the loud one — Rynn, the ledger says, though Maret rarely bothers with first names he has not been asked to use — about whether a debt of two lunches accrues interest. Vane does not look at him. There is no reason he should; Maret is one of a dozen instructors in the corridor at that hour, and he has never once, in fifteen weeks, given the boy cause to look twice. That is, Maret reflects, rather the point of doing this quietly. A boy who is never looked at cannot learn that he is being watched.

He is struck, walking on, by an unhelpful thought he does not write down: that the two lunches Rynn is arguing about are a smaller and more honest economy than the one currently running in his own coat pocket, where a folded flag, a wiped hand, and four lines in a private notebook are all being traded against each other at an exchange rate nobody has agreed on.

It is, Maret reflects, the most either of them has said to the other about anything that was not a scheduling conflict in three years of sharing a corridor. It confirms nothing he did not already suspect and settles nothing he had hoped it might. It does, at least, tell him that he is not alone in the folding of this particular flag — that whatever Bavel wrote for the record and whatever Bavel actually believes are two different documents, kept in two different places, the same as his own ledger and his own notebook. Two men, apparently, running the identical filing system without ever having discussed it.

He does not find that reassuring. He finds it, if he is honest with the small notebook and no one else, mildly funny, in the exhausted way a joke is funny at the end of a long shift — that the two adults in the building most likely to have already worked out that something is wrong with Aldric Vane have responded, independently, by writing two reports each and hoping the other one never gets read aloud in the same room.

His afternoon session runs itself; his students answer in seating order and he records what they say without correcting it, as he always does, and Vane — three seats from the window today — gives an answer about closed-loop resonance that is, if anything, more careful than usual, more textbook, more exactly the shape a first-year is supposed to produce and nothing more. Maret notices the carefulness. He notes, separately, that carefulness is itself a kind of data; a boy who does not know he is being watched does not usually work this hard at sounding unremarkable.

That evening, alone again, he sets the ledger, the notebook, and his own memory of the examiner's doubled reading side by side one more time, the way another man might set out cards, hoping a fourth look changes what the first three already agreed on.

It does not.

He picks up the pen. The notebook is not for conclusions — he has never once written a conclusion in it, only what he saw and when — but eleven weeks is not a long clock, and a clock he cannot stop is a clock he had better start running toward on his own terms rather than be caught by. He writes Vane's name, as he always does, at the top of a fresh line. Beneath it, where a lesser man might write *anomalous*, or *unconfirmed*, or nothing at all until he had an answer worth committing to paper, Maret writes a single word.

*Compare.*

He sits with the pen still touching the page for longer than the word requires, because the honest problem is not that he does not know what he saw. It is that he does not yet know what he is supposed to compare it to — a known shape from a text he has not found yet, a name he has not let himself think all the way through, or simply the next reading, and the one after that, until the pattern is undeniable to anyone standing where he is standing.

Eleven weeks. Three counts. One word, and no second one to put beside it.

He closes the notebook and puts out the lamp.
