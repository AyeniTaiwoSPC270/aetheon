# Chapter 45: The Name Called

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


Aldric had assumed, going into the closing ceremony, that the hard part of the day was already over. He'd fought the final. He'd won it. Standing in a line of eight remaining competitors while a herald with an enormous voice worked slowly down a scroll reading out House names, he discovered this assumption had been wrong in a way that mattered.

"It's the *names,*" Rynn muttered beside him, out of the side of his mouth, in the specific whisper that carried further than his normal speaking voice. "Nobody warned us it was going to be the names. I thought we just stood here and got a medal."

"There is a medal. Later. Apparently after the names."

"I hate the names already, and we haven't even gotten to them."

The herald was working down the placements in reverse order — eighth through first — each name followed by a House, a lineage, sometimes a small flourish of ancestry that drew a ripple of polite recognition from the noble tiers. Nessa Croft's name landed to a warm, familiar murmur; her mother's presence in the front terrace was, Aldric suspected, at least half the reason for it. Lira Voss drew a longer silence than most, the specific quiet reserved for the only elf in the year, watched by people deciding what to make of that fact all over again.

Then the herald reached third place, and the whole noble section did something Aldric hadn't heard it do all week — it went, briefly, completely still.

"Torval," the herald read, "of House Ashwynne."

The stillness lasted almost two full seconds before the murmuring started, low and fast, rippling backward through the tiers like something spilled. Aldric didn't know the name. He turned, without quite meaning to, and found Solen already leaning in, delivering the explanation before Aldric had finished forming the question.

"Old House," Solen murmured. "Very old. The kind that hasn't sent anyone to court in two generations and everyone assumed had died out quietly, on purpose, the way old money sometimes prefers to. I didn't know either, or I'd have told you before now — I don't like being surprised by information that's just sitting in a public record."

Torval himself, three places down the line, gave no visible reaction at all — the same unhurried stillness he brought to a fight, applied now to several hundred people rapidly recalculating who he actually was. Aldric understood, watching him, something he hadn't quite pieced together before: whatever Torval's circuits hid from a Mana Sense reading, his family name had apparently been doing something similar for two generations, quietly, on purpose, and nobody until this exact moment had thought to ask why a boy that unreadable might have practice at being unreadable.

Second place went to Tavish Ardell — House Ardell, the announcement drawing the same complicated ripple it had at the bracket posting, the specific murmur of a minor branch being noticed by people who'd have preferred, on balance, to keep not noticing it.

Then the herald turned to the final name on the scroll, and Aldric felt the whole arena's attention arrive on him at once, the way it had at the bracket board a week ago, except heavier now, settled rather than curious.

"First place," the herald read. "Aldric Vane."

He paused.

It was a small pause — half a second, no more, the kind of pause anyone might make while their eye found the next line down — but Aldric heard it the way he heard everything that mattered, arriving ahead of any thought about it, and understood, before the herald spoke again, exactly what had caused it.

There was no House to read after his name. No lineage, no ancestral flourish, nothing to fill the space every other name on that scroll had been given as a matter of course. The herald simply moved on to the formal closing line — congratulations, achievement, the crown's recognition of merit regardless of birth — but the gap where a House name should have been sat there for the entire arena to notice, and notice it they did, in a silence that lasted just slightly longer than Torval's had, before the applause finally arrived, warm and genuine and unable to entirely erase the shape of what had come before it.

*Just showing up looking the way you do,* Essa had said, yesterday, in the quiet behind the library. *That was the mistake, as far as this place is concerned.*

He understood, standing in that gap in the applause, exactly what she'd meant.

The King, at least, seemed entirely unbothered by it. He crossed the small ceremonial distance to shake Aldric's hand personally — an honor Aldric understood, distantly, was not standard for a first-year tournament, and which he suspected several noble families in the crowd were already composing letters home about — and said something warm and brief about talent finding its own level regardless of where it started, delivered with the specific, practiced sincerity of a man who'd said some version of this sentence many times before and had, nonetheless, apparently meant it.

Beside him, a boy close to Aldric's own age — the royal heir, unmistakably, by the specific quality of stillness the whole royal box arranged itself around — watched with open, unguarded interest that had nothing performed in it at all, the plain curiosity of someone who spent most of his life being watched and rarely got to do the watching instead.

Principal Solm stood a short distance back from the royal party, in the position protocol assigned him, and did not smile — not the way protocol would have permitted, not the way half the faculty around him were smiling. His face, in public, gave away exactly as much as it always gave away, which was almost nothing. But his eyes found Aldric once, directly, for perhaps two seconds, and something moved behind them that had no public register at all — private, warm, entirely unshared with the hundreds of people watching the same handshake — before he looked away again and became, once more, simply the Principal, standing where a Principal was meant to stand.

Aldric filed that too, the way he filed everything he didn't have time to examine properly. There would be time later. There was never time now.

Somewhere in the long middle stretch of the ceremony, between the medal itself and the closing remarks nobody would remember by supper, the herald worked briefly through the Noble Council's own attending dignitaries — a flat, procedural list of names and titles that drew no particular reaction from anyone, least of all Aldric, who was still half-occupied with the ache in his own legs. "Lord Cassian Veyra," the herald read, somewhere in the middle of perhaps thirty names, "of House Veyra, southern river trade." It meant nothing to him. He filed it anyway, the way he filed nearly everything now, and didn't notice — because there was no reason yet to notice — that something about the shape of the name snagged, faintly and without explanation, at a memory he couldn't immediately place.

It was Solen, predictably, who noticed the thing in the second-year section — a small gesture, unremarkable to everyone except a person who made a habit of watching things other people didn't think to watch.

"Don't turn obviously," Solen said, low, while the ceremony moved on to lesser placements and the crowd's attention drifted elsewhere. "Second-year section, six rows up, slightly left of the aisle."

Aldric found it without turning obviously, the way he'd learned to find most things by now. Varen — still only a name and a shape at this distance, still not re-encountered properly since the window eighteen days before Solen had finally supplied it — leaned toward Sable and said something too quiet and too far away to carry, brief, unhurried, the kind of sentence that took no more than a few seconds to deliver and clearly needed no more than that.

Sable's face didn't change. She simply nodded, once, and looked away, toward the ceremony, as though nothing at all had passed between them.

"What did he say?" Aldric asked.

"I don't know. I'm good, not omniscient." Solen's eyes stayed on the pair a moment longer, cataloguing something Aldric couldn't follow. "I don't like that I don't know. I'll add it to the list."

"The list is getting long."

"The list has always been long. You're just finally paying attention to how long."

The ceremony wound down the way these things always did — slower than it needed to, full of formal language nobody quite listened to, the actual medal itself a smaller and less dramatic object than four days of anticipation had built it up to be. Aldric stood through the last of it with Rynn's ongoing, half-audible commentary beside him and Solen's watchful quiet on the other side, and let himself, for the first time all week, feel something close to simple relief.

It didn't last as long as he wanted it to.

He caught the movement at the edge of the grounds almost by accident — a flicker at the far treeline beyond the arena's outer wall, where the day's remaining staff should have been packing away the last of the vendor stalls and instead, for just a moment, weren't moving the way people packing away stalls moved. It was small. It was probably nothing. It had the specific, deliberate economy of motion that people used when they didn't want to be noticed moving at all, and Aldric had spent two years learning to notice exactly that kind of economy, in exactly that kind of place.

He looked for it again and it was gone — swallowed back into the ordinary chaos of a tournament ending, vendors and staff and departing families all moving in the same general direction at once — and told himself, without quite believing it, that a tired mind after the longest week of his life was seeing patterns in nothing at all.

He didn't mention it to Rynn. He didn't mention it to Solen either, though he suspected, from the brief, sharp look Solen gave the same stretch of treeline a moment later, that he hadn't been the only one to notice something and decide it was probably nothing.

The celebration went on around them, warm and loud and entirely unaware of anything at all.
