# Chapter 7: The Morning Register

### Saga 1, Arc 2

### Saga 1, Arc 2

---

— Maren Vane —

The kitchen was dark when she came downstairs.

She did not light the lamp immediately. She stood at the bottom of the stairs for a moment with her hand on the post and let her eyes adjust, the way she had learned to do years ago when the children in the village needed her at odd hours and she had trained herself not to waste time stumbling. The kitchen resolved around her in gradients of grey. The table. The chairs. The shelf with its jars in their non-random order.

Josse was already at the table.

He should not have been at the table. He had two cracked ribs and a side dressing that she had checked twice before sleeping, and he was at his own table in the dark, sitting upright, both hands flat on the wood in front of him the way he sat when he was thinking through something he had not yet finished thinking through.

Maren looked at him for a moment. Then she went and lit the lamp.

The light came up slow and warm. Josse did not look up. She set the lamp on the hook above the table and sat down across from him, and for a while neither of them said anything. This was not unusual. They had been doing this long enough that silence between them was not empty.

"Ribs," she said.

"Manageable."

She looked at his face. He was not lying, which did not mean she was satisfied. She would check the dressing again before Aldric came down.

The lamp swayed slightly in a draft from the window gap she kept meaning to seal. The shadows moved. Josse's hands were still flat on the table.

"He's leaving today," she said.

"Yes."

Outside, the village was beginning its earliest sounds — the ones that happened before anyone was officially awake. A door somewhere. The sound of water. Maren listened to them and did not speak for another moment, and Josse did not either, and between them on the table the lamp held its small steady light.

"He didn't know it was coming," she said. Not a question. She had been a healer long enough to know the difference between intention and reflex — she had seen both in people's faces when something moved through them they had not called. She had seen it in Aldric's face last night before he sat down and stopped showing her his face.

Josse was quiet. Then: "No."

She nodded once. That was the thing she had needed to confirm, though she had already known it. She folded her hands on the table.

"He'll figure out what it is," she said.

"Yes."

"He'll do that on his own if we let him."

Josse looked up then. One look, full and steady, the kind that took stock of everything in it and gave back only what was needed. "Yes," he said again, and that was the whole conversation, the entirety of what needed to be said between two people who had made a thirty-second decision in a cold storage shed and had not revisited it once in all the years since.

Maren got up. She checked his dressing. She started the water for the morning.

When Aldric came downstairs, the kitchen was already running.

---

— Aldric Vane —

He smelled the water heating before he reached the bottom step.

The kitchen was lit and in motion — Maren at the far end doing something with dried stems that did not require his input, the table clear and set with the efficiency of someone who had been up for at least an hour. Josse was at the table.

Aldric looked at him for a half-second longer than he meant to.

Josse caught it. He lifted his chin slightly — not quite a nod, not quite a shrug — the gesture that meant *I'm aware of my own ribs, thank you* — and reached for his cup. Two cracked ribs and he was at his own table with his cup in his hand as if yesterday had been a long day and not a Greyback in the middle of the village square. Aldric filed this under *Josse*, which was its own category, and sat down.

"There's food," Maren said, without turning around.

"I can see the food."

"Then why are you just sitting there."

He ate. The kitchen ran around him — Maren's stems, the water, the particular sounds of the Vane household in the early morning — and Aldric sat in the middle of it and let it run. Something in him that had been braced since last night loosened slightly, the way a shoulder drops when you have been holding it up without knowing it.

He did not think about why.

There was a pressure behind his sternum — low, background, new. Not painful. It had been there since last night and he had been not-thinking about it with some success. It felt like the moment after you set something down that you have been carrying a long way: the absence of weight becoming its own sensation. He noticed it. He filed it. He reached for his bread.

"Someone dealt with the—" he started.

"Pell's boys," Maren said. "Last night. Wyla organized it." A brief pause. "She organizes everything eventually."

Aldric nodded. He had a thought about this that was almost a joke — something about Wyla's organizational radius expanding to include supernatural threats — and then did not say it, because it was not quite the right moment for it and he knew the difference.

"The horse?" he said instead.

"Cart's lame. Bren took it to the post road farrier this morning."

So they were walking. He had expected this. He picked up his cup and drank and across the table Josse set his own cup down and said, without particular weight, "Road food's by the door."

Aldric looked. His bag was already packed, sitting upright by the door with two parcels of road food wedged against the side of it — Maren's doing, clearly, because his bag had been in his room last night and he had not touched it.

He looked at the bag for a moment.

"When did you—"

"Eat," Maren said.

He ate.

---

The village was doing what it did.

Aldric moved through it once before leaving — he needed to return the herb bundle Maren had borrowed from old Presswick three days ago, a task that had been waiting since before any of this happened and which he completed now because it needed completing and because moving through Ashford was better than sitting still with the pressure in his chest and the bag by the door.

The village looked back at him.

Not all of it. Most of it was doing what it always did — the mill wheel, the smith's first ring of the morning, the two cats that lived near the baker's who did not care about anything that had happened or would happen. But some of it looked. Some of it looked and then looked away. Some of it looked and then greeted him exactly the same way it always had, which he recognized as a kindness and filed as such.

He was passing the post-house when Wyla came out of it.

She was a broad woman, Wyla, efficient in movement and in speech, and she did not waste time on preamble. She looked at him the way she looked at the weather — assessing, not particularly surprised by what it was doing.

"Vane boy," she said.

"Morning, Wyla."

She studied him for a moment. He waited. There was something she was deciding whether to say, which meant she had already decided.

"What you did yesterday," she said. She stopped. Started again differently. "The Greyback was three yards from the Cossel family." A pause. "Their youngest is four."

Aldric did not say anything.

"The district post went out this morning," she said. "Incident report. They'll send acknowledgment in a fortnight, maybe three weeks. Maybe longer." The way she said it made clear what she thought of fortnight acknowledgments. "There'll be talk about the ward crystal. There's always talk about the ward crystal." She looked at him directly. "The talk's been going on since before your parents moved here. Nothing's happened yet."

"I know," Aldric said.

She nodded once, as if this confirmed something. Then: "You're leaving today."

"Yes."

"Good," she said, and it was not unkind. "The exam's the exam. Go pass it." She turned back toward the post-house. "Tell your mother I'll look in on Josse."

She went inside. The door closed.

Aldric stood on the post-house step for a moment. Around him Ashford continued its morning — the mill, the smith, the two cats — and two village guards were visible down the lane near where the Greyback had been, doing the kind of standing-near-something that meant official presence had been established even if nothing official was currently happening. One of them glanced at him. Looked away.

He turned and went back to the house.

---

Daran was at the road's edge when he got there — dark-haired, bag over one shoulder, looking down the south road with the expression of someone who has already mentally departed and is waiting for the physical departure to catch up. He turned when he heard Aldric's step.

"Ready?" he said.

"Nearly."

Inside for the last time. Maren was drying her hands on the work cloth — the herbs done, the morning tasks run through, and she was at the part of her morning that included this. She looked at him. He stood in the doorway with his bag and said nothing because nothing needed to be said.

She crossed to him and pressed two fingers below the dried blood on his upper lip — the nosebleed, long dried, that he had not thought she had noticed. She checked it the way she checked everything: efficiently, without sentiment, her fingers reading for information. She stepped back.

"Eat when you stop," she said. "Not after."

"I know."

"You always say that."

"I always know it."

She looked at him for one moment more — not long, not searching, just a look that was the fullest thing she knew how to do and which he understood completely. Then she turned back to the workroom.

He went to the door.

Josse was standing in it.

He had moved from the table to the doorway — ribs and all, side dressing and all, the slow careful movement of a man who had decided where he needed to be and had gotten there. He was not leaning on the frame. He was standing in it the way he stood when he had decided something was worth standing for.

He looked at Aldric.

Aldric looked back.

There was nothing that needed to be said. There was nothing Josse would have said even if there were. He put one hand on Aldric's shoulder — brief, deliberate, the weight of it exact — and then he moved aside and let him through the door.

Aldric stepped out into the morning.

---

They walked.

The south road unrolled ahead of them pale and long in the early light, the treeline on either side holding the shadow of the night before, the village sounds falling away behind them as they went. Daran was half a step ahead the way he always was — he had always walked slightly faster than Aldric without appearing to hurry, a habit so old neither of them had ever commented on it.

They did not speak.

This was different from the silence of Chapter 5 — Aldric registered this without examining it. That silence had been easy. Full. The silence of two people who had so much to say they were saving it for later. This silence had edges. Not hostile — nothing between him and Daran had ever been hostile, not really — but deliberate. Both of them were choosing it, and the choice was visible, and neither of them addressed it.

The road curved slightly northeast. The treeline thickened. Behind them Ashford was beginning to disappear — not yet, not fully, but the first buildings were rounding out of the direct sightline, the way it happened when you left on this road, the gradual erasure.

Aldric watched the road ahead.

The pressure behind his sternum was still there — low and constant, the sensation of circuits that had fired hard and were still remembering it. He breathed through it. Filed it. The road was long and pale and the exam was at the end of it and he was good at moving toward things without looking at what they cost him.

Beside him — half a step ahead, always — Daran said:

*"It didn't look like you."*

No weight in it. Factual. The tone of a person reading something aloud from a page they had been studying since last night, delivering the final annotation before closing the book. Not accusation. Not wonder. Something between observation and verdict, spoken into the road ahead of them, not turned toward him.

Aldric did not respond.

He did not know what he would have said. He filed this too — the not-knowing, the half-beat where something in him surfaced and then did not make it to the surface, the drawer full and then closed again. He kept walking.

Daran said nothing else.

---

— Daran —

He had been thinking about it since the moment it happened.

Not obsessively — he was not built for obsession, he was built for filing and forward motion and the conversion of difficult things into usable energy. But the image had not left. The shimmer. The contact. The way Aldric's eyes had gone somewhere else for three seconds and come back. He had been standing close enough to feel the displacement of air and he had stood there and watched something move through his oldest friend that had nothing to do with the boy he had kept score against for nine years.

He had said what he needed to say. He had not said it for Aldric's benefit — Aldric would do what Aldric always did, which was file it and continue. He had said it because it was true and he needed to say one true thing out loud before he put it away.

*It didn't look like you.*

It had looked like something else entirely. Something that had been in there a long time, waiting, and had simply decided the moment had arrived.

He walked ahead.

The road was long. The exam was at the end of it. Whatever he had seen yesterday, whatever it meant, whatever it would eventually become — it was filed. He was already thinking about the assessment format, the circuit efficiency drills he had run that morning while Aldric was still asleep, the positioning of the evaluation hall and whether they would test mana output before or after technical form.

He was always forward-facing.

He walked ahead, and behind him the road curved, and Ashford rounded the bend and was gone.
