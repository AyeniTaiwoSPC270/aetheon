# Chapter 58: The Last Ordinary Week

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"It says *noted,*" Rynn said. "Not *fined.* Not *punished.* Noted. Do you know what that means?"

"It means they'll write it down," said Solen.

"It means they'll write it down *forever.* In ink. In a drawer that outlives us all." Rynn tapped the refectory door, where a fresh notice had been pinned overnight in Bavel's handwriting: *ALL LIBRARY LOANS TO BE RETURNED BY FRIDAY. FAILURE WILL BE NOTED.* Somebody had drawn a small, shocked face beside the last word. It was, Aldric was fairly sure, Rynn. "I've got a book on the history of drainage I never finished. I'm going to be noted for *sluices.*"

"Sluices are underrated," Aldric said, before he could stop himself.

Solen gave him a look of mild, flat interest. "Is that a quote?"

"Something I heard."

"From whom?"

"A person," Aldric said, "who reads a great deal."

Rynn looked between the two of them like a man watching a match he'd bet on and could no longer follow, and gave up, and opened the door.

It was the last week of the term, and the whole of Greyveil had gone soft at the edges.

The signs were everywhere, if you knew where to look. Trunks stood open in the dormitory corridors, half-packed and mostly abandoned, as though their owners had wandered off to reconsider. Second-years crossed the courtyard in loose clumps, carrying things they had no business carrying: a lamp, a chair, one enormous potted fern that two boys were moving between them with the terrible solemnity of a coffin. The lists for next year's sections went up on the east board on Friday. Everyone pretended not to be waiting for them. The pretense had lasted, at last count, about six hours.

And nobody, Aldric noticed, knew what to do with the year.

He'd expected it to be like finishing a book: a page, a last line, a closing of the cover. It wasn't. It was more like a house at the end of a long party, where everyone's on their feet and looking for their coats and no one wants to be the first to say what the evening had been. People said *well.* People said *that was a term.* Two first-years had a long, delicate conversation about the weather, in which each was clearly trying to avoid saying the word *tournament,* and each was clearly aware that the other was doing the same.

It was strange. It was almost restful. It was very much not restful.

"We should do something," Rynn announced, over the bread. "Tonight. The three of us. Something completely without purpose."

"That's most of what we do," Solen said.

"I mean *deliberately.*" He chewed, swallowed, and pointed the crust at Aldric like a small, edible sword. "No plan. No mystery. No one being attacked, no one being watched, no one giving a look at the back of anyone's head. An entirely uneventful evening. I've researched it. It's a thing people do."

"Where did you research it?"

"I read a book once." A pause. "It was about sluices."

"That's not a book about evenings."

"It had a *chapter.*"

Aldric found that he was smiling. It was a real one, unguarded, the sort he hadn't caught himself wearing since some time before the bracket went up. It felt like using a muscle after a long illness: unfamiliar, a little sore, and unexpectedly good.

"Fine," he said.

"Don't say *fine.* You say it like a man handing over a weapon."

"Yes," Aldric said. "Good. That's the plan."

---

They had the evening on the dormitory steps, because it was the only place any of them had ever chosen on purpose.

It was the same flight, the same worn edge, the same view of the empty courtyard and the dark window across it that no longer had anyone in it. Aldric sat on the third step. Rynn took the second, sprawled, one boot on the stone and the other on Aldric's shin, entirely without apology. Solen sat above them both with the exact economy of a man who had decided where he would be and was now merely completing the paperwork.

Rynn had brought a cake. It was a small, dense, slightly lopsided cake, and he refused to say where he'd found it. "It's a gift," he said, "from an anonymous friend. Or the kitchen. The distinction is legally murky."

"You stole it."

"I was *given* it, in a manner that should not be examined."

They ate. They talked about nothing. It was, Aldric thought, a very difficult thing to do properly, and the three of them were terrible at it: forty minutes of reasoned argument about whether wool could technically count as a sale, a heated exchange about the exact height of the ceiling in the refectory, and a long, patient explanation from Solen of why a goat that did not exist could nevertheless be said to have lost a wager.

"It's hypothetical," said Solen.

"It's *mine,*" said Rynn.

"You can't own a hypothetical goat."

"I'd like to see you take it from me."

"I could take it from you in a sentence."

"Try."

He tried. He was correct. Rynn took it with the wounded dignity of a man who had been robbed of something very small and very dear. Aldric laughed until his ribs hurt, and then, because it seemed to be the evening for it, laughed some more.

Torval came by around the fourth bell, carrying a wardrobe on his back. It was a whole wardrobe: oak, tall, the kind of thing two grown men would consider an afternoon's work. He did not seem to be in any difficulty. He set it down on the flagstones, nodded to the three of them with a civil gravity, and said, "It belongs to my cousin. He asked me to move it," in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

"Where's it going?" Rynn asked.

"Up," said Torval. He paused. "I thought I'd tell you. My House wants me home a week early." He looked, briefly, at Aldric. "It was good to fight you."

"It was," Aldric said. "I'm still sore."

"Good. It's meant to be." And he picked up the wardrobe again, and went up the stair with it, as if it were a satchel, and none of them said anything for a moment.

"That," Rynn said at last, "was the most Torval thing that has ever happened."

---

It was Rynn who suggested the awards.

He announced it sometime after the cake was gone. It was a *ceremony,* he said. A small one, held on the steps. Each of them would give out a prize, in a category of his own devising, to whichever of the other two most deserved it. There would be no speeches. There would be one speech. It would be his.

"Most Improved Vocabulary," he said, presenting Solen with an imaginary medal. "For a man who has gone from *no* to *I would prefer that you didn't.*"

"Thank you," said Solen. "I accept this on behalf of all my previous refusals."

"Loudest Person In A Room Where I Was Trying To Be Quiet," Solen said, and Rynn took a bow. "Most Likely To Say *Fine* When He Means Something Else," Solen went on, and Aldric raised a hand and was applauded, ironically, by two people who knew exactly how deserved it was.

"Most Likely," said Rynn, "To Run *At* Something."

The step went quiet.

It was not a large quiet. It came in the space of a breath, the way a shadow slides across a floor when a cloud passes the sun. Solen's hand, which had been half-raised in mock-applause, stopped where it was. Rynn's grin stayed on his face for exactly one heartbeat longer than it should have, and then, quite slowly, ceased to be a grin at all.

Nobody said the word. Nobody said *the attack,* or *the courtyard,* or *Solm,* or *five people in tournament uniforms.* But it was there in the air like a smell. A year of it, the tournament and the burst and the look on a stranger's face as he fled. It had been building, quietly, in three people who had all been very careful not to mention it, and it had found the shape of a joke to slip through, and was now standing on the step in front of them, waiting to be spoken to.

Aldric felt his mouth start to move.

He didn't know what he was going to say. Something true, probably. It was that sort of evening, and he was very tired of *fine.*

"—which," said Rynn, loudly, "brings me to the winner. Because the winner, and I want this on record, is the *goat.*"

Solen blinked.

"The goat," Rynn continued, standing, one hand pressed over his heart. "The hypothetical goat. Who has been *through* things this term. Who has lost a wager to a man who does not exist, been denied by a legal argument, been tried in absentia, and has *never once complained.* Ladies and gentlemen. The goat."

A pause.

Aldric started laughing. He couldn't help it. It came up from his chest, from somewhere underneath the shaking that he had been carrying since the night of the attack, and it wasn't at the joke. It was at the *timing.* At a boy who could not keep a secret for the life of him and had just kept the most important one of the year, in the space of one sentence, by being ridiculous.

Solen was laughing too, in his own way: a single, dry, helpless exhale through the nose, and then a long silence in which he did not look at either of them. "That was an extremely bad speech," he said, finally.

"I know," said Rynn, sitting down, and Aldric saw that his hands were not entirely steady. "I've been working on it for hours."

Nobody said thank you. It didn't need to be said. It hung there, on the step, exactly as heavy as it should be.

---

It was quite late when they went in.

Aldric stayed a minute after the others, the way he always did, for no reason but that it seemed rude to leave a room without looking at it. The steps were cold. The courtyard was black and silent. The dark window across the way was still dark, and he didn't wait for anything to move in it.

*Something sturdier,* he thought. It arrived without ceremony. He'd once told them that some people called them a group, in a courtyard, on these steps, and had not meant it as a compliment. He had not meant it as anything. It had simply been a word for three people who kept sitting in the same place.

But that wasn't what this was anymore. Nobody had decided it. Nobody had said a word. Somewhere between the bracket and the Final and a courtyard full of screaming, the three of them had stopped being a thing that happened to be true and had become a thing that was simply so, the way a house becomes home without anybody noticing the day the furniture stopped being borrowed.

It was, he thought, the best thing that had happened to him all year, and he had not been looking.

He stood, and stretched, and went up.

In his room the trunk lay open on the floor where he'd left it that morning, empty except for the half-packed edge of a winter coat. He looked at it for a long time. The lamp was low. Through the wall he could hear Rynn's voice, muffled and cheerful, arguing with someone about the last of the cake.

It occurred to him, standing there, with a coat in one hand and nothing at all to do with the other, that the evening was over.

That was all. It had been a good one. It had gone, in every way that counted, exactly right. And it was over, and the week was nearly over, and the year was nearly over, and there was no way to keep a night like that except to have had it.

He folded the coat. It took much longer than it should have.
