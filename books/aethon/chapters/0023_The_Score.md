# Chapter 23: The Score

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"Say it again," Rynn said, "but slower, so I can be offended properly."

"You finished eleventh."

"Out of how many?"

"Eleven," Aldric said.

Rynn considered this the way he considered most bad news — as though it might improve if he stared at it long enough. They were at the edge of the yard, waiting on Bavel's pairs board, and the morning drill had left a fine grey dust over everything, including Rynn's opinion of himself. "Eleventh isn't last," he said. "Eleventh is respectable. Eleventh says I was *pacing myself.*"

"Eleventh is last," Aldric said. "You're describing last using different words because the first set of words made you sad."

"That's a skill."

"It's a symptom."

Solen, three feet away and not participating, said, "You finished eleventh because you tried to knock Halvon's post over with your shoulder on the walk back and missed the last cast entirely."

"That's not relevant."

"It's extremely relevant. It's the whole reason."

Rynn opened his mouth to argue this and then, visibly, decided the argument wasn't worth the breath it would cost him, which was as close to conceding a point as Rynn ever came. Aldric had learned to recognize the shape of it — the same shrug he'd worn apologizing to the training post the week the punch had first thrown them together, translated now into something almost dignified.

It was, by any measure, an ordinary morning. That was the part Aldric had started to notice about ordinary mornings: how much of the term had become them. Three weeks since the burst. Two since the night in the yard. A week since Solen had made a joke land sideways into something that was apparently, now, a group. Nothing had happened since. He'd started to trust that.

He wanted, specifically, for nothing else to happen. Not because he'd stopped being curious about what he was — he thought about the burst more than he let on, more than he let *himself* on — but because *nothing happening* was the only condition under which he got to be ordinary in the meantime, and ordinary was a country he liked visiting. He had a mid-table print, a seat nobody watched, and two friends who thought elevenths were funny. That was, currently, the whole of what he wanted, stated as plainly as he ever let himself state anything.

Bavel came out with the pairs list and nailed it to the board without a word, which was as close as Bavel ever came to a greeting. The yard converged on it the way it always did — a slow crowd pretending not to be a crowd, everyone reading past their own name to see who else had drawn well.

Aldric's own name meant nothing to him yet, alphabetically buried in the middle of the sheet the way it always was, which suited him. He drifted toward it anyway, because not drifting toward it would have been its own kind of statement, and let the crowd carry him within four feet of the corner where Crest's cluster stood.

That was the thing about the term calcifying. Six weeks ago, four feet from Crest's corner would have meant something — a jab lined up, a slow smile, the whole performance. Now it meant nothing. Crest didn't turn. Didn't so much as adjust his weight the way people adjusted their weight around Aldric, the small recalibration he'd learned to read without meaning to. Crest's attention was fixed somewhere else entirely, and for one unremarkable second Aldric assumed that somewhere else was simply *away from him*, filed it under the general heading of *good*, and went back to finding his own name on the sheet.

It took him a full ten seconds — long enough to locate his pairing, confirm it was nobody interesting, and start turning away — before the shape of what he'd actually seen caught up with him.

Crest wasn't ignoring the board. Crest was reading it twice, and both times his eyes stopped in the same place, three lines down from where Aldric's name sat.

Rynn's name.

"Problem?" Milo asked Crest, low, the way you asked a question you already suspected the answer to.

"No," Crest said, not looking away from the sheet. "Just noticing how much conduct duty costs a person, over a term."

It was such a small thing. Said to nobody in particular, about nothing in particular, in the exact register Crest used for a hundred remarks a week that meant nothing and were designed to. Aldric had spent six weeks learning to let that register wash past him without slowing down. He almost did it now.

Almost.

What stopped him was the sequence, replayed: Crest hadn't been avoiding him all morning because Crest had run out of reasons to bother. Crest had been *busy*. Aldric had spent the last three weeks quietly grateful for a peace he'd assumed he'd earned — earned by being unremarkable, earned by being the kind of print that didn't draw a second look — and it had never once occurred to him to ask what the peace was actually made of, or what it cost, or who was paying it in his place.

He looked past Crest's shoulder to where Rynn was still arguing his eleventh-place finish into something more flattering, entirely unaware that anyone in the yard had spent the morning looking at him instead of at Aldric, and felt something in his chest do a small, cold recalculation.

"You're doing the thing," Solen said, appearing at his elbow with the specific quiet of someone who had, in fact, also noticed. "The head-tilt thing. The one you do when arithmetic doesn't come out even."

"I thought the quiet was for me," Aldric said.

"It was never for you," Solen said, not unkindly, in the tone of a man correcting a measurement. "You just happened to be standing where the quiet used to point."

"That's an upsetting way to put it."

"I'm an upsetting person. It's a known quality." Solen glanced once, briefly, toward Crest's corner, then away, tucking it in behind his eyes the way he tucked everything away — with no visible change in his face and a great deal happening somewhere behind it. "He hasn't looked at you in nine days. I counted."

"You counted that."

"I count most things. It's the only reason anyone tolerates me."

"Rynn tolerates you for other reasons," Aldric said.

"Rynn tolerates me because I know how many lamp brackets are on the east stair and he does not, and some part of him finds that morally offensive and worth staying close to correct." Solen's mouth did something that on anyone else would have been the beginning of a smile. "Forty-three, in case it's slipped."

"It hasn't."

"Good. I'd hate to have counted for nothing."

Rynn rejoined them at that point, having apparently talked himself into a version of eleventh place that satisfied him, and the three of them moved off the yard together in the loose, unhurried way that had become — without anyone deciding it, exactly — how they moved most places now. Aldric let the conversation carry him, contributed a joke about Rynn's post-avoidance technique that landed well enough to make Rynn threaten him with a lamp bracket, and kept, underneath all of it, one thought turning slowly like a stone in a pocket: *he wasn't watching me. He was watching what I have.*

The common room, when they reached it, was doing what it always did at this hour — filling slowly with the particular noise of people who'd survived a morning and intended to be loud about it. Someone had claimed the good bench by the window for a card game that had been running, by Aldric's count, since the second week of term and showed no sign of ever concluding honestly. Rynn folded himself onto the floor beside it uninvited, was uninvited a second time more forcefully, and stayed anyway.

"You're quiet," he said, up at Aldric, around a mouthful of someone else's bread. "Quieter than your usual quiet. You have a face."

"I have several. It's efficient."

"This one's the arithmetic one. Solen said you were doing the arithmetic one."

"I did say that," Solen agreed, folding himself onto the bench with the precision of a man completing a form. "I stand by it."

"I'm fine," Aldric said, which was true in the specific, narrow sense that nothing was currently on fire, and false in every sense that mattered. He didn't want to say the rest of it out loud — not here, not with Rynn six inches away and cheerful and utterly unaware that his own name had spent the morning under a kind of attention neither of them had a name for yet. Saying it felt like handing Rynn something heavy for no reason except that Aldric didn't want to carry it alone, and that wasn't a good enough reason. Not yet. Not until he knew what it was.

"You're doing it *right now*," Rynn said, delighted, pointing with the bread. "The face where you've decided something and you're not going to tell anyone what."

"That's most of my faces."

"It's the worst one. It means homework later."

"Everything means homework later, with you," Solen said. "That's not a diagnosis. That's just what it's like to know him."

Rynn considered this, decided it was a compliment, and returned to the bread and the card game with the single-minded satisfaction of someone who'd won an argument without noticing he'd lost the actual point of it. Aldric let him. It was, he thought, the kindest thing he could currently do — let Rynn have the ordinary minute, the one where eleventh place was the worst thing in his day, for exactly as long as that minute lasted.

He caught Solen watching him do it — the specific, deliberate choice not to say anything — and Solen, for once, didn't ask. He only inclined his head a fraction, the smallest possible acknowledgment that he'd seen the choice made and approved of it, and went back to whatever silent arithmetic occupied him between sentences.

It didn't last. Nothing this term seemed built to last more than an hour before the corridor found him again.

It was worse, somehow, than being watched himself. He'd built a whole careful architecture around absorbing attention aimed at him — flatten it, deflect it, give it nothing to push against. He had no equivalent architecture for attention aimed at Rynn, who had never had to build one, because Rynn's whole strategy for six weeks had been *throw the first punch and apologize loudly afterward*, and that strategy had no answer for someone patient enough not to need a first punch at all.

The corridor toward the common space ran past the administrative wing — the same stretch of hallway Aldric had walked eight days into term, waiting on a bench that turned out to have Solen already on it. He knew this corridor now the way he knew most of the academy: mapped, catalogued, unremarkable. Which was exactly why the voice from the alcove by the records window caught him the way it did — not because it was loud, but because it was Crest's, and Crest's voice in this particular hallway had no obvious reason to exist.

He didn't mean to stop. He told himself, afterward, that he hadn't meant to. But his feet slowed the way they slowed at a familiar tear in familiar fabric, the part of him that had learned to hear where a thing was about to fail before it failed.

"—I only ask because I'd hate for it to lapse on a technicality," Crest was saying, to someone out of sight past the alcove's edge — a clerk, maybe, or one of the junior staff who kept the duty logs. His voice had none of its yard register in it at all. No smile in it. Just a flat, careful precision, the sound of a boy reciting something he'd rehearsed until the rehearsal disappeared. "Two weeks of evening conduct duty, ending — when, exactly? I only want to be sure it's recorded as *completed*. Properly completed. Every night. Every signature."

A pause. A rustle of paper. Something Aldric couldn't hear, murmured low.

"No, no trouble," Crest said. "I just like things to be accurate."

Aldric stood very still in the corridor, Rynn's laughter fading somewhere ahead of him, and understood — with the same cold, complete certainty he'd felt watching his own hand move without him in Ashford — that he had just watched Crest ask, in a register built for exactly this, a question no one asks unless they already suspect the answer isn't what the record says.
