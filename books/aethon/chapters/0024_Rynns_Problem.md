# Chapter 24: Rynn's Problem

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

Rynn was sitting on the floor of the corridor outside the common room with his back against the wall and a single sheet of paper held in both hands like it might try to leave if he let go, and that was how Aldric knew, before he'd read a word of it, that eleventh place had just become the second-worst thing to happen to Rynn that week.

"Don't," Rynn said, when Aldric crouched down beside him. "Don't do the face. I can't deal with the face right now."

"Which face."

"The one where you already know it's bad before I tell you."

"I don't know anything yet," Aldric said, which was true for exactly as long as it took Rynn to hand the paper over, silently, with the particular care of someone passing along something he suspected might be contagious.

"If it helps," Aldric added, scanning the header, "eleventh place is looking a lot better than it did an hour ago."

"It does not help."

"I thought it might."

It was short. Academy notices always were — brevity being, apparently, a kind of institutional cruelty all its own. *Notice of Record Review*, it said, in the flat official hand of someone who'd written the same six words a thousand times and felt nothing about any of them. Beneath that: Rynn's name, the date of his original reprimand, and a single sentence that took Aldric two readings to fully understand, because the first reading his mind simply refused it.

*Evening conduct duty entry for the eleventh night of service found incomplete — supervising signature absent. Per Academy Conduct Code, Section 4.2, an incompletely recorded term of conduct duty is not considered served. Original infraction reverts to open status pending review.*

Beneath that, a date. Six days out.

"It's a mistake," Aldric said, out loud, the way you said a thing to make it true by saying it. "You did the duty. You counted the brackets wrong every single night and complained about it the whole time. Nobody serves conduct duty that reluctantly and doesn't actually serve it."

"I know I did it."

"Then it's a clerical thing. Somebody forgot to sign a page. It gets fixed with a conversation."

"That's what I thought," Rynn said, and there was something in his voice Aldric hadn't heard from him before — not the loud, apologetic panic of the administrative office, that first week, but something quieter and worse, the sound of someone doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish. "So I went and asked. This morning, before the yard. Asked the steward who signs the log — old fellow, does the east stair most nights, you'd like him, he complains about the brackets more than I do." A thin, unconvincing attempt at the old register, gone as soon as it arrived. "He remembered me. Remembered the night. Said he'd stepped out sick halfway through — his knee, apparently, does something specific and awful in cold weather — and the junior who covered for him never actually signed anything, because nobody told the junior he was supposed to. It's not that I didn't do it, Aldric. It's that the *paperwork* didn't."

"So tell them that. Tell them exactly that."

"I *did*. That's the part—" Rynn stopped, started again, quieter. "That's the part I don't understand. The steward already told them. He went to the office himself yesterday, soon as he heard someone was asking. Said it plain, put it in writing. And it's still — " he tapped the paper with one finger, hard enough that it nearly tore, "—still open. Still six days. Nobody's changed it."

Aldric read the notice a third time, looking now not at what it said but at what it declined to say — no acknowledgment of the steward's statement, no mention of any dispute, no space at all for the plain, boring, entirely mundane truth that everyone involved apparently already agreed on. Just the original sentence, sitting there with the flat permanence of something that had already been decided and was only now being announced.

That was the moment the shape of it changed for him — not gradually, the way most understanding arrived, but all at once, like a shell finally giving out along a seam he hadn't known was under strain. He'd read the notice as an accident twice over, an honest mistake compounding an honest mistake, the kind of thing academies generated by the yard because academies ran on paper and paper failed the way everything failed, eventually, under enough weight. He'd been wrong. An accident didn't survive a direct, written correction from the one witness who could settle it. An accident got fixed with a conversation, the way he'd said it would. This hadn't been fixed. This had been *held open*, deliberately, by someone with enough reach into the process to make a settled fact stay unsettled — and there was exactly one person in Aldric's recent memory who'd stood in a corridor asking, in a voice built for exactly this, whether a piece of paper was recorded as *completed. Properly completed. Every night. Every signature.*

"Crest," he said.

Rynn looked up. "What?"

"Yesterday. In the administrative corridor. I heard him asking about your log — whether it was recorded as complete. I didn't think anything of it. I thought it was just him being — " Aldric searched for the word and settled on the honest one — "petty. Small. The kind of thing he does that costs him nothing and means nothing. I didn't think it *meant* anything."

"He can't just — " Rynn started, and stopped, because they both already knew that he could. That was the whole shape of it. Crest hadn't forged a signature or invented an infraction or done a single thing that would show up as his fingerprint on anything. He'd found a real gap — a sick old man's knee, an uninstructed junior, six weeks of ordinary institutional carelessness that happened to a hundred students a year and mattered to none of them — and he'd simply asked the right question of the right person at the right moment, and then, Aldric suspected, asked a second and a third question after that, quietly, through whatever channel a Halvon had access to that a groundskeeper's honest correction apparently didn't. He hadn't broken a single rule. He'd used the rules the way Aldric used a thin thread — finding the weakest point and putting exactly enough pressure there to make it fail on its own, leaving nothing behind that could be traced back to a hand.

*The exception is never a bigger fire. The exception is a smaller one, put in exactly the right place.* Aldric had heard him say something close to that once, in Doven's classroom, arguing a counter rule for an exercise that hadn't meant anything at the time. It meant something now.

"This isn't him being petty," Aldric said slowly, working it out as he said it, the way he'd worked out the diagram in Mana Theory — by refusing to reach for the word he didn't have and describing the failure instead. "Petty costs him nothing and gives him nothing back except the look on your face. This costs him effort. Somebody had to go and find that gap, and somebody had to go and make sure it stayed found after the steward tried to close it. That's not spite. That's a plan."

"A plan to do *what*? I'm not — Aldric, I'm not important. I'm not you. Nobody's building a plan around *me*."

"That's what I thought too," Aldric said, and didn't say the rest of it — that he'd spent six weeks assuming Crest's silence toward him was a kind of surrender, when it had actually been Crest choosing, with real patience, to spend his attention somewhere the return was cheaper and the target had no defenses built for it at all. Rynn had never needed a defense against this. Rynn's entire strategy, for six weeks, had been to throw the first punch and let the consequence land where it landed, because the consequence had always, so far, been survivable — a reprimand, two weeks of lamp brackets, a story to tell later. Nobody had prepared Rynn for a fight with no punch to throw, fought entirely on paper, in a language he didn't speak and Crest had grown up fluent in.

"What happens at the review," Aldric said. "If it goes forward. If nobody fixes it."

Rynn's jaw worked once before he answered, and when he did his voice had lost the last of its bravado entirely, gone flat and young in a way that made Aldric's chest tighten. "The steward told me, after. Said it kindly, the way you tell someone kindly because you've had to tell other people the same thing and it never gets easier to say. If the Board finds the duty unserved, it's not a second reprimand. It's treated as the *first* infraction never having been resolved — which means it goes up as a fresh case, on a record that already has Crest's name attached to what started it, and I don't have—" he stopped, started again. "There's no house behind my name to write a letter about it. No father on the Council to have a quiet word. For a noble kid, this is an afternoon's inconvenience and an apology nobody really means. For me it's—" he didn't finish the sentence. He didn't have to.

"Six days," Aldric said.

"Six days."

They sat with that for a moment, the corridor's ordinary noise going on around them — someone's laughter from the common room, a door somewhere, the whole indifferent machinery of a Tuesday afternoon that had no idea it was currently deciding whether Rynn got to stay in it.

Solen found them like that, which was not, Aldric had learned, ever entirely an accident. He read the paper over Rynn's shoulder without asking, the way he read most things, and was quiet long enough that Rynn started to fidget before he finally spoke.

"Six days," Solen said. "That's not a mistake's timeline. A mistake gets fixed in an afternoon, by whoever's embarrassed about it. Six days is a *board's* timeline — someone had to request a slot on a schedule that fills up weeks out. Which means this was requested before the steward's correction even reached the office."

"You can tell that from a date," Rynn said, not quite a question.

"I can tell that from having watched my father request slower things for faster reasons my whole life," Solen said. "It's not magic. It's paperwork. Paperwork is just arithmetic that hates you personally." He handed the notice back with the same careful two fingers he'd used to hold it, as if it might still be capable of getting worse by touch. "Whoever did this didn't want it fixed quietly. They wanted it fixed in a room with witnesses, on a date they chose, where the only version of events that gets to matter is the one written down first."

"That's grim, even for you," Rynn said.

"I contain multitudes," Solen said. "Most of them grim." He looked at Aldric, and something in his flat, precise face shifted a degree toward something that wasn't quite sympathy but stood near enough to it. "You've got the face again."

"I know."

"The arithmetic one."

"I know," Aldric said again, and this time didn't try to talk himself out of it. Aldric looked at the notice again, at the date circled at the bottom in the same flat official hand, and felt something settle into place that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with the particular, cold clarity he only ever felt right before he did something he wasn't entirely sure he should.

"I'm going to fix it," he said.

"How?"

"I don't know yet," Aldric admitted. "But I know what it isn't going to be. It isn't going to be me hitting him, and it isn't going to be me standing in an office trying to out-argue somebody whose family probably funds a wing of the building we're sitting in." He folded the notice once, carefully, the way Solen folded things when he wanted the corners square. "It's going to be something he doesn't see coming until it's already happened to him. He built this quietly. I'm going to take it apart the same way."

Rynn looked at him for a long moment — not with the relief Aldric had half expected, but with something more complicated, something that had *worry* in it alongside the gratitude, the specific worry of someone watching a friend pick up a weight they weren't sure they should be carrying alone.

"That's not really an answer," he said.

"No," Aldric agreed. "It's a deadline. Six days is enough time to find one. Ask me again in five."
