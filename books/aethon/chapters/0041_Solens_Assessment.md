# Chapter 41: Solen's Assessment

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


Nobody had asked Solen to do any of this. That was, Aldric was starting to realize, more or less the entire point.

He'd spent a good ten minutes looking for him after the crowd thinned following Crest's match — not urgently, just the low, habitual awareness of where his people were that he'd never quite managed to switch off, even in a stadium this size. He found Rynn first, still arguing with Seya about the honey stall's exchange rate for favors versus coin. He found Solen last, which was, he reflected, usually how it went.

"You're not competing," Aldric said, finding him exactly where he'd expected — a narrow gap between two sections of stands where the noise thinned out and the sightline to the fourth ring was, apparently, unusually good, if you knew to look for it. "You lost your own bracket match yesterday. I heard, secondhand, from someone who heard it from someone else, which is exactly how quietly you managed to lose it."

"I lost efficiently," Solen said, not looking away from the ring. "There's a difference between losing badly and losing without drawing attention to it. I chose the second one on purpose."

"That's still losing."

"It's losing *tidily.* I stand by the distinction." He tilted his head, the smallest fraction, toward the ring below, where two competitors Aldric didn't recognize were mid-exchange, one of them favoring a defensive style that read, even to Aldric's untrained eye, as unusually patient for a first-year. "Watch him. Not the one attacking. The one absorbing."

"Why?"

"Because in three matches, if the bracket holds the way I think it will, you're going to be standing across from him, and I'd rather you already know what he does before you find out the expensive way."

Aldric watched. The boy — narrow-shouldered, unhurried, dressed in colors Aldric didn't place — wasn't winning through force at all. He was winning through patience, letting his opponent spend everything trying to break a guard that gave just enough to absorb and never enough to actually crack, waiting for the exact moment exhaustion made the attacker sloppy before answering, once, precisely, and ending it.

"That's not a style," Aldric said slowly. "That's a whole personality."

"It's both. He's counter-based. Ward-and-return. He doesn't attack until you've already spent the thing that would have let you survive the answer." Solen's voice stayed perfectly flat, the way it always did when he was saying something that mattered to him more than the register let on. "His name's Tavish Ardell. House Ardell — minor branch, Varen's family ward, which is its own small story nobody's told me the whole of yet. Second seed."

"That's a lot of information for someone I've never spoken to."

"I've been watching him for two days. It's not information, it's a habit I couldn't switch off once I started."

Aldric turned that over, and found, underneath the flat delivery, something that hadn't been there in quite the same shape before — not just Solen's usual counting, but something closer to *care* about being right, about this specific piece of arithmetic landing correctly, in a way his eleven-second theories about a stadium's crowd noise never quite carried.

"You're doing this for me," Aldric said. Not a question.

"I'm doing it because it interests me. You benefiting from it is a secondary feature."

"Solen."

A pause. Longer than usual. "Fine. Both things are true. I don't see why they can't be."

It was, by Solen's standards, close to a confession, and Aldric let it sit there without pushing further, because pushing had never once gotten more out of Solen than patience did. Two years of this friendship, and Solen had a well-documented habit of declining to share what he'd counted — the burst in the yard, timed and never explained; the second-year at the window, named eighteen days late and only once he'd decided the delay itself had become its own small unkindness. Whatever this was, standing in a gap between stands doing unpaid reconnaissance on Aldric's behalf three rounds before it would matter, it wasn't that habit. It was something else, offered before being asked for, and Aldric didn't have a name yet for how much that mattered to him.

"What's he weak against?" Aldric asked instead.

"Nothing obvious. That's what makes him dangerous — not power, precision. He doesn't overextend. He doesn't get emotional. He waits exactly as long as it takes and not a second longer." Solen's eyes tracked the ring, calculating something Aldric couldn't follow. "The only real weakness I've found is that his patience assumes his opponent will eventually overextend on their own, the way most people do under enough pressure. If you don't — if you make him wait longer than his plan accounts for — I don't know what he does. I haven't seen it tested. Nobody's made him wait that long yet."

"That's not much of a weakness."

"It's the only one there is. I'd rather hand you one real gap than three imaginary ones."

Below them, the match ended the way Solen had predicted it would three exchanges earlier — Ardell's opponent, worn down and increasingly desperate, threw everything into one final overcommitted bolt, and Ardell simply waited it out, absorbing the last of it on a guard that hadn't moved more than an inch all match, before answering with a single precise counter that put the boy down clean.

The crowd noise for it was smaller than for Crest's match, smaller than for Aldric's own, the specific quiet reserved for a fighter whose skill was too controlled to be exciting to watch, which Aldric understood, now, was probably exactly how Ardell preferred it.

"He didn't even look tired," Aldric said.

"He wasn't. That's the whole point of the style. He spends almost nothing, the whole match, and lets you spend everything instead." Solen finally turned to look at Aldric directly, which he did rarely enough that it always landed with a small, deliberate weight when it happened. "You're going to meet him. Semifinal, if the bracket holds — no. Not the semifinal." Something shifted in his expression, a correction mid-thought. "The final. You're going to meet him in the final."

"You don't know that yet. The semifinal hasn't even been drawn."

"I know the bracket. I know who's left in each half. I've counted the remaining names on both sides more times than is probably healthy, and there's exactly one path through the other half that ends anywhere except across from you in a final, and it requires three separate upsets in a row against three people who have given me no reason, so far, to expect them to lose." Solen said this the way he said everything that mattered — without ceremony, flatly, before Aldric had a chance to brace for it. "You're going to fight Tavish Ardell in the final. I'd put money on it, if I were the kind of person who bet on things instead of the kind who just knows them."

Aldric stood with that for a moment, the arena noise going on around him, unbothered by the fact that something in it had just quietly rearranged itself. It should have felt like too much, said too early, with two whole rounds still standing between now and any final at all. It didn't. It felt, instead, like the first genuinely useful thing anyone had told him all week — not a warning, not a threat, just a fact, delivered flatly and far too soon, exactly the way Solen had once delivered a name that had taken eighteen days to arrive.

"You said that like it was nothing," Aldric said.

"It's not nothing. It's just already decided, as far as I can tell, and I don't see the use in being dramatic about a thing that's already decided." Solen looked back toward the ring, where the next match's competitors were already filing in. "I thought you'd rather know now than find out standing across from him with no warning at all."

"I would. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet. I might be wrong." A pause, and then, in the particular register Solen reserved for the one dry joke he allowed himself per conversation: "Though I'd remind you that I have, historically, not been."

Aldric almost smiled at that — would have, if the weight of what he'd just been handed hadn't settled in first, quiet and unglamorous and entirely his to carry now, three rounds early, with nobody around him yet aware that the whole shape of the tournament had just been quietly, correctly, predicted out loud by the one person in the year nobody thought to watch.

"Does anyone else know you can do this?" Aldric asked, once the next match's noise had started to swallow the space between them.

"Do what?"

"See the whole board before anyone else has finished reading their own corner of it."

Solen considered the question with more weight than it probably needed. "A few people suspect it. Nobody's asked directly before. You're the first."

"Why me?"

"Because you're the only one who'd actually use it instead of just being impressed by it." He said this without any particular warmth, the way he said most true things, and turned back toward the ring, already counting something new. "Impressed doesn't help either of us. Useful does."

Aldric didn't have an answer for that either, so he didn't try to give one. He stood there instead, beside the quietest, most exact person he knew, watching a stranger named Tavish Ardell win a fight without spending anything at all, and let the shape of the next several days rearrange itself around a single flat sentence that hadn't asked to be believed and had earned it anyway.
