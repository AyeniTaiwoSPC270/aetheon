# Chapter 1: The Boy Who Watched the Sky

### Saga 1, Arc 1

---

— Aldric Vane —

The herbs were hanging upside down again.

Aldric counted them as he pulled on his boots — bundles of grey-green and dusty brown suspended from the kitchen beam like a small, fragrant ceiling. Maren re-hung them every few days, rotating which ones went where, and he had long since given up trying to understand the system. There was a system. He was certain of that. He was equally certain it would take approximately forever to learn it, so he had decided not to bother.

"The ones on the left are for headaches," Maren said, without looking up from the pot.

He hadn't asked.

"I know," he said.

"You were counting them."

"I count things."

She made a sound that was not quite agreement and not quite amusement and set a bowl on the table in front of the chair he hadn't sat in yet. He sat in it. The porridge was the colour of winter mornings and smelled like honey and something faintly smoky, and he ate three bites before he remembered he was supposed to be thinking about something.

The mage at the harvest market.

The left hand. The way it had moved — not a gesture exactly, more like a correction, like adjusting the angle of something only the mage could see. And then the light had gathered between his fingers as though it had been waiting there all along.

Aldric tried it under the table. Two fingers extended, wrist tilted at what felt like the right angle. He focused. He waited.

Porridge steamed. A fly landed on the windowsill.

Nothing happened.

"Elbow off the table," Maren said.

He put his elbow back on the table, because it hadn't been on the table in the first place, his hand had been *under* it, and this was an unjust correction, but he had learned that pointing this out never went the way he hoped.

From the workshop came the sound of Josse's plane moving along a piece of timber — slow, even strokes, like a very relaxed heartbeat. The workshop shared a wall with the kitchen and Aldric had grown up hearing that sound. When he tried to picture quiet, it was always that sound he reached for first.

Maren came to the table and set down two things: a small piece of paper and a coin.

"Wyla's put aside the root oil," she said. "Tell her I'll settle the rest at the end of the week."

Aldric looked at the paper. One item, four words. He could have remembered this without paper.

"I know," she said, reading his expression. "Take the paper anyway."

He took the paper. He tucked the coin into his boot because his jacket had a hole in the left pocket — had had a hole for three weeks, and he kept forgetting to mention it, and so did she, and at this point it had become something that simply existed between them, like a joke that had never been spoken aloud.

The door to the workshop opened and Josse came through, wiping sawdust from his hands onto a cloth that was entirely sawdust already and therefore defeating its own purpose. He was not a tall man but he stood like one — solidly, without apology, taking up exactly the amount of space he intended to. He looked at Aldric in the particular way he had of looking at him: like he was checking something, quietly, that he didn't need to say out loud.

"Errand first," Josse said.

"I know."

"Then wherever you're going after."

"The field."

Josse nodded, as if this confirmed something reasonable. He reached into his vest pocket and set a second coin on the table beside Maren's paper. "For the bread," he said. "The round one, not the flat one. Your mother prefers the round one."

"I prefer the flat one," Maren said.

"The round one," Josse said, with great patience, and returned to the workshop.

Maren watched the door close. "Get the round one," she said.

"I was going to," Aldric said.

He ate the rest of his porridge, rescued the paper from where it had already crumpled slightly in his hand, and went out into the morning.

---

Ashford in early autumn was the best version of Ashford.

The harvest was done and the village hadn't yet braced for the cold, so everything sat in a brief interval of relief — the smell of cut grain still in the air, woodsmoke starting to layer in from chimneys, the trees at the north end of the main road running gold and orange and a red so deep it was almost brown. Aldric had lived here his whole life and he still noticed it, every year, the way the light changed in autumn. It came in at an angle that made ordinary things look like they were being remembered rather than seen.

He got the root oil from Wyla, who was a broad woman with enormous hands and the specific kind of efficiency that came from decades of not wasting movement, and who said nothing beyond the transaction because nothing beyond the transaction needed to be said. On the way out, a woman he didn't recognise — passing through, from the look of her, a trader's satchel over one shoulder — glanced at him and then glanced again, the way people did when they caught the white hair and the red eyes and couldn't quite account for them. Aldric was used to it. He kept walking.

He got the bread — round — from the bakery, where the smell hit him like a physical thing and made the coin feel very small, and then he went back out into the street and considered whether he had done enough of what he was supposed to do yet.

He had done all of it, technically.

He was free.

---

Daran found him two streets from home, which was not unusual. Daran fell into step beside him, dark-haired and already talking.

"You took forever," Daran said.

"I was on an errand."

"I know you were on an errand. I was *waiting*."

"You could have come to the door."

Daran considered this with the expression of someone reviewing a suggestion that had not occurred to them and did not intend to occur to them in future. "I have something to show you," he said, and held out his right hand.

His fingers were positioned in a configuration that Aldric recognised — just barely, from the same harvest market performance, the same mage, the same afternoon.

"Where did you—"

"Pamphlet." Daran's jaw set in the way it did when he was about to describe something he'd paid too much for and considered it worth every cost. "The man at the paper stall. I traded my lunch for a week."

"*A week?*"

"It's a *foundational positioning pamphlet.*" He said it like that, with the italics audible. "For basic mana channelling. *Foundational.*"

Aldric looked at the pamphlet hand. The fingers were not quite right — he was almost certain — but he couldn't have said with confidence what right looked like either. "Does it work?"

"I've been practising since yesterday." Daran focused. His brow furrowed in the way it did when he was running at something headlong, which was the way he ran at most things. He held the position for a long moment.

Nothing happened.

"...Maybe it takes longer," Aldric offered.

"It takes practice," Daran said, with enormous dignity. "I've had *one day.*"

Aldric shifted the bread to his other arm and looked at the hand configuration again. He put his own fingers in roughly the same arrangement, tilted his wrist. It felt strange — not uncomfortable, just unfamiliar, like the shape of something he'd almost held before.

A pressure flickered behind his sternum, there and gone so fast he wasn't certain it had been there at all. Like a coal shifting in a banked fire.

He blinked.

"Well?" Daran said.

"Nothing," Aldric said. "I don't think either of us are doing it right."

Daran took this as an opening to explain, at considerable length and with references to the pamphlet he had unfortunately not brought with him, what the correct technique probably was.

Aldric walked home beside him and listened and thought, privately, about the pressure, and what it had felt like, and whether it meant anything at all.

---

The field beyond the eastern fence was theirs in the way that things belong to children — not by law or arrangement, just by use. They had been coming here for three years. The grass grew long because nobody needed the field for anything, and there was a place where the ground rose slightly, just enough to see the south road between the trees, and on a clear afternoon the light came in flat and golden and made everything feel very far from Ashford and very close to somewhere else.

They dropped their things at the field's edge and worked for the better part of an hour.

*Worked* was a generous word. What they did was: attempt things. Repeat them. Argue about whether the attempt had been right. Attempt them again differently. Neither of them had significant magical instruction — the academy pamphlet aside, Daran's expertise came from watching, listening, and the specific overconfidence of an eight-year-old who had decided to be excellent at something. Aldric's came from the same places, with the addition of a memory that stuck to details like burrs, which was useful for watching mages perform and less useful when neither of them could confirm whether what they'd remembered was accurate.

Daran could hold the hand position for nearly a minute now without his arm getting tired. Nothing came of it, but the endurance felt like progress and he treated it as such.

Aldric kept trying different wrist angles. He wasn't sure what he was looking for. The pressure from this morning hadn't come back, and he'd stopped expecting it, which was probably why it caught him off guard when it happened again — brief, directionless, like someone pressing a thumb gently against the inside of his chest and then thinking better of it.

"*There*," he said, before he could stop himself.

Daran looked up. "There what?"

"Nothing." He lowered his hand. "I thought I— nothing. I think I had it wrong."

Daran watched him for a moment with the expression he got when he wasn't sure whether to push. He usually pushed. This time he didn't, which meant he'd filed it away for later, which was somehow worse.

They moved on. They practised things that didn't work and argued about *why* they didn't work and lay in the grass for a while and looked at clouds and debated, with complete confidence and no evidence, which one of the academy's six years was the hardest. Daran had opinions about curriculum sequencing that he had constructed entirely from pamphlets. Aldric had opinions about living arrangements that he had constructed entirely from imagination. Both of them argued as if they knew.

It was, Aldric thought, a very good afternoon.

---

The light had gone long and golden by the time he noticed the movement on the road.

He almost missed it. He'd been on his back, watching the sky redden at its edges, half-listening to Daran describing what he'd read about the academy's combat practicals, and then something at the corner of his eye made him turn his head.

A figure on the south road, between the treeline and the first bend. Moving steadily, unhurried. A staff in one hand, robes that caught the late light in a colour that wasn't quite brown and wasn't quite grey. Moving with the particular ease of someone who had walked a long way and intended to walk further and had made a private peace with both facts.

He sat up.

Daran stopped talking mid-sentence, followed his eyeline, and sat up too.

They watched.

The figure didn't know they were there. Didn't slow, didn't look toward the field. Just moved along the road with that unhurried certainty, and the staff in his hand caught the light once, twice, and then the road bent south and the trees took him, and there was only the road.

Neither of them said anything.

The grass shifted in a small wind. Somewhere in the village a door opened and someone called a name — someone else's name, nothing to do with them. The sky was going from gold to something softer.

Daran sat up straighter. He was looking at the road still, at the place where the bend was, at the nothing that was there now.

"We'll both get in," he said.

It wasn't a question. It didn't have the inflection of a question. It was the inflection of something already decided, stated aloud because it felt like the right thing to do with the silence.

Aldric looked at the empty road.

"Yeah," he said.

The light finished its change. The field went quiet. Somewhere above the treeline, the first star appeared — very faint, barely there, like a small thing testing whether it was welcome.

Neither of them moved for another minute.

Then Daran stood up and said he was going to be late for dinner, and Aldric said he probably was too, and they went back through the grass toward the fence, bickering about nothing, the way they always did when the day was ending and neither of them wanted to say so.
