# Chapter 9: The Weight of a Number

### Saga 1, Arc 3

### Saga 1, Arc 3

---

— Aldric Vane —

The assessment hall was on the Mage Quarter side of the border road, which meant the streets changed about ten minutes before they reached it.

Aldric noticed it the way he noticed most things — without deciding to. The packed earth gave way to fitted stone. The buildings stopped leaning into each other and stood upright, properly spaced, with the specific confidence of structures that had never needed to make room. The people moving through these streets moved differently too — not faster, not more purposefully, just with the ease of people who had always belonged in spaces like this and had no reason to think about it.

He kept walking.

The hall itself came into view around a gradual bend — stone-faced, three storeys, wider than anything in Ashford was wide. Not decorated. Just large, in the way of buildings that did not need to advertise what they were. A queue of applicants had already formed along the outer wall. Maybe thirty people. Mostly his age, a few older. The social gradient was visible before he was close enough to read anyone's face — in the cut of coats, the quality of boots, the particular ease of people who had been prepared for this specific day by tutors who had sat the same exam themselves.

Aldric was wearing his best clothes. He had been aware of this in the abstract since he packed them. He was aware of it now in a different way. He did not adjust anything.

Daran was already scanning the queue, cataloguing. "Mostly minor noble," he said, quietly. "A few from the outer districts. Two who look like they've had private instruction." He paused. "The one in the grey coat has been here before."

"How do you know?"

"He's not looking at the building."

Aldric looked. The boy in the grey coat was standing with his arms crossed, staring at the middle distance with the expression of someone waiting for something he had already done once to be over. Daran was probably right. Daran was usually right about things he had decided to pay attention to.

They joined the queue. A woman near the front glanced back at the new arrivals, her eyes catching on Aldric's hair — a second, automatic, the same second he'd gotten at the city gate — and then she turned back around. Behind them, two boys in matching dark coats spoke to each other in low voices that carried anyway.

Aldric filed it and waited.

---

Registration was brief. A clerk at a folding table checked their names against a list, confirmed their kingdom correspondence, and handed each of them a numbered tile. Aldric's was fourteen. Daran's was six — they would not be assessed in the same order. He had not thought about this. He noted it now and let it go.

The waiting hall was long and ceilinged and smelled of old stone and lamp oil. Rows of benches. The thirty applicants distributed themselves with the instinct of people sorting themselves into the groups they already belonged to — commoners toward the back and sides, nobles in clusters at the centre, a few people who occupied the ambiguous middle ground between them standing near the walls. Aldric and Daran sat near the back. Daran was reading the examination correspondence for what was, by Aldric's count, the eleventh time.

"You have that memorised," Aldric said.

"I know." He kept reading.

"You memorised it before we left Ashford."

"Checking for things I might have missed."

"In the document you have memorised."

Daran looked at him. It was not a hostile look. It was the look of someone who had decided this line of conversation was beneath engagement and was communicating that decision clearly and without malice.

Aldric looked at the ceiling. Stone vaulted, functional, with a crack running from the east corner toward the centre that someone had filled and then ignored. He filed the crack and waited.

---

Stage one was a long room with rows of desks and a proctor who explained the rules in the tone of a man who had explained the same rules so many times that they had ceased to carry individual meaning and had become a single sound. The exam was distributed face-down. Turned on instruction.

Aldric worked through it at a pace slightly faster than comfortable — circuit fundamentals first, which he knew, then elemental classification, which he had read everything he could find about and knew at the level of someone who had read everything they could find, which was not the same as knowing it the way the noble applicants knew it but was enough. Historical applications were the weakest section. He answered what he was confident about and made reasonable inferences for the rest.

He finished with time remaining. Sat back. Did not look at Daran.

The proctor called time. Around him, the scratch of quills stopping.

Daran's tile was face-down on the desk. He had finished earlier.

---

Stage two was individual. They were called by number into a smaller room off the main hall — a low-ceilinged space, practically furnished, with a measurement instrument on a stand at its centre. Crystal housing, calibrated steel frame, a glass indicator column that Aldric recognised as the same category of tool the travelling assessor had used in Ashford, built to a different standard. More precise. More expensive. The kind of instrument that did not guess.

He waited on the bench outside with the others, counting down the numbers ahead of him. The candidates who came out gave nothing — tight faces, or carefully neutral faces, or the flushed relief of people who had passed a threshold they had been afraid of. None of them said anything. The room ate the information and kept it.

Daran was number six. He went in. He came out four minutes later.

Aldric looked at him.

Daran sat down. His face was the same face — controlled, sealed, jaw set. But there was something behind it now that had not been there in the inn, or on the road, or in the waiting hall. Not readable from the outside. Just present, in the way a change in air pressure was present before weather arrived.

He did not say anything. Aldric did not ask.

Numbers seven through thirteen came and went. The bench emptied one seat at a time.

"Fourteen."

Aldric stood. He walked into the room.

---

The examiner was a woman in faculty grey, middle-aged, with the economical attention of someone who had assessed several hundred candidates and had learned to see what mattered quickly. She did not look at his hair. She explained the procedure without inflection: approach the instrument, place both hands on the contact plates, channel output at a comfortable sustained level for five seconds on her count.

He approached. The crystal housing came to about his chest height. He placed his hands on the plates — cool metal, worn smooth at the contact points by years of palms.

"When you're ready."

He had been ready. He channelled — or did what he understood channelling to be, which was to reach for the thing that was always present somewhere below his sternum and let it move outward at a measured pace. Not the pressure sensation. Not the thing from Chapter 6. The ordinary part of him, the part that had always been there and had responded to the preliminary assessor in Ashford and had been clean then and would be clean now.

The indicator column in the crystal housing lit.

The examiner watched it. Her expression did not change. The column climbed — past the lower gradations, past the midpoint, continuing. Her eyes moved to the instrument. Back to the column. She made a small adjustment to something on the frame — not a correction, a calibration check — and looked at the reading again.

She picked up her stylus. Made a notation on the sheet in front of her. The notation was longer than the ones she had been making.

"Thank you," she said. "You can step back."

He stepped back. She did not look at him again. He walked out.

He did not know what she had written. He had no frame for what the column's height meant in absolute terms — no reference point, nothing to compare it to. The preliminary assessor in Ashford had checked her instrument twice. This examiner had checked her calibration. The pattern was the same. He filed it in the same place he had filed it in Ashford and kept walking.

---

Stage three was outside.

A walled yard behind the main building, open to the sky, flagstoned and clean. A training construct stood at its centre — an enchanted practice form in the rough shape of a human torso mounted on a weighted post, its surface a dull iron-grey that shifted and darkened when active. One at a time. The others observed from behind a marked rope line. Two faculty examiners sat at a table to the side, scoring sheets in hand.

The construct adapted. That was what the candidate ahead of him said, quietly, to no one in particular — it adjusts to your output, finds your ceiling, tests against it. Aldric filed this and watched.

The noble boy in the grey coat went fourth. He had been here before — his technique showed it, clean and economical, a structured approach that produced solid scores without drama. He knew how to perform for an assessment. Aldric noted what he did and did not try to replicate it.

His own turn. He stepped inside the rope. The construct activated — the iron-grey surface brightening, weight shifting, the post settling into a balanced stance that was not a fighting stance but was close to one.

He worked. Not brilliantly — he did not have the formal vocabulary of technique the trained applicants had, the named positions and precise sequencing. What he had was adaptability and output, and the construct found both. It pushed. He adjusted. He produced what was asked of him without producing more than was necessary and without reaching for the pressure sensation, which was present and available and not something he was going to touch in a walled yard with faculty examiners six feet away.

He stepped back. He could not read the scorers' faces.

Then Daran.

He stepped inside the rope and the construct activated and Aldric understood, watching, that the previous four candidates had been doing a different thing. Not worse. A different thing. Daran moved with the specific efficiency of someone who had not studied the problem but had become the answer to it — no excess, no adjustment, just clean and fast and absolutely certain in every motion. The construct adapted. Daran adapted faster. The faculty examiners stopped writing at one point, then resumed. The construct's surface went darker than it had for any previous candidate — straining toward whatever its ceiling was — and Daran simply continued.

He finished. Stepped back. His breathing was even.

Around the rope line, two or three of the noble applicants looked at each other. No one said anything.

Aldric looked at Daran's face. Still sealed. Still carrying the thing from the practical room that he had not named and Aldric had not asked about.

It does not matter, Aldric thought, without meaning to think it. And then, immediately, because the thought had already arrived and could not be unfiled: *it does not matter how well he did. The combat stage can't—*

He stopped.

He stopped, and he looked at the thing he had not been looking at, and he understood what Daran's face had been doing since he came out of the practical room.

He said nothing. He walked back to the bench.

---

The wait was forty minutes.

The waiting hall again. The applicants distributed differently now — some clustered, some solitary, the specific topology of people who had done everything they could and were now purely subject to outcome. Aldric sat. Daran sat beside him and said nothing and Aldric said nothing and the forty minutes passed the way long silences pass, which is both quickly and not at all.

He did not look at Daran. He looked at the cracked ceiling.

He did not think about Ashford. He did not think about a field at dusk, or a pamphlet of hand positions, or two boys making a promise they both believed completely. He did not think about any of it. The drawer was closed and it was going to stay closed because if he opened it now he did not know what would come out and it was not the time and there was nothing to be done about any of it and he did not know for certain and there was still a chance, there was always still a chance until there wasn't—

The lead examiner entered the hall with a sheet of paper.

The room went quiet.

---

He read alphabetically. The family names of Valdenmere's minor noble houses came first, and each name called produced a small movement — a figure rising, stepping forward, the cluster at the centre thinning. Aldric watched the sheet in the examiner's hand and counted without meaning to.

A commoner name. Then another. The hall continued to thin.

"Vane."

He stood. He turned.

Daran was still sitting on the bench.

Aldric stood there for one second. Two. He looked at Daran's profile — the set of his jaw, the stillness of him, the way he was looking at the middle distance at nothing that was there. The examiner was still reading. Someone jostled past Aldric's shoulder, moving toward the front.

Daran did not look at him.

The last names were called. The examiner folded his sheet. Around Aldric, the successful candidates were beginning to move and talk — relief finding its voice, the ordinary noise of people who had gotten what they came for. Someone to his left laughed at something. The sound was very loud.

He looked back at the bench.

Daran was gone.

Not gone — walking. Already at the far end of the hall, moving toward the exit with the unhurried, deliberate pace of someone who had decided where he was going before he stood up. He did not look back. He passed through the door and the door swung behind him and he was not there anymore.

Aldric stood in the space where he had been sitting.

The noise of the hall continued around him — conversations starting, names being exchanged, the bureaucratic continuation of the process, the clerk at his folding table already directing the successful candidates toward the next steps. All of it continuing. The world doing what it did, which was continue.

He stood there.

---

— Daran —

He had known in the practical room.

Not hoped against. Not calculated odds. Known — in the specific, quiet way of something you have always suspected becoming the thing you can no longer not know. The instrument's column had climbed and stopped and the examiner had noted it and moved him through and he had walked back to the bench and sat down and begun the process of deciding what to do with a fact that could not be undone.

The combat yard had been — what. Real. Everything he had was real. The construct had found his ceiling and he had exceeded it and the faculty examiners had stopped writing and none of it counted for anything he needed it to count for, which was also real, and which confirmed the thing the instrument had already told him: the world had a system, and the system had a door, and the door required something he did not have and could not train into existence and had never had.

His name was not called.

He stood up while the examiner was still reading — while there were still three names left on the sheet — because he already knew and because staying to hear the end of a list he was not on was not something he was going to do. He walked to the door. He did not look at Aldric, who was standing in the middle of the hall holding his tile and not moving, white-haired and red-eyed and accepted, which was the thing they had both wanted and which was now only one of them's thing.

He pushed through the door.

Outside the air was different from inside — hotter, direct, the full weight of the afternoon on the stone. He stood on the steps for a moment and looked at the street. The Mage Quarter going about its business. People who were already whatever they had been going to become, moving through a city that had accommodated them.

The promise had been real. He needed to be clear about that. He was not revising it, was not deciding retroactively that it had been naïve or foolish or that he had known it would break. It had been real. They had both believed it completely.

That was the thing that could not be undone.

He went down the steps. He turned away from the inn, because the inn was the direction things had been and that was not the direction he was going. He did not know yet what direction he was going. He would find out.

He walked.
