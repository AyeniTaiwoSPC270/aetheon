# Chapter 22: Lirien Is Already Watching

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

He has been staring at the back of Lirien's head for eleven minutes before he notices he's doing it, which is precisely the kind of failure of tradecraft that would have gotten him laughed out of a village he no longer entirely has the right to miss.

"You're doing the thing again," Rynn whispers, not quietly enough, from the desk to his left.

"I'm not doing a thing."

"You're doing a thing where you look at the side of somebody's head like it owes you money."

"I'm thinking about circuit density," Aldric says, which is a lie so poorly constructed that even he can hear the seams in it.

"Sure you are," Rynn says, and goes back to his own diagram, which is, as usual, technically correct and drawn with the confidence of a man who has never once doubted a line he put on a page, right up until the moment it turns out to be wrong.

"He's been at this since we sat down," Solen observes, from Aldric's other side, not whispering at all, in the specific carrying flatness of a man who has never once seen the point of lowering his voice for something everyone in a four-desk radius has already noticed. "Eleven minutes, by my count. I'm curious how long it runs before you notice yourself."

"I noticed myself several sentences ago," Aldric says. "I'm simply choosing not to react to being observed, which is, I'll remind you, the entire premise of this class."

"It is not the premise of this class."

"It's the premise of my morning."

Mana Theory meets in the same steep half-bowl of benches it always has, Maret at the front with his ledger open and his coat doing whatever it is Maret's coat does when he isn't actively using the pocket that matters, and today's diagram is a mana-storage array with a fault Aldric spots almost before Maret has finished chalking the last line of it — a containment ring drawn one increment too wide for its own throughput, the kind of thing that would bleed slowly rather than fail all at once. He does not say so aloud. Being right in this room has a cost, and he has already spent this term's quota of Maret's four-second pauses on things he didn't mean to be right about.

Lirien sits two rows ahead and one seat to the left, which puts her, by the specific geometry of this room, directly and unhelpfully inside his sightline whenever he is meant to be looking at the board. She has not turned around once. She has not, as far as Aldric has been able to determine across several sessions of increasingly undignified observation, looked at him at all since the yard — not the glance over a shoulder that even Crest occasionally spares him, not the sideways calculation Seya does so openly it barely counts as subtlety. Nothing. A closed door with the light left on behind it, which is somehow worse than a door that's simply shut.

He wants to know why. It has become, over four days, an actual want, distinct from curiosity — the specific, itching need to understand a thing that has decided not to be understood, made sharper by the fact that she is one of exactly two people in this building who watched the ring in Bavel's yard and drew a conclusion from it, and the only one who drew a conclusion Aldric cannot begin to guess at.

"Halvon," Maret says, without inflection, "the array."

Crest answers cleanly — he always answers cleanly, the polish is the one thing about him that never slips — and somewhere in the middle of a correct sentence about return-path throughput, his eyes drift sideways, find Aldric's face, and follow it precisely far enough to see what it's aimed at.

"Vane's not listening," Crest says, mild as milk, not breaking his own answer's rhythm. "Vane's doing inventory."

A ripple of quiet laughter, the kind that costs Crest nothing and costs Aldric a half-second of his composure he'd rather not have spent. Lirien does not turn around. If anything, Aldric thinks — and he is aware, thinking it, that he may simply be looking for a reaction that isn't there — her shoulders set very slightly straighter, the way a person's do when they've heard something and decided, deliberately, not to have heard it.

"I was reading the diagram," Aldric says, mild right back, "which is more than I can say for the last time you tried it."

"One. I read it until it fell apart," someone mutters from the third row, in a passable imitation of his own voice from a month ago, and Aldric can't even be annoyed, because it is, on reflection, a fair hit.

Maret does not comment on any of it. Maret's pen does not so much as pause. Aldric has learned, across enough sessions now to call it a pattern, that Maret only stops for the things that matter to him, and apparently a boy staring at the back of a girl's head does not currently rank.

Aldric's own partner for the stability exercise is Rynn, which both of them privately understand to be a mismatch bordering on comedy — Rynn's idea of talking someone out of their concentration involves volume rather than precision, and Aldric spends the first attempt entirely undisturbed, holding his construct steady while Rynn works through an increasingly desperate catalogue of provocations that includes his hair, his eating habits, and, with real creative effort, a wholly invented rumour about his relationship with a goat.

"That's not even a good rumour," Aldric says, not moving, not dropping the construct by so much as a flicker. "You didn't even name the goat."

"The goat isn't the point, Vane, the goat is a *device*—"

"Swap partners," Maret says, somewhere behind them, and the room reshuffles for a second round, and it is in the reshuffle — desks scraping, partners crossing paths, everyone's attention scattered across six simultaneous small failures of concentration — that Aldric finds his own sightline drift, entirely without his permission, back toward the one desk in the room he has been instructed by every functioning part of his own judgment to stop watching.

It is twenty minutes later, in the second half of the practical exercise — the same stability drill, hold a small mana construct steady while your partner tries to talk you out of your own concentration, an exercise Aldric privately suspects Maret invented specifically to watch which students crack under a voice instead of a spell — that he sees it.

Lirien is not paired with anyone he can see clearly; the desks have shifted for the exercise, and her partner is turned half away, murmuring something designed to be irritating. Lirien's own hands are steady around whatever she's holding, unhurried, the same economy she brought to three bolts at eleven-second intervals in a yard that thought it was watching something else. But at the corner of her desk, where her left hand rests against the wood while her right does the actual work, there is, for just a moment, a thin white rim of frost creeping outward from her fingers across the grain — not drawn there, not intended, the kind of leak that happens when concentration is being spent somewhere other than where it's supposed to go.

It vanishes almost as soon as he sees it. Her hand moves, unhurried, wipes the desk clean with the side of her palm as though brushing away chalk dust, and the moment is gone so completely that if Aldric hadn't been looking — hadn't spent eleven ill-advised minutes doing exactly the thing Rynn accused him of — he would never have known it happened at all.

It is such a small thing. It is, by any reasonable measure, nothing — a first-year's control slipping for half a second under an exercise built to make control slip. But Aldric knows what a leak at the seams looks like from the inside, knows the specific, private embarrassment of a thing escaping you that you'd rather keep filed, and the frost on Lirien's desk is not the frost of someone thinking about nothing in particular. It is the frost of someone thinking, very hard, about something she does not want anyone in this room — least of all him — to know she's thinking about.

He does not say anything. There is, he understands with some force, nothing to be gained from saying anything; whatever this is, it belongs to her exactly as much as the burst in the yard belongs to him, and he has spent his whole life learning what it costs to have something like that noticed before you're ready.

But he knows, now, with a certainty that has nothing to do with guesswork, that whatever she saw when she turned away from the ring to watch the gate instead, it did not leave her as unmoved as the rest of her has spent four days insisting it did.

Class ends the way it always ends, with Maret closing the ledger and saying nothing further, and the room breaking apart into its usual currents — Crest and his orbit toward the door first, because Crest always leaves first, Nessa and Essa somewhere in the middle, Pell lingering by the window as though the room might tell him something on the way out that it didn't tell him during. Lirien is gone before Aldric has finished packing his own satchel, out and down the corridor at the unhurried pace of someone who has never once in her life needed to hurry, and by the time he reaches the door there is nothing left of her to watch except the fact that she was there.

"You're doing the face again," Rynn says, falling into step beside him.

"I don't have a face."

"You have several faces. That one's the thinking-too-hard one. You had it in the yard, you had it after the warden business you still haven't told me about properly, and you've got it now." Rynn considers this with the specific seriousness he reserves for questions he actually cares about the answer to. "Is it about her?"

"It's about the diagram," Aldric says, which fools no one, including himself.

He carries it back to the dormitory with him, through the corridor, up the stairs, past the window that used to interest him considerably more than it does tonight — and it is only once he's lying in the dark, listening to Rynn's breathing even out across the room, that he realises what's actually strange about the evening. It isn't the frost. It isn't Crest's jab, or Maret's silence, or the particular shape of not being looked at by someone who is, apparently, looking at everything else in the room instead.

It's that he hasn't mapped a single exit since he walked in.

Fifteen weeks. Every room, every corridor, every yard and stairwell and lecture hall he has set foot in since the carriage brought him through Greyveil's gates — cataloged on arrival, without exception, without his even deciding to do it, the same reflex that once counted a village down to its last countable evening and, four nights ago, counted a warden's lantern and a yard's four exits before the man had finished introducing himself. It has never once failed him. It has never once asked his permission, either — it simply happens, the way breathing happens, a habit built by a childhood that taught him exactly what it costs to be the last one who notices a room has changed.

Tonight he came in, sat down, and thought about a girl who won't look at him instead, and the door, the window, the distance to both, went entirely unrecorded. He does not know what to do with that fact except turn it over, once, twice, in the dark, the way he might turn over a stone he isn't sure is worth keeping.

He lies there turning that over for longer than the fact should reasonably require, and underneath it, quieter, the question that actually keeps him awake: she saw everything in that yard — the burst, the ring, Maret arriving unseen at the gate — and she has spent four days behaving as though she saw nothing at all.

He would very much like to know why the only person who saw everything is the one person pretending she didn't.
