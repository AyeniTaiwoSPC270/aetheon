# Chapter 5: The Road Begins Here

### Saga 1, Arc 1

### Saga 1, Arc 1

---

— Aldric Vane —

He was up before the household was.

Not by much. The sky through the window above his bed was the specific grey that came just before the light committed to being light, and the village outside was quiet in the way it was only for that one hour — before the market stalls went up, before the first carts came through on the south road, before Ashford remembered it had things to do. Aldric lay still for a moment longer than he needed to. Then he got up.

His pack was already assembled. He had done it the night before, methodically, the way he did things he didn't want to think about too hard — keeping his hands busy so his head didn't have to work. Spare clothes. The pamphlet, folded into the inner pocket where it wouldn't crease. The small amount of coin Josse had pressed into his hand three days ago without comment. Everything accounted for. Nothing left to do but go.

The workroom door was open. Through it he could see Maren's jars on the lower shelf, arranged in their specific order that was not alphabetical, not by size, not by anything Aldric had ever been able to categorise — just the order she had always kept them in, the one that made sense to her hands. A shallow bar of early light was beginning to come through the south-facing window and lay across the drying rack. The herbs hanging from the ceiling beams moved slightly in a draught from somewhere.

He stood in the doorway for a moment. He was not aware he had stopped walking.

Then Maren came in from the other side and he straightened and kept moving, because standing in doorways looking at jars was not something he planned to be caught doing on the morning of his departure.

She had a cloth bundle in her hands. She set it on the table beside his pack without ceremony.

"Road food," she said. "Nothing that'll turn."

"I know how to eat."

"You know how to forget to eat. Different thing."

He picked up the bundle. It was heavier than it looked. He didn't ask what was in it.

Maren moved to the worktable and began doing something with her hands — sorting, checking, the morning work that started before everything else and would still be going after he'd been on the road for an hour. Her back was to him. He watched her for a second.

"Maren."

She turned. Looked at him directly, the way she always had — not past him, not around him, but at the specific person standing there.

"Travel well," she said.

That was all. It was enough.

He was still figuring out where to put what she'd just done to him when Josse came in from outside, already returned from wherever he'd been — the woodpile, probably, or the fence along the back that always needed something. He looked at Aldric's pack, then at Aldric.

"Keep your boots dry the first day," he said. "If you start wet you stay wet."

Aldric looked down at his boots, then up at Josse. "We're four days from summer rain."

"Doesn't matter. Rule holds."

There was nothing to say to this that improved on it, so Aldric didn't try. He shouldered his pack. Josse clapped him once on the shoulder — brief, firm, the same weight as everything Josse did — and stepped back. Maren had turned back to her work.

Aldric left.

---

The south road ran through the edge of Ashford before it became actually the south road, which meant he had a few minutes of village to move through before the fields opened up and the journey was real. Morning was happening around him. The baker two streets over had his door propped open and the smell came out warm and specific. A cart was being loaded outside the farrier's — a big horse standing with the practised patience of an animal that had done this before. Somewhere up near the well, two people were having the early version of an argument that would probably finish itself by afternoon.

He passed Wyla's stall. She was setting out her goods in the same order she always did — root oil at the front, dry goods behind, the small locked box for the things people bought quietly. She didn't look up. He didn't stop. The account between the Vanes and Wyla would presumably persist without him in it.

He kept moving. His feet knew the village. Left at the baker, straight past the guild hall, down the gentle slope toward the south road marker — a carved stone post that had been there longer than anyone he'd ever asked about it. The stone had a crack down the middle from some winter years ago and someone had filled it with mortar that had never quite matched. He had walked past it more times than he could count. He looked at it the way he looked at everything this morning — the way he always did, which was the problem, because the way he always did suddenly felt different from the inside and he couldn't work out if anything had actually changed or if that was just him.

He decided it was just him. Filed it. Kept walking.

---

Daran was already there.

Of course he was. He was standing at the road's proper edge — the point where the packed-earth village track gave way to the broader stone-laid road that the kingdom maintained — with his pack on and his eyes on the middle distance, like he'd been waiting long enough to start thinking about the destination rather than the departure. He heard Aldric coming and turned.

"You're slow," he said.

"I had breakfast."

"I had breakfast and I'm still here first."

"You live closer to the south road than I do."

Daran considered this. "That's not the point."

"What is the point?"

"The point is I'm here and you're just arriving, which I am noting for the record."

"The record no one keeps."

"I keep it," Daran said, already turning back toward the road, already moving. "Get your count up. We're losing light."

They weren't losing light — the morning was just beginning to properly arrive, the grey tipping into gold at the horizon ahead of them — but Aldric fell into step alongside him without arguing, because this was how it always went and he didn't see any reason today should be different.

---

The road south from Ashford was the kind of road that made walking feel like the correct speed for the world. Wide enough for two carts to pass without trouble, stone-laid with good drainage on either side, lined at intervals with the old wayfarer's markers that told you how far to the next village, the next water, the next place a traveller might sleep. The countryside around it was the low rolling farmland of central Valdris — the same fields Aldric had grown up looking at, golden now in the late summer, stubble from the first harvest cut and the second growth coming in low and green.

Daran was talking about the practical assessment.

"—the circuit evaluation is the part I want to be clean on," he was saying. "The written I'm not worried about. The combat section should be straightforward. But the circuit evaluation is the one with the most variables because you can't fully control how the instrument reads you on a given day, and I want to understand what their weighting actually is between raw output and circuit quality—"

"I don't think they publish the weighting."

"No, but there are accounts. From people who went through it. There was a section in the back of the pamphlet about what the examiners are generally looking for in the practical stage and it mentioned circuit efficiency specifically as distinct from raw output, which implies they're scoring them separately, which means—"

"You read the back of the pamphlet?"

Daran stopped walking for exactly one step. "I read the whole pamphlet."

"I know you read the whole pamphlet. I meant the back. The appendix section."

"It has useful information."

"It has three pages of administrative notes about the appeals process."

"Which I may need."

"You're not going to need the appeals process, Daran."

"No," Daran said, with the specific quality of certainty that meant he'd decided something and filed it somewhere load-bearing, "I'm not. But I read it anyway."

Aldric laughed — not a polite laugh, a real one, the kind that came out before he'd decided to make it. Daran's mouth did the thing it did when he was trying not to be pleased about something, which meant he almost certainly was.

They walked.

The sun was properly up now, the gold going white at the edges the way it did when the day was going to be warm. Around them the fields were doing what fields did — growing, sitting, being entirely unconcerned by the two boys on the road. A hawk was working a thermal over the far tree line. Two crows were having a disagreement on a fence post. The world was going about its business.

The pressure came when he was mid-sentence.

He'd been saying something — about the exam, probably, or about Daran's obsessive pamphlet literacy, something ordinary — and then it was there, the same way it was always there: no announcement, no build, just present. Moving through the centre of him. The particular quality of pressure that was not quite heat and not quite weight and not quite either of those things and didn't have a name he'd found for it yet.

He didn't break stride.

He didn't finish his sentence immediately — there was a gap of maybe a second, the kind of gap that could be a breath or a pause or nothing — and then he picked up where he'd been and kept going. Daran was already responding to whatever he'd said. The conversation continued.

The pressure passed. He filed it. Same as always.

Daran was saying something about the weighting of the combat section relative to the practical assessment and whether sheer technique could offset a lower output score and what the historical pass rate had been for applicants from non-mage families. Aldric listened. He responded. He was present in the conversation.

He was also, underneath that, not thinking about the feeling that had just moved through him and left again. He was very specifically not thinking about it, which was almost the same thing as not thinking about it, and would have to do.

---

Ashford went away.

There was no dramatic moment of it — no hill he crested to look back, no clearing of trees that gave a final view. The road bent south-west around a long curve, following the line of the old drainage channel that ran along the field edge, and by the time the curve was done Ashford was behind the tree line and there was nothing behind them but road and sky.

Daran was in the middle of a sentence. Aldric was in the middle of listening to it.

The road ahead was long and pale in the morning light, running straight toward the first wayfarer's marker and then curving out of sight. Four days to Valdris Prime. The formal exam at the end of it. Everything that came after.

He didn't look back.

The road was there. They were on it.

Daran finished his sentence. Aldric responded. They kept walking.
