# Chapter 32: Sable's Warning

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"I don't actually know what having a name is supposed to change," Aldric admitted, three days after Solen had handed it to him mid-sentence about a seating chart. "I thought it would feel like progress. It mostly feels like homework I haven't started."

"That's because it is homework," Rynn said. "You've got a name and no idea what to do with it. That's the worst kind of information. Worse than no information at all, honestly, because at least *no information* doesn't sit there looking at you."

"Solen would know what to do with it."

"Solen already told you what he's doing with it. He's cross-referencing. That's Solen for *waiting, but with paperwork.*"

"You make it sound like an insult."

"It's not an insult, it's a translation. Everything Solen does has a boring word and an exciting word, and he only ever tells you the boring one. 'Cross-referencing.' You know what that actually is? That's *detective work.* He's doing detective work and calling it filing, because if he called it detective work he'd have to admit he found it fun, and admitting things are fun appears to be against some private code of his."

"He'd deny that."

"He'd deny it in the most boring possible sentence, which would itself be proof," Rynn said, with the satisfaction of a man who had just, in his own estimation, won an argument against someone who wasn't present to lose it.

Aldric had turned the name over enough times in three days that it had stopped feeling like a discovery and started feeling like a stone in a pocket — present, unremarkable most of the time, occasionally caught between two fingers when he wasn't paying attention. He knew what Varen was, on paper: second-year, well regarded, unaffiliated with Crest by any thread Solen had found. He did not know what Varen wanted, and a name with no want attached to it was, he'd come to agree with Rynn, closer to a puzzle piece than an answer.

Nobody in his own group had a way to find out more. Rynn's information stopped, reliably, at gossip he'd already repeated twice. Solen's stopped at whatever the written record would confirm, and a second-year's private reasons for watching a first-year did not, apparently, appear in any written record Solen had access to. That left, as far as Aldric could tell, exactly one avenue worth pursuing, and it was the one he liked least: someone who actually knew Varen, asked directly, on Varen's own terms rather than at a careful, deniable remove.

He didn't get the chance to choose when that conversation happened. It chose him instead, two days later, in a gap between classes he hadn't planned to spend anywhere in particular.

---

There was a narrow cut-through behind the library's east wall that Aldric had catalogued in his first week and never once used — a strip of flagstone barely wide enough for two people abreast, running between the library's outer stone and the low wall of the kitchen block, roofed by nothing but a scrap of the same grey sky that covered the rest of the academy. Students used it, when they used it at all, to avoid the crowded main corridor between sessions. Aldric had catalogued it, in his first week, as *shortcut, unremarkable, rarely occupied* and had never once had reason to revisit the entry.

He revisited it now because someone was standing in the middle of it, plainly waiting, in a spot too deliberate to be coincidence.

She was older than him — second-year, by the look of her, though nothing about her collar told him which house, if any — with the specific, weathered self-possession of someone who had earned her place at the academy the hard way and had stopped, a long time ago, needing anyone's permission to occupy a space. Her clothes were plain, well-kept, mended rather than replaced, the visible economy of a student who had arrived without the cushion most of the year's second-years took for granted and had simply never seen a reason to pretend otherwise. She didn't smile. She didn't do anything designed to put him at ease, which he noticed immediately, because most people who wanted something from him at least attempted the performance first.

"You're Vane," she said. Not a question.

"I am. You have the advantage of me."

"Sable." She offered nothing else — no house, no year beyond what he could already guess, no explanation for why she'd chosen this particular gap of stone and sky to stand in and wait for him. "You've been asking about a name. Badly, from what I hear. You asked Croft. You asked Pell. Neither of them told you anything useful, and both of them mentioned it to someone, who mentioned it to someone else, and eventually it reached me."

Aldric felt the specific, cold recalibration of a man discovering his careful discretion had not, in fact, been discreet at all.

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. Comfort wasn't the errand I came here to run." Sable studied him for a moment with an expression that gave away exactly as little as her voice did — not hostile, not warm, simply present, the way a wall is present. He'd braced, walking into this, for something closer to Crest's register: the oblique threat, the courteous cruelty, the sense of a performance being run at his expense. He got none of that. What he got instead was worse, in its own quiet way, because there was nothing in it to push against.

"You know Varen," Aldric said.

"I know Varen better than anyone at this academy who isn't Varen." She said this the way Solen reported a lamp-bracket count — a fact, stated flatly, with no evident pride in the ownership of it. "Which is why I'm here instead of him. He doesn't know I'm having this conversation. He'd be annoyed if he found out. I've decided that's an acceptable cost."

"Cost of what?"

"Of telling you the one thing you actually need to hear, since apparently nobody else has bothered." Sable's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. "His interest in you isn't nothing. You've already worked that out — you wouldn't have gone asking Croft and Pell about it otherwise. What you haven't worked out, because nobody's told you, is that his interest has a cost attached to it. Not to him. To you."

"What kind of cost."

"I don't know yet. That's not me being careful. That's me being honest — I genuinely don't know what it is, only that it exists, and that it's the kind of thing that doesn't announce itself until it's already happening to you." She said it the way a physician might describe a symptom she couldn't yet diagnose: plainly, without comfort, because comfort would have been a lie and she plainly had no use for those. "I've watched him take an interest in exactly one other person, before you. It didn't go anywhere good for that person, and it wasn't because Varen meant harm. He doesn't. That's almost the worst part of it."

Aldric felt something in his chest go very still, the same stillness he'd felt standing across from Lirien in an empty yard, except colder, because Lirien, at least, had been telling him something about himself. This was something about a stranger, aimed at him, from a source with no visible reason to care whether he understood it or not.

"Why are you telling me this," he said. "If it costs you, why bother?"

"Because I watched the other person find out the cost the hard way, and I decided I'd rather not watch it happen twice." For the first time, something in Sable's flat composure shifted — not warmth, exactly, but something adjacent to it, the specific weariness of someone who had carried a regret for longer than she wanted to and had finally found a use for it. "That's the whole of my reason. I'm not warning you off him because I dislike you. I don't know you well enough to dislike you. I'm warning you because I liked the last person enough to wish someone had warned them, and nobody did."

"What happened to them?"

Sable looked at him for a long moment, and something in her expression told Aldric, with a clarity he didn't especially want, that this was the question she'd been waiting for and had already decided not to answer.

"That's not mine to tell you," she said. "It's theirs. Ask them yourself, if you ever work out who they are — which you won't, not from me, and not soon." She straightened, the conversation visibly closing itself the way Solen's conversations closed, except without any of Solen's care about softening the edge of it. "I've said what I came to say. His interest has a cost. I don't know its shape yet. When I do, or when you do, one of us should probably tell the other."

"Wait — "

She was already moving, unhurried, the narrow gap between the library wall and the kitchen block swallowing her the same way it had produced her, without ceremony, without a backward glance, and Aldric stood alone in the strip of grey daylight with considerably less certainty than he'd walked in with.

He had a name, and now a warning, and the warning had somehow made the name feel less solved rather than more — a shape with weight to it now, and no edges he could see clearly enough to plan around. He thought of Sable's flat, unbothered honesty, and of the specific, deliberate way she'd declined the one question that might have actually helped him, and understood, standing there, that he had just been handed exactly as much as she'd intended to give him and not one word more.

He was three days closer to understanding Varen than he had been a week ago, and somehow further from feeling settled about any of it than he'd been the whole eighteen days he'd spent simply not knowing the name at all. He filed the conversation the way he filed most things that didn't have an answer yet — carefully, completely, in a drawer that was, he was beginning to suspect, never going to run out of room.

*His interest has a cost. I don't know its shape yet.*

Neither, now, did Aldric. He carried that into his next session and sat through the whole of it without hearing a single word of the lecture, turning the sentence over the way Solen turned a folded page — looking, without much hope, for a corner that would finally square.

He told them, that evening, a version of it — the name attached to a face now, the warning without the weight of who'd delivered it, because some part of him wasn't ready yet to explain why a second-year he'd never met had gone out of her way to find him in a gap between buildings nobody used.

"A cost," Rynn repeated, turning the word over the way he turned over most things, slowly and with real suspicion, as though it might be hiding something smaller and more specific inside it. "That's very ominous for something with no actual details attached. I don't love it. I'd have preferred a number. A cost with a number is a problem. A cost with no number is just a *feeling*, and feelings are much harder to plan around."

"Welcome to my week," Aldric said.

Solen, across the table, didn't say anything for a long moment — which Aldric had learned, by now, meant he was doing arithmetic rather than declining to engage — and when he finally spoke it wasn't a question at all.

"Whoever told you that," Solen said, "knew enough to warn you and not enough to tell you why. That's a very specific kind of person to have access to. I'd like to meet them eventually." He didn't ask who. Aldric noticed the omission, and understood, not for the first time, that Solen had simply decided the answer would arrive on its own schedule and saw no reason to spend effort chasing it early.
