# Chapter 12: The First Move

### Saga 1, Arc 3

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---

— Aldric Vane —

The module assignment posting had gone up at the tail end of the previous evening, and by morning the first years had organised themselves around it the way people organised around any new piece of information — according to what they already believed before they read it.

Combat Application was first bell, second day. Staging area outside the combat yard. Practical clothing. No equipment provided for the first session.

Aldric had read this, noted it, and gone to sleep.

The staging area turned out to be a flagstoned rectangle between the main building's east wing and the combat yard's outer wall — open to the sky, wide enough to hold the full first-year cohort with room to move, narrow enough that voices carried without effort. The yard gate was set into the far wall, iron, closed. Through the gap beneath it: flagstone, and beyond that the yard itself, which he could not yet see properly and did not try to. That introduction would come when it came.

He arrived four minutes before the bell, found a position toward the rear-right of the space with a clear sightline to the gate and to most of the cohort, and waited.

The cohort filled in around him. The same sorting as the assembly hall — noble students gravitating to the front and centre, commoner students distributing to the edges, the demi-human taking a position near the far wall with the self-contained quality Aldric had noted in the common space. The elf arrived exactly on time and stood where there was space. Nobody made room for her. She did not appear to require it.

Crest Halvon was already there when Aldric arrived.

This was, Aldric suspected, deliberate — not the arrival time itself but the fact of being settled and visible before others arrived, which was a way of establishing that you had chosen your ground rather than been assigned it. He stood in the front-left of the staging area, at ease, in conversation with the boy on his right — dark-haired, slightly shorter, who laughed at whatever Crest said with the practiced ease of someone who had made laughing at the right times a social habit. On Crest's other side, a girl with dark red hair pulled back, who was not laughing — watching instead, with the particular quality of attention that belonged to someone doing real-time calculation.

Crest had noticed Aldric the moment Aldric entered the staging area. Aldric had noticed Crest noticing him. Neither of them had acknowledged this. The two minutes since had been, in their way, a negotiation.

The bell rang.

The instructor had not yet appeared.

---

It was a simple move. Aldric had to give him that — it was cleanly constructed.

Crest did not cross the staging area. He did not lower his voice. He spoke at the volume of someone making a general observation to no one in particular, which meant everyone, and the content arrived in the staging area the way well-placed comments always arrived — as though they had simply appeared, without a sender.

"Interesting cohort this year." He was still looking at the gate, which was still closed. "First years always are. You can usually read the shape of things from the first day — who's going to make it to third year, who got in on family connections, who's going to spend the entire foundation year catching up to everyone else."

He paused. The boy to his right made a sound that was mostly agreement.

"The hair is new, though." And now Crest turned, briefly, in the specific direction of Aldric. Not pointed — a glance, as though he had simply been looking around the staging area and Aldric happened to be in the range of that look. "White hair at the academy usually means something, doesn't it? Old bloodline. Interesting lineage. Someone back a few generations who made an impression." He let this sit for a moment. "Can't quite place the family, though. Vane — that's not a name I recognise from the register. Which either means the family is very new to the register, or very old and very quiet, or—" Another pause, this one slightly longer. "—or the hair is a coincidence, and there isn't really a story behind it at all."

The laugh that came was from Crest's immediate orbit — the dark-haired boy, a couple of others who had been listening. Not large. Calibrated. The kind of laugh that was designed to be a social signal rather than a response to something genuinely funny — *this is the established reading, agree or decline.*

The staging area had the quality of a held breath. Not the whole staging area — the noble cluster was doing its social calculus, most of them landing on the safe read, a few staying quiet in the way of people who had not yet decided. The commoner students near the edges had gone carefully still.

Aldric considered the move.

It was well-constructed. The target was correct — the white hair was the most visible marker of his anomaly, and the bloodline question was the right lever because it ran in two directions: if he had notable bloodline, how did a commoner end up with it, and if he didn't, the hair was pretension without substance. The delivery was clean. The laugh was already in the room. The expected responses were anger, which would give Crest a posture to respond to; embarrassment, which would confirm the framing; or silence, which in the current atmosphere would read as acceptance.

He had been in smaller rooms with this kind of move. Ashford was a village and villages had their own versions of Crest Halvon, less polished and less careful, but the architecture was the same. You learned the architecture young or you didn't survive it.

"You're right," Aldric said. "There isn't a story."

His voice was level, unhurried. Not loud — the staging area was quiet enough that it did not need to be loud. He was looking at Crest the way you look at someone you are having a normal, unremarkable conversation with, which in this context was its own kind of statement.

"I grew up in Ashford. It's four days south. Population about two hundred, depending on the season. I spent most of my childhood doing errands and failing to replicate hand positions from a pamphlet." He paused for a fraction of a second. "The hair has been there the whole time. If it means something, it hasn't told me yet."

The laugh that came this time was different.

Not large — smaller than Crest's, in fact. But distributed differently. It came from the middle of the cohort rather than from one cluster, which meant it had not been organised in advance, which meant it was a genuine response to something that had landed. One sharp sound from somewhere to his left — instinctive, quickly suppressed. A quieter one from the right.

The silence that followed was also different.

Crest's move had been built on a premise — that the white hair required an explanation, and the absence of explanation was itself damaging. Aldric had accepted the premise entirely and extended it one step further: there is no story. I am a boy from a village with unusual hair and no interesting lineage. I am telling you this plainly and without embarrassment. What does that leave you with?

What it left Crest with, structurally, was nothing. He could pursue the angle, but pursuing it now would mean insisting that the absence of story was itself the point, which was a harder position to hold publicly because it was more obviously contempt rather than genuine curiosity, and contempt required a social cost to attach itself to.

Crest turned back toward the gate.

"Foundation year will sort it out, then," he said, at the same casual volume. "It usually does."

The dark-haired boy — Milo, Aldric thought he had heard someone say, Milo Draft — laughed again. But it was a fraction shorter than the previous laugh, and a fraction later, and the specific quality of it had changed in a way that was small enough to miss if you were not paying attention.

Aldric was paying attention.

He went back to watching the gate.

---

Bavel arrived at one minute past the bell.

He came through a side door in the east wing rather than through the yard gate, which Aldric noted because it meant the instructor had his own access and had chosen to let the cohort wait in the staging area for a reason rather than from delay. He was not a large man — medium height, spare, with the particular economy of movement that belonged to people who had spent a long time in environments where unnecessary motion had consequences. He crossed the staging area at a pace that communicated clearly that the session had started the moment he appeared and the cohort would catch up or it wouldn't.

He stopped at the yard gate and looked at the assembled first years with the expression of someone taking a very rapid inventory.

"Combat Application. Foundation year. My name is Bavel." He pulled the gate open. "In."

No preamble. No welcome. The gate was open and the yard was beyond it and the session had begun.

The cohort moved.

---

In the reorganisation as students filed through the gate — the brief, fluid moment when groupings dissolved and reformed around the new geometry of the yard entrance — Aldric's attention moved across the staging area one final time.

He found Seya standing three positions to Crest's left, moving with the group toward the gate. She was not looking at Crest. She was not looking at the yard. She was looking at Aldric, with an expression he could read precisely because it was the expression of someone who had just finished running a calculation and arrived at a result they found worth noting.

Not sympathy. Not hostility. Not the careful nothing of social neutrality. Something sharper and more specific than any of those — the expression of a person who understood exactly what had just happened and what the result of it meant, and was filing the result somewhere it would be useful.

The calculation was this, as best Aldric could read it: the move had not worked. Not entirely, and not in the way it was designed to. And she knew it, and she was not certain yet what she was going to do with that knowledge.

It lasted one second.

Then she turned toward the gate and was through it, and the yard received them all, and Bavel was already speaking.

Aldric filed it and followed.
