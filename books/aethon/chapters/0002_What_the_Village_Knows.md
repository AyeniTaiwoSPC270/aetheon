# Chapter 2: What the Village Knows

### Saga 1, Arc 1

---

— Aldric Vane —

The herbs came in bundles.

That was the thing nobody told you about healing work — not the smell, not the early hours, not the neighbours who arrived at the door with something wrong and left it on the threshold like an offering. The thing nobody told you was the sheer quantity of bundling. Maren Vane had been tying dried stems together since before Aldric was old enough to hold a knife, and she showed no signs of stopping.

"Hold it tighter," she said, without looking up.

"I am holding it tighter."

"You're holding it like it insulted you."

"It's scratching my hands."

"Everything scratches your hands. You've complained about rope, about bark, about the handle on the good bucket." She finally looked up. Something in her expression suggested she found this deeply funny and had decided not to show it. "Hold it tighter."

Aldric held it tighter.

The workroom was the smallest room in the house and somehow the fullest — bundles hanging from the ceiling beams, jars arranged along the lower shelf in an order that looked random and was not, a shallow drying rack under the south window where three varieties of something pale and threadlike had been laid out since yesterday. It smelled like the inside of a forest that had been pressed flat and stored. Aldric had grown up in this smell. He could no longer tell whether he found it pleasant or simply familiar, which he suspected was the same thing after long enough.

Maren worked the way she did everything — without wasted movement. Her hands knew the bundles the way his hands knew the grip of a practice sword. She didn't have to think about the knot.

"Wyla sent the root oil," Aldric said. "She says the account's settled through to the end of the month."

"She always says that."

"Is it true this time?"

"Probably." Maren set down a finished bundle and reached for the next. "She's a good woman. She just loses track."

"Of money she owes people."

"Of time. It's different." A pause. "How was Daran yesterday?"

"Fine. We practiced."

"Mmm."

There was an entire conversation in that sound and none of it was spoken. Aldric had grown up fluent in his mother's *mmm*. This one meant: *I know what you were doing, I'm not stopping you, I simply want it noted that I know.* He had been hearing variations of it his whole life and had never once figured out how to respond to it, so he tied the bundle tighter and said nothing.

The morning moved the way mornings did in Ashford — without hurry, and with the specific texture of a place that had been doing the same things for a long time. Through the workroom window he could see the lane: Tam Orvish crossing toward the well with his dog at his heels, the Birret girl chasing something small and fast through the gap between the houses, the light falling at the angle it always fell in late summer, long and yellow and a little hazy at the edges.

He had looked at this lane his whole life. He could not have said whether he loved it or was simply used to it.

---

The neighbour was a woman from the far end of the village — Aldric didn't know her name well enough to be sure of it, which meant she wasn't one of Maren's regulars. She had something wrong with her wrist. She held it at the angle people held things when they were trying not to acknowledge how much it hurt.

Maren took one look and brought her inside.

Aldric made himself small against the far shelf. This was the protocol — he had learned it early. Healing work was private work. You kept your eyes on something else and your ears to yourself and you did not ask questions after.

What he did notice, without meaning to: the way Maren's voice changed when she worked. Outside, in the lane, she was direct and a little dry and occasionally sharp in the way that made people feel they'd received something useful rather than something gentle. In here, with the wrist laid carefully across her palm and the examination beginning, her voice dropped a register and slowed. She asked about the pain without making the pain the point. She told the woman what she was doing before she did it.

The woman left with a wrapped wrist and a small jar of something from the second shelf and the expression of someone who had arrived carrying a weight and was trying to remember what it felt like without it.

Maren watched her go, then turned back to the workroom bench.

"You can stop staring at the jars," she said.

"I wasn't."

"Your ears were."

Aldric said nothing to this, which she accepted as a reasonable response. They went back to the bundling.

After a while she said, without preamble: "You're going to get in."

He looked up.

She was tying a knot and not looking at him. "The academy. You're going to get in. I want you to know I know that, so you don't spend the next year pretending I'm worried."

"I wasn't going to—"

"You were calculating how to tell me. You've been doing it since the harvest market." She finished the knot. Set the bundle down. "I'm not worried. I am going to miss you terribly, but that's not the same thing."

He didn't have anything to say to that either.

She picked up the next bundle. "Now hold this properly. You're strangling it."

---

Josse came back from the yard around midday, carrying sawdust and the particular silence of a man who had been working in it for several hours and was not yet ready to leave it behind entirely. He set his tools by the door — in the specific order he always set them, which Aldric had once tried to disrupt as a child and deeply regretted — and came to find where Aldric was sitting in the kitchen with a bowl of something Maren had left on the table.

He looked at the bowl. He looked at Aldric.

"What is it?" he said.

"Lentils."

"Good."

He sat down across the table, poured water from the jug, and drank most of it. Then he looked at Aldric again in the way he looked at things — steadily, without performance, like a man reading the grain in a piece of wood.

"You practice yesterday?" he said.

"Yeah."

"Any different?"

Aldric thought about the pressure sensation — the two flickers, unnamed, already filed under *strange and set aside.* "Not really," he said.

Josse nodded slowly. This was not disappointment. It was simply acknowledgment that the answer had been received.

"You'll know when it changes," he said.

"How?"

Josse considered this with the same steadiness he considered everything. "Because you won't be able to set it aside," he said. Then he reached over and took a piece of bread from beside the bowl — not asking, because he was Josse and this was his kitchen — and stood up. "I've got the Harrel job until sundown. Tell your mother I'll be back after."

"She knows."

"She always does." He picked up his tools on the way out, in the correct order, and was gone.

Aldric ate his lentils.

*You'll know when it changes. Because you won't be able to set it aside.*

He thought about that for a while. Then he finished eating and went to wait for Daran.

---

Daran arrived the way Daran arrived — without announcement, already mid-thought, as if the conversation had started somewhere around the corner and he'd simply brought it with him.

"—because the left hand position in the third diagram is wrong," he said, coming through the gate. "Or not wrong. Incomplete. Look." He had the pamphlet. He had clearly had it out since this morning. He held it up before Aldric had managed to stand fully.

"Hello to you too," Aldric said.

"The index finger. It says align to the second knuckle but if you look at the actual drawing the alignment is past the second knuckle. Someone drew it wrong or wrote it wrong. Either way it doesn't match."

Aldric looked at the diagram. He could not immediately tell what Daran was seeing. He had learned over many years that this did not mean Daran was wrong. "Okay," he said.

"So I've been doing the left hand position wrong this entire time."

"Or the drawing is wrong and you've been doing it right."

Daran stopped. Appeared to consider this. Appeared, faintly, to be annoyed that he had not considered it himself. "That's also possible," he said.

"Fifty-fifty."

"I don't like fifty-fifty."

"Nobody does."

They sat on the fence at the edge of the yard — the back fence, the one facing the field and not the lane, which was their default position for conversations that didn't need an audience. The afternoon was warm and the field beyond the fence was doing its late-summer thing of looking very slightly golden at the edges. Aldric had looked at this field his whole life too.

Daran put the pamphlet away with the care he always gave to things he was using. His hands were already rougher than they should have been for fifteen — he'd been doing this, the drilling, the repetition, long enough to mark him. Aldric had noticed this before without fully examining it.

"Wyla had a new batch of firestone candy," Aldric said.

"I know. I already bought some." He did not offer to share.

"Obviously."

"You had all morning to go."

"I was bundling herbs."

"Then that's a you problem."

Aldric looked at the sky. "You're a terrible person."

"I'm an efficient person." But Daran reached into his pocket and held out three pieces without looking at him, the way he always did when he was pretending not to be generous. Aldric took them.

They sat in the good silence of people who had been in each other's company long enough that silence wasn't a problem. A bird moved through the high grass at the field's edge. The Birret girl went past on the lane side, shouting something at someone out of view.

Ordinary afternoon. The kind that doesn't announce itself.

After a while Daran said: "My father spoke to the assessor."

Aldric looked at him.

"He comes through again in the spring. Before the academy applications." Daran was watching the field. His voice was level. "He said there's a preliminary review you can request. Just to know where you stand before the formal exam."

"Are you going to?"

"Probably." A beat. "You should too."

"Yeah," Aldric said.

It wasn't really a question, and it wasn't really an answer. It was just two boys acknowledging that the horizon was closer than it used to be, and neither of them wanted to be the one who looked away first.

Daran left when the shadows started getting long. No drama in it — just a *see you tomorrow* over his shoulder and the gate swinging shut, and then Aldric was alone in the yard with the late afternoon going amber around him.

---

The village had its evening sound.

Aldric sat on the back step for a while after dinner and listened to it — the Harrel yard going quiet across the way, someone somewhere doing something with a bucket, the low exchange of voices from the lane that meant the Orvish family were out in the cooling air after the day's heat. It was a sound he had listened to his whole life. He wondered, not for the first time, what it would sound like from somewhere else.

Not badly. He didn't wonder about it badly. Just — with curiosity. The way you wondered about a door you hadn't opened.

Later, in his room, he lay in the dark with the window open and the village settling into its nighttime quiet and thought about nothing in particular for a while.

Then the pressure came.

Not the flicker from practice — this was longer. A slow building sensation in his chest and his hands, spreading outward, like something trying to fill its own shape and not yet knowing what that shape was. It was not painful. It was not comfortable. It was the feeling of something present that had no name attached to it yet.

He lay still.

It moved through him and past him the way a sound passes through a room — there, fully there, and then diminishing, and then gone. He waited a moment after it ended to make sure it was finished.

It was.

He turned onto his side.

In the lane outside, something small moved through the dark — an animal, or just the wind in the hedge. The night was warm and very still.

*You'll know when it changes,* his father had said. *Because you won't be able to set it aside.*

He set it aside. He went to sleep.
