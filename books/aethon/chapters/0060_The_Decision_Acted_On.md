# Chapter 60: The Decision, Acted On

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Daran —

Hask said a full sentence.

It happened a little after the sun cleared the stable roof, in the ordinary middle of the ordinary morning, with the slops bucket in one hand and two fingers half-lifted for the usual greeting. Daran had already begun the second form. He had his back to the yard and his weight on the wrong foot, and for a moment he honestly believed he'd misheard.

"Cousin's cart goes south Thursday," Hask said.

The two fingers came down. The bucket swung. Hask, having discharged the whole of his correspondence for the year, continued across the yard toward the hogs at the same pace, without a glance back, and Daran stood with the blade still extended, completing a turn that had gone entirely out of his head.

Five words, about a cart. He had known this man the better part of a year, and had never once heard him string more than three together in the presence of a living creature.

*He's been counting too,* Daran thought. It was an uncomfortable idea. It suggested there had been an audience.

He reset. Did the turn again. It came out fine.

He did not say *thank you.* Hask would have hated it. He lifted two fingers instead, at the man's back, and the man, without turning, lifted his own in reply, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was, Daran reflected, the most generous thing anyone had done for him since a village he no longer named.

---

He had woken without the number.

That was the odd part. Every morning of the last year, in the grey before the light was worth anything, it had been there behind his eyes like a coin held under the tongue. It had told him how far he'd come, and how far he had left, and had never once asked his opinion. He had never said it aloud. He had promised himself, somewhere around the fortieth day, that he'd never say it aloud, and he never had, not to Hask, not to a horse, not to the ceiling.

This morning it hadn't been there.

He had lain on the cot, listening to the stable shift and breathe below him, and waited for it. It didn't come. Not a hollow, not an ache where it had been. It was simply gone, the way a sound is gone when you notice, only afterward, that the clock has stopped.

*Ah,* he'd thought.

That was all. *Ah.* He'd rolled out of bed and pulled on his boots and gone down to the yard, and it was only when Hask had said his sentence that he'd understood the arithmetic. The count had run out. Sometime in the night, while he slept, the last day of a year he'd built out of nothing but stubbornness had quietly turned over and become the first day of whatever came next.

It hadn't felt like anything. That was what he'd been braced for, all those mornings: some kind of bell. And there was no bell. There was a yard, and cold hands, and a cheap sword, and a Wednesday.

He was, on balance, fine with that.

Before going down he had taken stock, the way a man does before a journey, sitting on the edge of the cot with his boots half laced. A cheap sword. A hilt wrapped twice more than it needed. A coat with a repair he'd done himself, badly, and a second he'd had done better. The last of his coin under the tack-room board. The cot itself, which he'd been given for nothing and had never once been asked to explain. It was not a great deal. It was, by his own careful accounting, exactly enough.

---

The forms went well, and then they didn't, in the manner of forms.

He worked through the first six the way he always did, before the day had time to give him reasons not to. They ran without asking permission. His feet knew the ground. The wrap on the hilt, which he'd redone twice more than the job required, sat in his palm like a hand he'd been shaking for months. Somewhere in the fourth, a horse behind the stable wall blew out through its nose at something only it could smell, and it sounded so much like disgust that Daran nodded at it, one professional to another.

Then the seventh, and the seventh did what it had done every day for the better part of a year.

It was a low, awkward thing, a reverse cut out of a crouch, meant to open a man's guard from below while the wrist did most of the lying. The book had a diagram for it, with the weight marked in tidy percentages. He had followed them for two months and fallen over on schedule. He had burned the diagram, metaphorically, and gone on falling over off the page. He'd come, somewhere around the middle of spring, to think of it less as a form than as a person who disliked him.

*Good morning,* he thought, dropping into the crouch. *Shall we?*

The cut came out an inch shy of the line. The weight went wrong in the follow-through. His left knee took the blame, as it always did.

He reset. He did it again.

It was wrong, and a little different from yesterday's wrong. He noted it. That had been the whole method, in the end: get up, be wrong, get up, be wrong more interestingly. Nobody had ever shown up at the edge of the yard to tell him he was doing it right. He had stopped waiting for the man some time in the summer. He had decided that the grizzled figure who spoke nine words and changed a life was a story told by people who'd never had to do it alone, and that the real instructor was just the morning, over and over, which was patient in a way no human being had ever managed.

*I trained myself into a sword school,* he thought, rising out of the crouch. *The alternative was waiting for one to be handed to me.* It was still a good joke. He had nobody to tell it to, but it kept well, like a coin in a boot.

He did the seventh again. And again. And again.

---

The afternoon was long.

Hask came out twice more, once for the well, once for a horse with a stone in its shoe, and neither time said anything at all, which Daran found he was grateful for. The light moved across the yard, and the shadows of the stable roof crept slowly toward the well and away from it. His shoulders began to complain at a reasonable hour. His hands, which had thickened and broken and reset themselves so many times this year that he thought of them as a work in progress, did what they were told.

He did not think about the road east.

He noticed, somewhere in the fifth hour, that he hadn't. It was a strange thing to notice: an absence, sharp as a missing tooth. For months the road had been a presence, a place his eyes did not go, and the not-going had cost something every time. Now it just lay there, past the tree line, running toward a hall and a city and the boy he had once promised to follow into both. It wasn't guarded. It wasn't avoided. It was only a road, going somewhere he wasn't.

*A field at dusk,* he thought, testing it the way you test a bruise. *We'll both get in.*

It was a memory. It was a good one. It didn't hurt at all.

That was the thing that surprised him, in the end. Not that it was gone, but that it was *finished.* The way a good tool is finished: complete, useful, and no longer required for the job at hand. He set it down carefully, the way you set down something that has been carried a long way, and did not pick it up again.

He drew the sword. The seventh, once more, before the light went.

---

It came in the last hour, and it came so quietly that he almost missed it.

He dropped into the crouch. He set his weight, his hand, his eyes. The cut went out low and fast, on a line he could not have drawn if you'd paid him, and the wrist did its lie, and the follow-through carried the blade up through the imagined guard, and his weight came round behind it in a single, seamless motion that had no seam in it anywhere.

It landed.

He stayed exactly where he was, in the crouch, sword high, waiting for the feeling.

Nothing arrived.

He'd expected as much. He had learned, in the stable yard on a grey morning long ago, what the day a thing finally works looks like: the flat, unremarkable click of a bolt sliding home. There was no light. No music. Just the plain, complete sense of a cup set down, and a body that would now simply expect this of itself, forever, without ceremony. The floor had risen an inch. Tomorrow he would stand on it and not notice.

He rose slowly out of the crouch, and he waited for the old unease. The dread of the new floor. The knowledge that he would have to find a new failure to measure himself against, or the whole apparatus of getting up would stop making sense.

It didn't come, either.

*Oh,* Daran thought. *I've stopped needing one.*

He stood there a while, in the long gold light, with the sword in his hand and the horse behind the wall blowing gently through its nose, and turned it over. It was such a small thing. A year ago he would have said that finishing the seventh was the whole point. It wasn't. It had never been the point. The point was that a man could get up every morning and be wrong until it worked, and that he had done that, and would go on doing it, whether or not anyone was watching or a hall had a name on a list.

He sheathed the sword. It took a moment to find the mouth of the scabbard. His hands were tired, and quite steady.

---

The sun went down behind the stable, and the yard filled slowly with blue.

He stood at the well and drank, and washed his face, and looked at his hands in the last light: the thickened palm, the two knuckles that no longer closed, the scar along the thumb. They were not a boy's hands. He had a vague, distant sense that he ought to feel something about that, and found he didn't, particularly. They were good hands. They would do.

He was not thinking about the academy. He was not thinking about the promise, or the hall, or the field, or anyone in either place. He noticed that he was not, and did not take it for a victory. It was only a fact, like the weather.

He was thinking about tomorrow.

The cart went south on Thursday, Hask had said. Two days. He would need boots that would last, and a coat, and the last of the coin under the loose board in the tack room, and something to say at the door of a yard three towns away, in front of a man who would look at him once and decide. He'd worked on the something for months. It was still not good. He would work on it some more.

He looked south.

Past the tree line, past the last of the blue, the road bent out of sight toward a place he had never seen. He didn't know what the man there would ask him first. He didn't know what he'd say. He didn't know if he was good enough, or whether good enough was even the question, or what a person did the day after he'd finally, quietly, stopped counting.

He found, standing there in the dark, with a stable at his back and a road ahead of him, that he was curious to hear it.
