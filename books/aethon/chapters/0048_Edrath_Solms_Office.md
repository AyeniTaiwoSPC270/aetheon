# Chapter 48: Edrath Solm's Office

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Edrath Solm —


The blank incident report had been sitting in front of him for the better part of an hour, and in that hour Edrath had managed exactly one sentence: *At approximately the ninth bell, during the closing gathering following the first-year tournament, five individuals disguised as tournament staff attempted an assault on my person.*

It was accurate. It was also, he suspected, the least useful sentence he had ever written in twenty-two years of filing reports that mattered.

He crossed it out — not because it was wrong, but because the moment he tried to write the second sentence, the one that would need to say *why*, his pen simply stopped, the way a man's feet stop at the edge of a drop he hadn't noticed was coming. He sat with the crossed-out line for a while, listening to the castle-quiet of his own office at an hour when even the corridor lamps had been trimmed low, and reflected, with the small, tired humor he permitted himself only when entirely alone, that a man who could face down five armed attackers without his pulse changing was apparently no match at all for a single sheet of official stationery.

A junior steward appeared briefly with a tray of tea he hadn't ordered, set it down with the specific over-formality of someone who had clearly been told to check on the Principal without being told why, and retreated before Edrath could either thank her or send her away. He looked at the tea for a moment — steam still rising, entirely untouched — and reflected that whoever had decided a pot of tea was the correct institutional response to an assassination attempt had never actually had to write the report that followed one.

A knock arrived before he'd found a second attempt worth crossing out.

"Come."

Bavel let himself in without waiting for the invitation to finish, which was, Edrath reflected, roughly the correct amount of formality for a night like this one. He looked considerably worse than he had an hour ago — the adrenaline gone, the exhaustion arrived to collect what it was owed.

"Both of them are secured," Bavel said, dropping into the chair across the desk without being offered it. "Quietly, like you asked. They're not talking, and I don't expect that to change quickly."

"And the three who ran?"

"Gone. City watch found nothing past the treeline but footprints that stopped existing the moment they reached the road." Bavel's mouth twisted, not quite a smile. "Professional. Whoever trained them wasn't training amateurs, even if the amateurs themselves didn't know much."

"I expected as much." Edrath set his pen down, precisely parallel to the blank sheet, a small ritual of order in a night that had offered him none. "Thank you, Instructor. Go and rest. You've more than earned it."

Bavel didn't move. "You're writing the report."

"I'm attempting to."

"You've written one sentence in the time it took me to secure two prisoners and walk here." It wasn't an accusation. It was, from Bavel, close to concern, delivered in the only register he apparently owned. "That's not like you."

"No," Edrath admitted. "It isn't."

He didn't offer anything further, and Bavel — to his considerable credit, and after a pause that made clear he'd noticed the door being closed rather than opened — didn't press for it. He simply stood, gave the smallest and least ironic nod Edrath had seen from him all year, and left the office exactly as unceremoniously as he'd entered it, closing the door with the particular care of a man who understood, without being told, that whatever was happening on the other side of that desk wasn't his to witness.

Alone again, Edrath allowed himself, for the first time all night, the luxury of simply sitting still.

The trouble with the report — the actual trouble, the one he hadn't said aloud to Bavel and had no intention of saying aloud to anyone — was that an honest account of tonight required an honest account of *why*, and the honest account of why led somewhere he had spent twenty-two years declining to write down. Five attackers had come for him specifically, not for the school, not for the Crown's guests, not for any of the dozen political currents that ran through a night like this one. For him. Which meant someone, somewhere, had decided that removing Edrath Solm from his post was worth the very considerable risk of doing it in front of the King.

He had a name for that someone. He did not have proof, and a name without proof, filed in a formal report to the Crown, was worth exactly nothing except the considerable danger of making an enemy aware that he was being watched before Edrath had anything real to watch him with.

So the report would say what reports said. An incident. Contained. Under investigation. The specific bloodless language that let institutions continue functioning without ever quite admitting how close they'd come to not functioning at all.

Tomorrow would bring its own considerable weather regardless of what he wrote tonight — a King's private, pointed questions before he'd even finished his morning tea, a Council session that would spend two hours performing outrage and perhaps ten minutes discussing anything useful, and at least one dignitary, Edrath suspected, expressing concern for his welfare in a tone carefully calibrated to sound like concern rather than the thing it actually was. He had sat through enough of those sessions to know exactly which of the thirty faces in that chamber would ask the loudest questions and mean the least by them. He had never yet sat through one wondering whether the true author of the evening's trouble was sitting quietly among the people asking after his health, and he found he did not care for the novelty.

What it would not say — what nothing he ever filed would say, not this year and very possibly not for several more — was the other thing sitting underneath tonight, the thing that had kept his pen from moving for an hour that had nothing to do with the report at all.

*A first-year student placed himself between me and a trained attacker, with no idea why anyone would want to hurt me, and no idea what either of us actually is.*

He drew a second sheet toward himself, blank, and found — with the specific, quiet alarm of a man watching his own hand move somewhere he hadn't consciously sent it — that his pen had already begun forming a greeting that had nothing to do with the Crown, the Council, or any office that required his signature in an official capacity at all.

*It has been some years since I last wrote to you, and I had hoped, when I did, that it would be to tell you the boy was simply thriving — which, in fairness, he is. He is also, as of tonight, considerably more visible than either of us would have preferred him to be at this stage.*

He stopped. Read it back. Felt the particular vertigo of a man who has just watched a door open that he had spent a very long time keeping shut, not because he'd forgotten it was there, but because opening it had never once, in all those years, seemed like the responsible choice.

*He does not know what he stopped tonight, or why it mattered that he was the one standing there when it happened. I am not certain he is ready to know. I am considerably less certain, after this evening, that the choice of when he learns it is still entirely mine to make.*

It was, he thought, setting the pen down at last, the most honest sentence he had written all night, and also the single sentence he was least prepared to actually send anywhere. A warning delivered too early could frighten a boy who had already survived more in two years than most people survived in a lifetime, and could do it for no better reason than an old man's guilty conscience finally catching up with him on a bad night. A warning delivered too late — and here was the thought he kept circling back to, the one that had truly stopped his pen the first time — might arrive after there was nothing left for it to protect.

He thought of Aldric's face in the courtyard, the specific, unpracticed honesty of *I wasn't planning to be. It happened faster than planning* — a boy who had not stopped to calculate the odds because some older, better part of him had simply refused to let a gap stay a gap. It was, Edrath reflected, exactly the kind of instinct that got good people killed eventually, and exactly the kind he had no right whatsoever to discourage, because it was also, very plainly, the same instinct that had once made someone else choose to protect this particular child at considerable cost to themselves, long before Edrath himself had ever been asked to carry any part of that promise forward.

*Something out there already knows more about him than we've told him.* He had said as much to Bavel, in the wreckage of the courtyard, and had meant it as an observation. Sitting here now, in the quiet, it had curdled into something closer to an indictment — not of whoever had sent tonight's attackers, but of every year Edrath himself had spent deciding that *not yet* was the same thing as *taking care of it.*

He read the unfinished letter through once more, in full, weighing every line against the two futures it might create, and found that he could not, tonight, tell with any confidence which of them was the kinder one.

So he did not send it.

He folded it instead — not into the fire, which would have been the easier and in some ways the more honest gesture, but into the same locked drawer where he kept the handful of other things too dangerous to leave in an ordinary desk and too important to destroy — and told himself, with the specific, practiced calm that had carried him through every crisis this office had ever handed him, that there would be a better night for finishing it. A quieter one. A night when the decision wasn't still vibrating with the adrenaline of having very nearly lost the option to make it at all.

He did not entirely believe himself. He rarely did, on nights like this one, and had long since stopped expecting that to change.

The incident report, when he finally returned to it, took him considerably less time to finish than the letter had — an hour's paralysis dissolving, once he'd stopped pretending the two documents belonged to the same part of his mind, into the flat, procedural language he could have written in his sleep. *Contained. Under investigation. No further threat to students or staff at this time.* All of it true. None of it the truth that mattered.

He signed it, sealed it, and set it in the outgoing tray for the morning courier, and sat for a while longer in the low lamplight with the locked drawer at his elbow and the unfinished letter inside it, thinking not about tomorrow's official inquiries, or the King's undoubtedly pointed questions, or even the two prisoners currently keeping his night watch considerably more occupied than they'd have liked.

He thought about a boy who filed everything he couldn't yet look at properly and went to sleep anyway, and wondered, with a doubt that had no comfortable answer waiting on either side of it, how much longer that particular kindness was actually his to keep giving.

The tea had gone cold untouched. He drank it anyway, out of the same stubborn, practical habit that had gotten him through every other long night this office had ever handed him, and found it exactly as unremarkable as he'd expected — which was, in its own small way, the first honestly comforting thing that had happened to him since the first table went over. Not every problem tonight had to be the large kind. Some of them were simply cold tea, and a man could still choose, even on a night like this one, which kind of problem to let himself mind.

He banked the fire low, locked the drawer a second time out of habit rather than necessity, and did not go to bed for a long while after that — not because there was anything left to do, but because the quiet, once he finally let himself sit inside it without a report or a letter demanding to be written, was the first honest quiet he'd had all day, and some part of him understood that it wouldn't last.
