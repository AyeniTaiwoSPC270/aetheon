# Chapter 13: Ten Words

### Saga 1, Arc 3

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---

— Aldric Vane —

The Combat Application session ended at the second morning bell and the cohort filtered back into the main building with the specific energy of people who had been doing something physical for an hour and had not yet transitioned back into the register of people who sat in rooms. The common space received them at near capacity — louder than it had been at orientation, the furniture already carrying the early territorial markings of a cohort that had been here long enough to have preferred positions.

Aldric had a position near the notice board.

This was not sentiment — the notice board was at the edge of the room with a wall behind it, which gave him a sightline to the full space without requiring him to orient toward the centre. He stood with his back three inches from the stone, letting the room assemble around him, and catalogued it in the lower register of the filing instinct: the version that ran passively in the background when there was no new geography to acquire and people were the primary available information.

Crest's cluster had reformed at the front-centre, same coordinates as every other time. Milo to the right, slightly further back than yesterday — a distance of perhaps six inches, which was not six inches, it was the morning's accumulated data about what standing next to Crest cost. Seya to the left, one step back, watching the room rather than contributing to the conversation. This was her consistent position. He had noted it.

Rynn was standing in the middle of the common space.

Not near a wall, not at a table — just standing, in the specific way of someone who had never learned to orbit the edges of rooms because it had never occurred to them that edges were the safe option. He was running warm from the session in the visible way of people whose output ran hot — a slight flush, a restlessness in the way he held himself that was not aggression but its precondition. Aldric had clocked him twice now: the assembly hall, the staging area. Both times the same quality — more present than the room required, attention distributed outward rather than inward, the specific alertness of someone who was always slightly ready for the next thing.

He had not assigned significance to this. He assigned it now.

---

The second move came seven minutes into the break.

Crest was not looking at Aldric when he started talking. He was looking at Milo, which was the same technique as yesterday — the oblique address, the comment structured as general observation rather than directed statement. But the calibration was different. Yesterday's move had the quality of a precisely placed stone — the contempt was the subtext and the surface was curious, almost reasonable. Today the surface was thinner.

"The session was instructive," Crest said, at carrying volume. "You can see where the gaps are early. Some students have real foundations — the work done before arriving here. You can feel it in how they move." He paused, the deliberate pause of someone constructing a sentence they have pre-built. "And then there are students who have been working from something self-taught. No real grounding. Whatever they managed to put together from pamphlets and guesswork in whatever village they came from." Another pause, slightly longer. "The academy will correct for that, I suppose. If there's enough to work with. Which isn't always the case."

The contempt was not subtext this time. It was text, dressed in one thin layer of plausible deniability that was insufficient for its function — visible to everyone in the room, including Crest, including Crest's own assessment of the move, which was that the first move not landing had produced something in him that was slightly less controlled than yesterday.

The response from his orbit was the same as yesterday — Milo's laugh, a couple of others. But it was a beat slower, and the people who had done the social calculus yesterday and landed on *agree* were doing it again with slightly less certainty, because the room remembered what happened yesterday and the calculation now included that variable.

Aldric was already assembling his response.

He did not get to deliver it.

---

There was a sound — not a voice, just the sound of someone moving fast — and Rynn crossed the common space in three steps and swung.

It was not a clean punch. It was the swing of someone who had moved before the decision to move had fully completed — more momentum than technique, the kind of hit that came from having a very short gap between *this is wrong* and *I will stop it.* It caught Crest across the jaw-cheekbone line with the flat of the knuckles rather than the full force of a proper hit, which was why Crest went back one step instead of down. The sound it made was the specific sharp crack of a hit landing in a room that had gone completely silent at the speed of a held breath.

Crest's head snapped to the side.

He did not go down.

For one second — a full second, which was a long time for a room to stay silent — nothing happened. The common space held the specific quality of a space that has just witnessed something that has changed the social temperature permanently and is recalibrating around the new fact of it. Then three things happened at once: voices, the sound of chairs moving, and Milo taking two rapid steps backward in the direction of away.

Rynn was still standing where he had stopped, forward in his stance, both hands half-raised in the posture of someone who had thrown one punch and had not yet decided if the situation required more. His face said: it probably does not require more but he was leaving the option available.

Crest straightened. His lip was split — the bottom edge, where the knuckles had caught it on the way past. He brought one hand up and touched it, looked at his fingers, looked at Rynn with an expression that was doing a great deal of work simultaneously: the genuine shock of someone who had not assessed this particular variable, the social machinery of recovery operating in parallel, and underneath both of those, in a register he was successfully suppressing, something that was closer to anger than composure.

He did not go for Rynn.

"You've just—" he started.

"I know," Rynn said. His voice was too loud for the room, which was its normal volume. "I know what I've done."

Crest's recovery was good. Aldric watched it happen in real time — the straightening, the deliberate lowering of the hand, the shift into the register of someone who was not rattled, who was simply dealing with an unfortunate and predictable outcome of associating with people below a certain standard. He opened his mouth.

Aldric stepped into the space between them.

Not heroically — not with the posture or performance of someone placing themselves at physical risk, because the risk was low and performing it would have been wrong. He just stepped into the gap, which was approximately four feet, and said:

"The administrative office is going to want a very specific account of where everyone was standing."

He said it in the tone of someone making a practical observation about weather. Not to Crest, not to Rynn — to the general space, which contained both of them and about twenty other students who were now acutely aware that whatever they said next was going to form part of someone's official record.

The room did not go quiet — it had already been quiet. What happened was that the specific quality of the quiet changed. The escalation in Crest's posture lost one degree of temperature. Not because of anything Aldric had done that was impressive — because the observation was accurate, and accuracy in that register at that moment was the most disorienting possible response to the situation, and confusion was a gap, and gaps were what authority walked through.

Seya, from her position one step back, produced a sound that was not quite a laugh — something shorter and more involuntary, quickly suppressed. Her expression, when Aldric glanced at it, was the expression of someone who had not expected that specific move and was filing the new data.

Crest looked at Aldric for a moment.

"You're going to regret being associated with this," he said, at the volume of someone delivering a clear and final statement. Then he turned away, straightening his collar, performing the complete recovery with sufficient quality that a person watching from a distance might have believed it.

The door to the common space opened.

---

The staff member who entered was not Bavel — a junior administrator by the look of him, mid-twenties, with the expression of someone who had been told that the first-year common space required attention and had arrived to find the specific situation that phrase had been covering for. He assessed the room in two seconds: split lip on one student, stance on another, twenty witnesses doing their best to appear uninvolved.

"You." He pointed at Rynn without apparent surprise. "Administrative office. Now."

He looked at the room. "Everyone else. Next module."

The common space emptied with the speed of people who were profoundly grateful to have somewhere to be.

---

— Rynn —

In the thirty seconds between *you, come with me* and *everyone else, module,* Aldric was standing two feet to his left.

Rynn was still coming down from the hit — not the physical part, which was already fading, but the specific heat of the decision that had produced it, which took longer to metabolise. He was aware of several things simultaneously: the administrative office was going to be a conversation he was not going to enjoy, his hand hurt in the specific way of hands that had hit something harder than expected, and the boy with the white hair had just said the strangest thing he had ever heard anyone say in the middle of a brawl.

He looked at him.

"That was a weird thing to say," he said. It came out at his normal volume, which was slightly too loud. He did not adjust it.

The boy looked back at him with the expression of someone who was deciding something. "It worked," he said.

Rynn considered this for a moment. It had, actually, worked. He was not certain that made it less weird. "You're the one from Ashford," he said. "With the hair."

"Still yes to both."

The staff member was waiting with the particular patience of someone who was going to stop being patient in approximately four seconds.

Rynn went.

Down the corridor, following two steps behind the administrator's efficient pace, he turned the last thirty seconds over in the part of his mind that was not occupied with the impending administrative unpleasantness. The white-haired boy from Ashford had stepped into the middle of a situation that was not his, said something that had no right to work, and then confirmed it had worked with the flat certainty of someone reporting a fact.

He had not planned to have a friend today.

He was not entirely certain that was still true.
