# Chapter 54: What He Carries Back

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"You went to the post house," Rynn said, "and came back looking like the sky had insulted you personally. I need details."

"I posted a letter."

"That's not details. That's a receipt." Rynn dropped onto the dormitory step beside him with the total confidence of a man who had never once been refused a seat. "Did the clerk say something? Was there a line? Did you cry over the letter? You can tell me. Solen's not here, so it's only me who'll judge you."

"I'm here," Solen said, from two steps above, without looking up from whatever he was counting. "I'm judging in advance. It saves time."

Aldric looked at the two of them and felt the story sit in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed the wrong way.

There was a version he could tell. A man in irons, a hillside, some soldiers, a very upright person in white armor. He could feel exactly how it would go, the way you can feel a joke landing before the punchline is out. Rynn would say *and then what*. Solen would say *and then who*. And Aldric would say *and then it was over*, and they would both look at him with the same expression, waiting for the rest of it, because a story was supposed to have a rest of it.

He didn't have a rest of it. He had a face.

"It was a long letter," he said. "I was very moving."

"Liar," Rynn said, delighted.

"It's efficient," Aldric said, and stood up before either of them found the seam.

---

Dinner was loud, which helped, and then dinner was over, which didn't.

He had a plan for the evening. It was a short plan: walk somewhere quiet, sit down, think about a man's face until it either meant something or stopped. Aldric had made worse plans. He'd made several in the last month that involved running at things.

The trouble was that the academy had no interest in his plan.

A second-year with a stack of ledgers walked straight through his path and apologized to a pillar. Two first-years argued about whether the tournament bracket had been rigged, in the loud, careful voices of people who hoped to be overheard. A notice on the east board announced, in Bavel's handwriting, that the yard would be closed for resurfacing on the following morning and that anyone found in it would be resurfaced with it. Somebody's kettle was screaming in a stairwell. Life at Greyveil went on, thorough and cheerful and indifferent, exactly as if a man hadn't been walked out of a valley at dawn in front of a single, silent witness on a hill.

He found, halfway across the lower court, that he'd stopped being angry about it. It wasn't the academy's fault. Nobody here knew. That was the whole problem, tidily stated, and it didn't feel any better for being tidy.

*Quiet,* he thought. *Somewhere with a wall.*

His feet, which had opinions of their own, took him to the library.

He made it as far as the foot of the gallery stairs before Solen caught up with him. Solen never ran. He simply arrived, the way weather did.

"You ate four bites," Solen said.

"I wasn't hungry."

"You're always hungry. It's a structural feature of you." He fell into step, hands behind his back, eyes on the floor a few paces ahead as though it might try something. "Rynn thinks you're being mysterious for fun. I've told him it isn't fun. He's decided that means it's worse mysterious, which is an improvement on his previous position."

"Solen."

"I'm not asking." He said it flatly, like a man reading a notice. "I want that noted. I've asked you a great many things this term and been told 'fine' by a person who couldn't have been less fine. I've retired the question. I'm just pointing out that I noticed, and that it's on the record, and that if anything you saw today has a shape you can say aloud, I'll be somewhere with a door that closes."

It was, Aldric thought, the kindest thing anyone had said to him all week, delivered in the tone of a man reporting a broken hinge.

"There isn't a shape yet," he said. "There's just a face."

"Then keep the face," Solen said, "until it grows a shape. I'll count in the meantime." He stopped at the stairs. "The arithmetic one. You're doing it again."

"I have several faces."

"You have one. It's just very good at looking like several." And he was gone before Aldric could produce a rebuttal, which was, Aldric considered, exactly the sort of exit a person built for himself when he wanted to leave a kindness where it had landed.


---

The corner was where it always was: the far end of the second gallery, behind the shelves nobody read because nobody was assigned them, where the ceiling dropped and the lamp had been put by someone who understood that people who came here wanted to be found only on purpose. Two chairs. A table with a ring burned into it from some long-dead student's mistake. A window the color of slate.

He had already stepped past the last shelf before he registered that the lamp was lit.

Lirien sat in the far chair, a book open in front of her, one hand resting flat on the page as if she were holding it down against a draft. She did not look up.

Aldric stopped.

Every instinct in him produced the same sensible instruction, which was to turn around. He had done it before, in doorways and on staircases, the small, dignified retreat of a person who has walked into someone else's silence. He was good at it. He had a whole technique.

He stayed where he was, and something odd happened in the back of his mind. It was the sensation of a drawer sliding open on its own.

*Not the first time.*

He tried, for a moment, to be reasonable about it. There were, after all, only so many quiet corners in a library, and Lirien was a person who valued quiet with the enthusiasm other people reserved for money. A pattern of two was a coincidence. A pattern of three was a habit. A pattern of more than that was something he did not currently have the vocabulary to call by name, and he had a very good vocabulary.

He tried to remember the last time he'd come to this corner and found it empty. He went back through the term, through the evenings he'd carried a problem up the stairs like a sack of flour, and the lamp was always already lit. He'd assumed the lamp was a feature of the room. He had, he realized, spent the better part of a year mapping every exit, every angle, every second-year who might be watching from a window, and had never once noticed that the one person in the school he couldn't read kept turning up in the place he went to be alone.

It was, he thought, an extraordinary failure of tradecraft. It would have gotten him laughed out of a village he no longer entirely had the right to miss.

Lirien turned a page.

He could ask. *Do you come here often* was, at least, the right kind of stupid. *Did you know I'd be here* was worse, because it had an answer, and he wasn't sure he wanted it.

"I can go," he said instead.

She didn't answer. She didn't look at him. Her left foot, under the table, moved out a few inches, and the far chair, the empty one, the one that had been tucked neatly in, slid back from the table with a small, dry sound against the stone.

Then the foot went back where it had been, and Lirien went on reading as though nothing at all had happened, which was the kind of thing she did so well that it was practically a signature.

Aldric looked at the chair.

He was aware that this was not a large gesture. In another person, in another room, it would have barely counted. It was a chair, moved four inches, by a girl who had never once, in a year of watching, said a thing she didn't mean.

He sat.

The library breathed around them: a shelf settling, a far-off cough, the small industrious noise of a lamp doing its best. He put his hands on the table and looked at the burn ring for a while, and for the first time all evening he did not think about the face. He waited for it to arrive, the way it had in the post house and on the road and all through dinner, and it didn't come.

That was strange. He tested it, carefully, the way you test a tooth. The face was still there. He could reach it if he wanted to: a tired, ordinary, relieved man, looking at a lantern in the way people look at a thing that has finally stopped being a threat. It hadn't gone anywhere.

It was just that, for the moment, he wasn't holding it alone.

"It's a very large amount of nothing you're not saying," Lirien said.

She still hadn't looked up. Her voice was as level as the frost she cast, and every bit as exact.

"I wasn't aware I was saying it loudly."

"You weren't. That's what makes it loud."

He should have had something dry ready. He had a great many dry things, kept for exactly such emergencies. What he had, at present, was a stone in his throat and a very small, very unhelpful urge to laugh.

"I saw something today," he said. It came out more honestly than he'd planned. "I don't know what it was. I don't think it was about me. I keep thinking it was about me."

Lirien turned another page.

"Do you want an assessment," she said, "or do you want me to keep reading?"

He thought about it. It was, all things considered, the most generous question anyone had asked him in a month.

"Keep reading," Aldric said.

"Good."

She did. He reached behind him for the nearest book, purely so his hands would have an alibi, and opened it somewhere near the back with the air of a man who had been meaning to read this for weeks.

It was a survey of municipal drainage in the southern provinces.

He read the first paragraph with great seriousness. He read the second with slightly less. Somewhere in the third, a sentence about the gradient of culverts made him think, without any warning whatsoever, of a very tired man in the back of a cart, and he turned the page a little too quickly.

"It's a good book," Lirien said, not looking up. "Sluices are underrated."

"I was just about to say that."

"You were on page two hundred and eleven. It's the appendix."

He closed the book and set it very gently on the table, as one might set down something that had betrayed him. It was the closest thing to a laugh he'd managed all day, and it came out through his nose, and Lirien, without a flicker, turned another page as though she had not heard it, which was how he knew she had.

Neither of them said anything for what might have been an hour. Somewhere in that hour the lamp guttered and steadied, and he understood that he would remember this evening, and that he would not be able to explain to anyone, Rynn least of all, exactly why.

When he finally stood to go, she didn't look up, and she didn't say anything. The far chair stayed exactly where she had moved it. She didn't push it back.

He noticed that on the stairs, and noticed himself noticing. He had walked into that corner carrying a thing he meant to turn over alone, in the dark, the way he turned over everything. He was walking out of it with the same thing, exactly as heavy, and the strangest sensation that somebody had been holding the other end.

Nobody had asked him a single question. It was the most thorough interrogation he had ever survived, and he had absolutely no idea what he'd confessed.
