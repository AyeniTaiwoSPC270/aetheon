# Chapter 8: The City That Didn't Notice

### Saga 1, Arc 3

### Saga 1, Arc 3

---

— Aldric Vane —

The wall had been visible for the better part of an hour before they reached it.

That was the first thing. Not the city — the wall. A pale stone line across the horizon that Aldric had taken, initially, for a ridge. Then it had kept not getting smaller, and he had understood. You could see Ashford from its own south road. You could see all of it — the grain store, the guild hall roof, the smoke from Presswick's chimney distinguishable from Wyla's if you knew which was which. Valdris Prime was visible from an hour away and showed you nothing except that it continued.

He filed that and walked.

The south gate was busy in the specific way of late afternoon — the working day not quite finished, the evening not quite started, everyone in the gap between moving with a kind of compressed efficiency. A post rider cut through the queue ahead of them and the queue reorganised itself around the gap he left without comment, without disruption, the way water moves around a dropped stone. Carts. Travellers in pairs and small groups. A woman with a covered basket who argued briefly with one of the gate guards and then didn't. The guards themselves — two of them, checking documents with the mechanical thoroughness of people who had checked documents a thousand times before and expected the thousand and first to be identical.

Daran had the travel correspondence. Of course he did. He had packed it in the outer pocket of his bag four days ago and had not moved it since.

The guard read it. Read it again. Looked at Aldric — the hair, the eyes, a second's additional processing — and then looked at the document again as if confirming they matched. They did.

"Purpose of visit?"

"Academy examination," Daran said.

The guard handed the correspondence back and stepped aside.

That was it. The city didn't care about them at all, and Aldric found this, walking through the gate into the noise and the press and the smell of four hundred thousand people living their lives, a genuine relief.

---

The Common Ring was packed earth underfoot, same as Ashford. He noticed this first, which probably said something. The same material — the same basic composition of compacted soil and years of foot traffic — but different in kind, the way a word changes meaning when you say it too many times in a row. This earth had been walked into itself by an order of magnitude more feet than Ashford's south road had seen in its entire existence. It was harder. It had given up.

Above it: everything else.

Market stalls in the process of closing — vendors covering their goods with practised efficiency, not bothering to call out to the end-of-day foot traffic because the end-of-day foot traffic had somewhere to be and wasn't stopping. The street opened into a wider thoroughfare that branched twice before he lost track of it, each branch busy, each branch leading somewhere that presumably mattered to the people going there. Buildings packed close enough that the upper storeys nearly touched overhead, the evening light arriving in the gaps between them at an angle that made everything look slightly more deliberate than it was. Somewhere to his left, something that might have been a forge. Somewhere ahead, a food stall that had not yet closed and was doing significant business.

A woman passing in the other direction glanced at his hair. A single second. Then she was gone, replaced by the back of a man in a grey coat who was replaced by two boys about their age who were replaced by a cart pulling slowly toward a side street.

"You looked at a map," Aldric said.

"Before we left."

"Of course you did."

"The assessment hall is in the Mage Quarter adjacent." Daran was navigating by something internal — he had the look of a person cross-referencing the street against a memorised diagram. "There are inns in the Common Ring near the district border. Cheaper than the Quarter, close enough to walk."

"Walk," Aldric said. "My feet are thrilled."

"Your feet have had four days to adjust."

"My feet have had four days to develop opinions. That's different."

Daran didn't respond to this, which was not quite the same as not hearing it. He turned left at the first branch, and Aldric followed, and the wider thoroughfare gave way to a street that was narrower and quieter and smelled considerably less of the forge and considerably more of something cooking that he couldn't identify but didn't object to.

---

The inn was called something with a bird in it — a painted wooden sign above the door, the paint worn enough that the bird in question had lost most of its specificity and become simply a bird, brown-ish, mid-air. The kind of inn that existed to provide a room and a meal and a pillow and did not have opinions about anything beyond that.

The innkeeper was a broad man with the manner of someone who had answered the same five questions for twenty years and would answer them for twenty more without finding them any more interesting. He looked at them. He named a price. Daran didn't negotiate — the price was fair — and counted coin onto the counter with the same efficiency he brought to everything. The innkeeper took the coin, turned, produced a key from a board of identical keys, set it down.

"Stairs, second floor, end of the hall."

They took the stairs.

The room was small. Two beds — narrow, practical — a window that looked onto the street below, and a table that held a candle and nothing else. Aldric set his pack down on the nearest bed and looked at the window. The city was audible through it in a continuous low register: voices, cart wheels on packed earth, something metallic at irregular intervals that he could not source. He had never slept with that much noise outside a window. He noted this the way he noted the room — dimensions, contents, exits — and filed it.

"Good map," he said.

Daran sat on his bed and began unlacing his boots with the methodical attention he brought to tasks that needed doing. "It was a reasonable inference from the district layout."

"Still."

Daran didn't say anything, but the set of his mouth changed fractionally. It was the closest he came to acknowledging a compliment.

---

They ate downstairs — inn food, functional, hot — at a table near the back wall where the noise from the front room was present but not intrusive. Daran ate quickly and without much attention, the way he always did when something else was occupying the part of his mind that would otherwise be used for meals. He had the examination correspondence out on the table. Not open — he had read it enough times to have it indexed. Just out.

"Written theory first," he said. "Circuit fundamentals, elemental classification, historical applications. They've published the general framework but not the specific questions. I've covered the likely distributions."

"How likely?"

"Likely enough." He paused. "The practical assessment is weighted higher than written. Three to two, roughly."

"Roughly."

"Based on the historical pass rates and what I could find in the academy's published intake criteria. The weighting isn't stated explicitly. But the pass rate for written-only strong candidates is lower than for practical-strong candidates, which implies the practical carries more."

Aldric ate. "You got all that from the intake criteria."

"I read carefully."

"You read like you're looking for weaknesses."

Daran looked up. Something in his expression acknowledged the accuracy of this without finding it worth commenting on. "The combat application is third. That's the one with the most variance — it depends on who's proctoring and what they decide to test. Last year they used paired sparring. The year before, solo assessment against a training construct."

"What did you decide to prepare for?"

"Both."

Of course. Aldric reached across the table for the bread — he'd been at it already and had worked through most of what remained — and tore a piece. Downstairs, someone near the front said something that made three other people laugh. The sound landed briefly and then the ordinary noise of the inn absorbed it.

"And after?" Aldric said.

Daran looked at him.

"After the exam. When we're in." He kept his voice even. When, not if. "What's the first thing?"

Daran considered this with genuine seriousness, the way he considered everything. "The dormitory allocation. Find where we are. Get oriented before the first day." He paused. "You'll want to find the training rooms before anyone tells you where they are. You're better when you've already walked the space."

Aldric looked at him.

"I'm noting patterns," Daran said. "Not a compliment."

"Sure." He ate the bread. "First thing I'm doing is finding somewhere quiet. The dormitory's going to be—" He thought about four hundred thousand people and the noise through the inn window. "There are a lot of people in this city."

"There are a lot of people at the academy."

"You know what I mean."

Daran did, probably. He didn't say so. He folded the examination correspondence — once, along its existing crease — and put it back in his pack's outer pocket, a motion so habitual it barely qualified as deliberate. He finished what was left of his meal. Pushed back from the table.

"Exam is morning registration," he said. "We should be there early."

"We'll be there early."

"I'll wake you."

"I'll be awake."

Daran looked at him with the expression of someone who had been woken up by Aldric's failure to be awake at least once and had indexed the data. "I'll wake you."

---

The room was dark except for the street light through the window, which was not much light but was more than none. Daran had been quiet for long enough that his breathing had changed — slower, more deliberate, either asleep or so close to it that the distinction didn't matter. He slept the way he did everything else: efficiently, without waste.

The city kept going.

Aldric lay on his back and listened to it — the low continuous register of four hundred thousand people who didn't know he was here and didn't need to. Somewhere below, a door opened and closed. Somewhere further, voices at a volume that suggested the gap between conversation and argument without committing to either. The irregular metallic sound was still happening. He still couldn't source it.

He was not thinking about the exam.

He was not thinking about the written theory weighting or the practical assessment or whether the combat stage would use paired sparring or a construct, and he was not thinking about what it would feel like to hear his name called, and he was not thinking about what it would feel like if it wasn't. He was not thinking about Daran's face across the table, already slightly ahead of himself, already in a room that was one day away. He was not thinking about a line delivered on a south road four days ago and not repeated since. He was not thinking about Ashford. He was not thinking about a workroom with herbs on the ceiling beams, or a doorframe, or two fingers pressed briefly below a dried cut.

The drawer was closed.

Something moved at the edge of what he was not thinking about — formless, not quite arriving, a pressure that wasn't the pressure he knew, or was the same pressure wearing a different face. He waited for it the way he had learned to wait for it: still, not engaging. It came to the edge and did not cross.

He set it aside.

The city kept going. The window held its pale rectangle of light on the floor. Daran's breathing was steady and even and took up no more space than it needed.

Aldric closed his eyes.

He went to sleep.
