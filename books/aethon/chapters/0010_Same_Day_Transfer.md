# Chapter 10: Same Day Transfer

### Saga 1, Arc 3

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---

— Aldric Vane —

The official was a woman with grey in her hair and an expression that suggested she had done this exact thing many times and found it neither exciting nor unpleasant. She stood at the head of the assessment hall's exterior yard with a clipboard under one arm and read names from it in the same tone a person might use to read a shopping list.

"Successful candidates for same-day transfer to Greyveil Academy. When your name is called, collect your belongings and proceed to the convoy staging area. Carriages depart in one hour. Do not be late."

Aldric was already holding his pack.

His name had been on the first list — the results list — and now it was on this one. Two lists in one afternoon. He was starting to feel like a piece of furniture being moved between rooms.

He followed the group out through the yard's side gate, into a wide courtyard where four carriages waited in a line. They were plain and functional — dark wood, no crests, the kind of transport that said *the institution is providing this* rather than *someone important is inside.* Two drivers per carriage, already sitting. A junior official at the staging area directing students with small pointing gestures.

Aldric counted the group as they assembled. Around twenty. Maybe twenty-two. He had not paid close attention to the other candidates during the exam — he had been paying attention to the exam — but he could read the group now well enough. The noble students moved as if the convoy had been arranged specifically for them, which in some sense it had. The commoner students moved as if they were trying to take up a precisely correct amount of space — enough to be present, not enough to invite comment.

He was familiar with that particular kind of walking.

He chose the second carriage. Window seat on the left, which put his back partly to the interior wall and gave him a sightline to the door. He did not think about why he did this. He just did it.

---

Valdris Prime took a long time to leave.

The convoy moved north through streets that grew quieter as the residential districts gave way to the outer ring, then to the northern gate with its traffic of carts and merchants and people moving in both directions on the road. The city had a weight to it — a density of presence that Ashford did not have, that Aldric had barely started to adjust to in the days they had been here.

*Had been here.* He and Daran.

He put his attention on the window.

Through the glass: the northern gate arch, the pale stone giving way to packed road, the treeline beginning on either side as the city's edge fell back. The mountain became visible ahead — large, old, the kind of geography that made you aware of geological time in a way that was briefly disorienting. Somewhere up there, built into its base, was a place that was about to become his life.

The carriage smelled faintly of pine resin and something metallic. There were five other students in his carriage. Three of them were talking quietly — two boys and a girl, all noble by the look of their clothes, the girl doing most of the speaking and the two boys nodding with the body language of people who had agreed with her before she started. A fourth student, a boy about Aldric's age with dark hair and a very upright posture, sat reading a small book with the concentration of someone blocking out everything around him. The fifth was a girl with red-brown hair who sat with her hands folded in her lap and looked out the other window.

Aldric looked at the mountain and filed the carriage.

One of the noble boys shifted in his seat and said, to no one in particular: "I expected private transfers. My father took a private carriage to his intake."

"That was forty years ago," the girl said, without looking up from whatever she was saying.

"Still."

Aldric thought: *your father's carriage probably smelled better.* He did not say this. He turned back to the window and watched the city finish disappearing.

---

The road climbed.

The treeline thickened on both sides and the air through the cracked window edge changed — cooler, drier, carrying the particular smell of altitude that Aldric associated with the higher fields east of Ashford in autumn. The mountain was ahead and then it was beside them and then they were inside the shadow of it, moving along a road that had been cut into the slope.

He saw Greyveil first as a shape.

It resolved gradually from the treeline — stone against stone, walls that had been built to be part of the mountain rather than placed in front of it. The lower sections were darker, carved directly from the rock face. Above them: towers, structures, the geometry of a very large building that had been added to over a long period by people who were not always talking to each other. It was older than anything Aldric had seen. It was larger than the correspondence had made it sound.

He had read the line *the Greyveil Academy of the Arcane Arts, located at the northern edge of Valdris Prime* and constructed a building from those words. What he had constructed was wrong. Not wrong in a specific detail but wrong in scale — the way a map is technically accurate and still fails to prepare you for the actual road.

He looked at it for a long time.

The carriage descended slightly, following the road around a final curve, and the gates came into view. Stone pillars. Iron. An arch above with something carved into it that he could not read at this distance. The gates stood open. Two figures in grey uniforms flanked the entrance with the still, managed posture of people whose job was to look like they were not waiting.

He had no word for what he felt.

He filed the image instead.

---

The intake process was efficient.

A receiving hall just inside the gates — high-ceilinged, stone, lit by the kind of suspended lamp arrangement that said someone had made an institutional decision about adequate lighting and implemented it consistently across the whole building. Two long tables with academy staff behind them. Students formed a line without being asked to. This, Aldric noted, said something about the group he was arriving with — they already knew how to queue in front of a table.

He mapped the hall while the line moved: two doors off the main space besides the entrance, one east and one west. Ceiling approximately four and a half metres. Exits confirmed. Thirty-odd students in total across both tables. The staff behind the tables moved through names with practiced efficiency — name, confirmation, room assignment, brief verbal orientation in the time it took to find the correct page.

When he reached the table, the staff member — a man of middle age with the specific beard of someone who had decided to stop shaving and then slightly reconsidered — found his name on the third page.

"Vane, Aldric." Not a question.

"Yes."

"First-year placement, Foundation track. Dormitory block C, third floor, room fourteen. Orientation begins tomorrow morning, first bell. Common spaces are open this evening. Curfew at second night bell." He slid a small card across the table with a room number printed on it and a hand-drawn map of the dormitory block on the back. "Next."

Aldric took the card, moved aside, and looked at the map.

Block C. He oriented it against the hall's layout and the gates' position and determined that C was north-east. He did not ask for clarification. He walked toward the east door.

---

The dormitory corridor was narrow.

Not uncomfortably so — two people could pass if they turned slightly — but narrow in the way of buildings that had been designed with function in mind and architecture as an afterthought. Stone floor, stone walls, wood-framed doors at regular intervals on both sides. The sounds of the other new students moving through the space came from behind him, ahead of him, through the walls.

Room fourteen was on the right side, third from the end.

He pushed the door open.

Small. Clean. A single bunk — frame, mattress, a folded blanket at the foot. A desk against the wall under a narrow window. A hook beside the door for a pack. A lamp on the desk, unit, beside a short candle stub.

The window faced north. Mountain behind it, which meant the light in the morning would come from the side. He noted this.

He set his pack on the floor and sat on the edge of the bunk.

The room had one bunk. He had known it would have one bunk. He had known since the letter, since the inn, since before — since the moment he had understood what three-stage meant and what sub-threshold meant. He had known. He still noticed it.

The absence was not a thought. It was not a name. It was more like the awareness of a space where a sound had been and was not anymore — the moment after a door closes when the room recalibrates around the new quiet.

He let it be there.

Then he filed it.

He looked at the window. The light had gone from gold to the particular flat grey that came just before dark — that narrow seam between day and night where colours lost their specificity. The mountain was a shape outside the glass. Beyond the wall, down the corridor, voices moved through the stone in fragments. Someone laughed once, short and bright. A door opened and closed.

Aldric put his pack on the hook. He sat back down on the bunk.

He mapped the room: one door, one window, one bunk, one desk, one lamp. Four metres by three and a half, approximately. Hook by the door, functional. Floor level, stone, no irregularities worth noting. Ceiling height adequate. No draft from the window.

Contents: himself.

He lay down.

The blanket smelled like it had been stored somewhere dry and a long time. The bunk frame was solid — no movement when he shifted his weight, which suggested either good construction or recent reinforcement, and he found he could not currently bring himself to care which. His boots were still on. He did not take them off.

The corridor sounds continued at their low, ambient level. The mountain was dark outside the window. Somewhere below, the academy was settling into its first evening with a new year of students, and Aldric Vane from Ashford — who had passed three exams today and arrived at a place he had been heading toward for as long as he could remember — lay on his bunk in a room the size of his bedroom at home, and listened to the dark arrive.

Tomorrow he would learn the shape of the place.

Tonight he would let the room be enough.

He closed his eyes.
