# Chapter 36: Lirien Watching

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Lirien —

The problem with most people's control was that they thought of it as a wall. Something you built once, thick enough, and then stood behind.

Lirien had never found a wall that didn't eventually crack somewhere nobody had thought to reinforce. She preferred to think of control as a debt, continuously serviced — paid down to nothing, every single time, so there was never a balance left over for anything to leak out of. It was less comforting than a wall. It was also, empirically, the only version of the idea that had never once failed her.

She proved it again now, in an empty stone courtyard behind the east wing that nobody used at this hour because nobody at this academy was awake at this hour who didn't have a specific reason to be, tracing a slow half-circle with two fingers extended and watching the frost spread outward from the point of contact in threads that interlaced exactly the way she told them to. No gaps. No unevenness where a hurried cast would have left one. The pattern reached its full radius, held, and began, on schedule, to recede — the cold receding from the stone the same unhurried way it had arrived, leaving nothing behind but a faint damp sheen and, for a moment, breath fog she hadn't bothered to suppress because there was no one here to read anything into it.

It was, by any reasonable measure, an unremarkable use of an hour. She had cast this particular pattern several hundred times over several years, in exactly this courtyard, and it had never once surprised her. That was the entire point of it. Surprise, in her experience, was what happened to people who hadn't paid down the debt in full.

She thought, doing the small maintenance work of reviewing the cast the way she reviewed everything — was the spread even, was the recession clean, was there any part of the pattern that had cost more than the plan called for — about Aldric Vane's shell, weeks gone now, leaking at its seams under bolts that shouldn't have troubled it at all. It was, she had decided somewhere around the second time she watched it happen, an unusually clean case study. His control wasn't absent. His attention simply went wherever he stopped actively pointing it, and something arrived in the gap that hadn't been invited. She had told him as much, eventually, when he'd finally asked the actual question instead of only staring at her like a puzzle he hadn't worked out yet he was allowed to ask about.

She had also told him about the eleven seconds. That part, if she was being precise with herself — and she was, on principle, always precise with herself, even where it would have been more comfortable not to be — was not confirmed. It was a pattern she had noticed across three separate incidents and extrapolated forward, the way any competent analyst extrapolated from insufficient data while being honest with themselves about the insufficiency. She believed something changed for him at that mark. She did not know it. The distinction mattered to her more than it seemed to matter to most people, who tended to round a strong suspicion up to a fact the moment it became convenient.

What she had not told him — what she had, in fact, not examined very closely herself, in the specific way she avoided examining anything that didn't resolve into a useful conclusion — was that she had started timing him well before she'd had any technical reason to.

The frost finished its recession. She reset her stance and ran the pattern again, because a thing worth doing once was worth confirming twice, and because it gave her hands something to do while the rest of her worked through an idea she would rather not have been working through this early in the morning.

The bracket went up today. She already knew where his name would sit in it, had known for several days, in the specific way she knew most outcomes before they were officially confirmed — not guessing, calculation, built from watching him drill against opponents whose ceilings she'd already mapped and finding, every time, that his ceiling simply hadn't been located yet. Fourth seed, she'd have said, if anyone had asked her to commit to a number before the board went up. She expected to be right. She was almost always right about numbers like this.

She did not examine, closely, why she had bothered to calculate it at all, three days before anyone posted a schedule that made the calculation relevant to her.

The second casting held as cleanly as the first. She let it recede, dried her hands on her sleeve out of habit rather than need, and turned to find Mireth watching her from the courtyard's far archway, arms folded, in the specific posture of someone who had been standing there for longer than she was willing to admit.

"You practice at an hour nobody civilized is awake for," Mireth said, by way of greeting, "which either means you're better than the rest of us or that you need more repetitions than the rest of us to feel like you are. I've never been able to decide which."

"Both, most days."

"At least you're honest about it." Mireth pushed off the archway and came no closer, which was itself a kind of message between them, two years of an unspoken agreement about the minimum distance a conversation required. "I hear the bolt-shell boy is seeded fourth."

"I hadn't heard."

"You're a very poor liar for someone who never says anything false out loud." Mireth's smile had exactly as much warmth in it as Lirien's usually did, which was to say a precisely calibrated and entirely intentional amount. "You've been watching that yard more than your own drills lately. People notice these things. I notice these things. It's practically my hobby."

"Your hobby is being wrong about people, historically."

"Once. I was wrong about you once, three years ago, and you've never once let me forget it." Mireth's eyes narrowed, not unkindly, in the way a rivalry that had outlasted its original reason sometimes narrowed — more habit than heat. "I'm not wrong about this."

Lirien didn't answer that, which was, she was aware, itself an answer, and one Mireth would file away with the particular relish she reserved for exactly this kind of small, unearned victory. She let her have it. There were battles worth the cost of fighting and battles that cost more to win than to simply let stand unresolved, and she had learned, over a rivalry considerably older than her interest in Aldric Vane, which category most of Mireth's provocations fell into.

"Go look at your board," Lirien said instead. "I'm sure it has something in it for you to be smug about."

"It always does." Mireth was already turning, unbothered, satisfied with the exchange on whatever terms she'd decided to keep score by. "Third seed. Try to look surprised when they read it out."

She left without waiting for a reply, which suited Lirien fine, since she hadn't intended to give one. She stood a moment longer in the emptying courtyard, running the exchange back the way she ran everything, checking it for anything she'd have preferred to leave unsaid, and found, uncomfortably, that the sentence she kept returning to wasn't anything Mireth had said at all.

*You've been watching that yard more than your own drills lately.*

It was, she thought, an inconvenient thing to have confirmed by the one person at Greyveil least inclined to let a fact like that go unmentioned. She filed it, the way she filed most things that didn't yet have a use, and went to see the paper for herself, the way everyone would, because that was the ordinary thing to do and she had no interest in being seen doing anything else.

---

The corridor outside the administrative wing was already a current by the time she reached it — first years mostly, moving in the specific compressed excitement of people who had been waiting for something and finally had permission to have opinions about it out loud. Lirien moved through it at her usual pace, which had never once been mistaken for haste, and found the board exactly where it always stood, framed now in fresher wood, thirty-two names sealed under glass where excitement couldn't reach in and change anything.

She found her own name first, out of habit — third seed, a fact she registered and set down again without particular feeling, since her own seeding had never been in question — and then, immediately after, his.

Fourth. Exactly where she'd said it would sit, if anyone had asked, which they hadn't, because she hadn't told anyone she'd made the prediction in the first place.

She watched, from the edge of the crowd, at the specific unhurried distance she kept from crowds generally, as the current around the board rearranged itself into its component reactions. Rynn's noise — loud, delighted, entirely unsurprised at his own middling placement, because Rynn's relationship with his own mediocrity had always been more comfortable than most people's relationship with their competence. Solen, arriving a half-step behind him, already converting the bracket into information rather than emotion, the way Solen converted everything. Crest's cluster, tighter than usual, studying a seeding near the top of the sheet with the specific stillness of people recalculating something they'd assumed was settled.

And Aldric, standing in front of his own name with an expression she had learned, over the course of a great many silent observations he did not know she'd made, to read as *doing arithmetic he does not want anyone to watch him do.* She watched him do it now. She was, if she allowed herself the honesty, rather good at watching him do it, had gotten good at it over a longer stretch of the term than she would have volunteered if anyone had thought to ask.

This was, she told herself, purely analytical. He was the most interesting technical problem the yard had produced all year — a control failure with no clean cause, an output that refused to plateau where every measurable factor said it should, a boy whose ceiling nobody, including him, had located yet. Watching him was simply due diligence. A competent analyst kept eyes on an unsolved problem until it solved itself or she solved it for them.

She held that thought for exactly as long as it took to notice she was still holding it, three days after she'd first told herself the same sentence, in the same tone, about the same boy, over a stone floor with nobody watching her use it.

It wasn't entirely true. She had known that, somewhere underneath the parts of herself she kept in better order than this, for longer than she was prepared to have known it. A purely analytical interest did not require her to have already worked out, unprompted, where his name would land on a bracket before the bracket existed. It did not require the specific, private satisfaction she'd felt seeing the number four sit exactly where she'd placed it three days early — not the satisfaction of a hypothesis confirmed, though she'd dressed it in that shape to herself at the time, but something closer to a want getting what it wanted and not particularly caring what shape she'd disguised the want in to get there.

She did not enjoy that discovery. She found, examining it further — because she examined everything further, that was simply what she did, whether or not the examination produced a comfortable answer — that she did not entirely know what to do with a want that had apparently been operating for weeks without her supervision.

Near the board, Aldric said something to Solen she didn't catch, and Solen answered with the particular flatness that meant he'd noticed something and decided, for now, not to press it — a habit she recognized because she practiced a version of it herself, daily, with rather more at stake than Solen ever seemed to think was riding on his own silences.

She did not go closer. There was no reason to, not one she could defend to herself in the same clean, precise terms she defended everything else in, and she had built her entire method on never doing a thing she couldn't defend to herself in clean, precise terms.

That, she thought, turning at last to walk back the way she'd come, at the same unhurried pace she'd arrived with, was very likely the actual problem. Not the boy. Not his ceiling, or his leaking control, or the eleven seconds she still couldn't fully account for and refused to call settled fact until she could. The problem was that she had picked his name off a board that didn't exist yet, three days before anyone else could have, and had done it so quietly and so completely that she hadn't noticed she was doing it until it was already finished.

A piece of paper under glass had told the rest of the year something they hadn't known this morning.

It had only told her something she'd already known, and simply hadn't been willing, until just now, to look at directly.

That bothered her considerably more than the bracket did.
