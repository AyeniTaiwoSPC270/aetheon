# Chapter 15: Circuit Fundamentals

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

"This diagram is wrong," said Instructor Maret. "Find out why."

He hadn't said good morning. He hadn't said his name. He sat down at the desk at the front of the room, opened a large ledger, uncapped a pen, and waited.

Aldric looked at the board.

Someone had drawn a circuit diagram on it before they arrived. A thick trunk line ran up from the bottom, split into three branches, and each branch split again into knots and fine lines. The whole thing looked finished. It looked like something a textbook would print without embarrassment.

That was the trap. He could feel it, the way you feel a step that isn't where your foot expects it.

Mana Theory ran on a different rotation from Combat, which meant this was the first time since he'd arrived that someone had put Aldric in a room and expected him to *know things*. The room itself was a steep half-bowl of benches, worn smooth by decades of students trying not to be called on.

"Answers are given aloud, in seating order, starting at the front," Maret said, without looking up. "Each answer is recorded. Nothing is corrected today. At the year-end review, your answers are compared."

Nobody breathed.

"Recorded," whispered Rynn, next to Aldric. "*Compared.* I was up until midnight polishing lamp brackets, and now I'm being *compared.*"

He looked as though he'd been hit by a cart and then asked to recite poetry. He had slid onto the bench four seconds before Maret started talking, and his hair still stuck out on one side.

"Forty-one brackets," he muttered. "On the east stair. I counted them."

"Forty-three," said Solen.

Aldric turned his head. Solen sat on his other side, neat as ever, a folded page squared to the edge of the bench in front of him.

"Two are behind the pillar," Solen said. "You can only see them from the landing."

"How do you know that?" Rynn hissed.

"I walked past them yesterday."

"You *counted lamp brackets*?"

"It took eleven seconds."

Aldric decided he was going to enjoy this friendship, or whatever it was going to turn out to be. Provided he survived the morning.

At the front, Maret's pen touched the ledger. "Mr. Halvon."

---

Crest stood up as though the room had been built around him.

The lip had healed almost completely. Only a slight shadow remained, and he carried it like a medal he refused to acknowledge. He didn't glance at Aldric. He'd made a point of not glancing at Aldric since yesterday, which Aldric found more interesting than being glared at.

"The diagram violates return continuity," Crest said. "The terminal at the third branch has no closing loop. In sustained flow the mana would accumulate against the terminus with no route back to the trunk."

Every word was correct. Every word had also been polished on someone's tongue for years.

"Recorded," said Maret. His pen scratched. "Miss Seya."

"Missing return path, third branch, as Halvon said," said Seya. She had the air of a girl who had never lost an argument by agreeing.

"Recorded. Miss Croft."

Nessa Croft, the noble girl who had asked him about curfew on the first day, stood up. She wasn't nervous. She wasn't showing off either. She looked at the board for a moment as if it were a mildly inconvenient fence.

"It doesn't go back anywhere," she said. "The last part is a dead end. Stuff would get stuck."

Someone near the front snickered. Nessa waited, looking politely uninterested, until they stopped.

"Recorded," said Maret, and Aldric thought the pen moved more slowly than it had for Crest.

"Mr. Halvon's phrasing is loose," a voice added from the second row. It was the elf, Lira Voss, dry as paper. "Not continuity. Loop closure. The flow continues just fine. It just has nowhere to *end*."

"Miss Voss, you were not called on." Maret's pen kept moving. "Recorded regardless."

Aldric's stomach did something complicated.

The diagram wasn't the problem. He could see the problem. There was a dead end at the far tip of the third branch, drawn as neatly as everything else, and any flow that went in would simply stay there. But he'd learned this from the inside. He knew what it *felt* like when a line went nowhere. He didn't know a single one of the words the others had just used.

*Return continuity. Loop closure. Terminus.* They fell out of these people's mouths like coins.

He had a pamphlet. He'd had *one* pamphlet, and Daran had paid a week of lunches for it.

The thought landed harder than he'd expected. He shoved it away.

"Mr. Solen."

Solen stood. He did it without any fuss, the way he did everything.

"Node six is drawn as a terminal," he said. "A terminal can hold flow but not release it. Sustained input would saturate the upstream branch. The fix is a return path from six to the trunk."

Two sentences. No wasted words. Aldric saw Crest's shoulders tighten.

"Recorded," said Maret. "Mr. Vane."

Aldric stood.

He was very aware of his own hands. He was very aware of the room, and of the exact number of people who would enjoy watching him fail. Crest, Milo, Seya. A couple of nobles near the back who'd been eyeing his hair since the assembly hall.

*Bluff,* said a small useful voice in his head. *Pick one of their words and use it.*

He didn't like it. It would work for about a sentence. Then Maret would ask a follow-up, and the whole thing would collapse in front of everyone, and Crest would get exactly what he'd been denied since he'd arrived.

So he didn't.

"I don't know the word for it," Aldric said.

Someone snickered. Crest's mouth curved.

"Can I say what it does instead?"

Maret's pen paused. It didn't stop. It just hesitated, the way a hand does over a step it hasn't seen before. "Go on."

"The last knot is a dead end." Aldric pointed at it. "If you push anything through, it has nowhere to go. So it piles up, and then it pushes back the way it came."

"That's what Mr. Solen said," Crest said, without turning around.

"Mostly." Aldric kept his eyes on the board. "But it wouldn't fail at the dead end."

The room went quiet in a different way.

"It'd fail *here.*" He stepped closer and put a finger on the branch just before the terminal, where the lines pinched to almost nothing. "This part. It's thin. Pressure doesn't go to the nearest wall. It goes to the weakest one. So that's where it'd tear."

He stopped. He realised he'd said all of it without a single formal word.

Nobody moved.

Maret's pen had stopped. Not paused. *Stopped*, hovering just above the ledger, and it stayed there for a count of two.

Then it moved on again and wrote.

"Recorded," said Maret.

That was all. His face hadn't changed at all. Aldric thought he'd probably have seen more emotion on the board.

Crest turned around at last. It was the first thing he had said to Aldric all morning.

"Read that in a pamphlet, did you, Vane?" he said, lazily.

It was a *good* line. It had exactly the right amount of contempt, and half the room laughed. It would have landed perfectly, a few days ago.

"One," said Aldric. "I read it until it fell apart. Then I read the pieces."

There was a beat.

Then Rynn snorted so violently that he had to bury his face in his sleeve, and the laughter that followed wasn't the same laughter. Even Pell, the lupine boy by the window, huffed through his nose. Crest sat down more slowly than he'd stood up.

Aldric sat too. His knees weren't quite steady.

"That," Solen murmured beside him, without moving his lips, "was not what I expected."

"Neither was I," Aldric muttered.

"Good. I dislike being right about people too early."

---

The rest of the round passed in a blur. The remaining students repeated versions of the same three terms. Maret recorded them all in the same even hand, and none of them earned a stopped pen.

Then he closed the ledger and stood.

"Exercise," he said. "Right hand, palm up. Draw a thread of mana across the palm. One thread, no thicker than a hair. Hold it for sixty seconds."

"Sixty seconds," Rynn whispered in horror. "Of *thin*."

"Thin is the whole point, Mr. Rynn."

Aldric put his hand out and thought about not doing anything.

That was the trick. Everything he'd been taught by accident, and everything he'd kept from being taught at all, said the same thing: go small. Keep it small. Whatever you have, don't hand it over all at once. Ashford had made him careful long before this room had.

He drew the thread.

It came easily. It always did. A fine bright line across his palm, thin as a hair, steady as a taut string. He held it and did not push. He kept his breathing level and his face bored.

Around him the room hummed with small effortful sounds. Someone's thread frayed and snapped. Crest's, three benches up, was perfect. Rynn's kept flaring and jumping at the edges, and he swore under his breath every time.

Maret walked the rows.

He didn't hurry. He passed along each bench and looked at each hand for a moment, the way a tailor might look at cloth. Aldric counted his steps without meaning to. Nine, ten, eleven.

Maret stopped at his desk.

Aldric kept his eyes forward. He kept the thread thin. He kept his breathing exactly where it had been.

*One.*

Maret didn't say anything.

*Two.*

Aldric's thread didn't waver. He had the strangest feeling he was being read.

He'd had it once before. In the Ashford guild hall, when the assessor had checked his result twice and said nothing at all. It was the same pause. It even had the same shape.

*Three.*

*Four.*

Maret took a small notebook out of his coat, a different notebook from the ledger, and wrote a single line. It took no time at all. Then he put it away and moved on to the next bench.

Aldric held the thread for another thirty-one seconds.

"Time," said Maret.

---

"Read chapters two through four of the foundation text before the next session," said Maret, at the door. "That is all."

The room emptied noisily, like a bucket tipped over. Rynn stumbled out first, muttering about lamps. Crest went past without a glance. Seya glanced at him, briefly, in the way of someone adding a line to a very long list.

Aldric took his time packing his things.

"Don't turn around," said Solen quietly.

Aldric didn't. He put a book into his bag very slowly.

"He stopped at your desk for four seconds," Solen said. He was squaring the corners of a page, and he did it perfectly. "He stopped at nobody else's."

Aldric's hand paused on the strap.

"Maybe he liked my thread."

"Maybe."

"You counted," Aldric said.

"It's a habit." Solen tucked the page away.

He left before Aldric could reply. That was becoming a habit too.

Aldric stood alone at the bench for a moment. Then he turned, very casually, as if he were looking for something he'd dropped.

Instructor Maret was back at the front desk.

The big ledger lay open in front of him, every answer from the morning laid out in careful ink, for anyone to read. He wasn't looking at it. He was standing very still, facing the window, one hand resting on the pocket of his coat.

The one with the small notebook in it.

The ledger stayed on the desk, open.

The notebook did not.
