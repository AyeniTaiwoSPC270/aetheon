# Chapter 44: The Final

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


The King of Valdenmere turned out to be a smaller man than his portraits suggested, and considerably more bored-looking, at least until the moment Aldric actually stepped into the arena, at which point every single thing about his posture changed at once.

Aldric noticed this the way he noticed everything that mattered before he'd decided to notice it — a fact arriving ahead of any thought about it — and filed it, because there wasn't time to do anything else with it. The Grand Arena was fuller than it had been for any match yet, tier upon tier of House colors and commoner overflow crowding the cheap stone above the noble boxes, and the noise that met him walking out wasn't the noise of a crowd anticipating a good match. It was the noise of a crowd that had already decided this one mattered more than the others, for reasons that had very little to do with swordplay or spellcraft and everything to do with a story they'd all quietly agreed, over the last four days, that they wanted to watch finish.

Rynn's voice found him even over that, because Rynn's voice found him over most things. "You've got this," he shouted, from somewhere in the stands Aldric couldn't immediately place. "You've absolutely — I can't see you properly, is that you, the one who looks nervous—"

"That's me."

"GOOD LUCK THEN."

Somewhere closer to the noble tiers, considerably quieter, Solen was almost certainly counting something. Aldric didn't look for him. He'd learned, across two terms, that some kinds of support worked better unobserved.

Tavish Ardell was already in the ring, and looked, as he had in every match Aldric had watched him fight, entirely unbothered by the size of the crowd or the weight of who was in it. He inclined his head once, formal, the first direct acknowledgment he'd given Aldric in four days of sharing the same tournament.

"Vane," he said.

"Ardell."

"I've watched your matches. Bale, then Torval." A pause, unhurried in exactly the way Solen had warned him it would be. "You win by making yourself difficult to predict. I want you to know, before we start, that I've accounted for that. I don't need to predict you. I only need you to eventually spend more than you can afford."

"That's very honest of you."

"There's no advantage in hiding it. You already know how I fight. Solen scouted me two days ago, from the gap in the east stands. I saw him." The faintest suggestion of something that might, on a less controlled face, have been amusement. "I let him watch. It didn't change anything I planned to do."

Aldric filed that too — a small, cold confirmation that whatever quiet advantage Solen thought he'd handed him, Ardell had already priced it in — and felt, for the first time all week, something closer to real fear than nerves.

*You've made him wait longer than his plan accounts for,* Solen had said, three days ago, in a gap between the stands. *That's the only gap there is.*

It was the only plan he had left. He decided, walking to his mark, that it would have to be enough.

For the first three minutes, it very nearly wasn't.

Ardell's water technique rose the moment the judge called the ring open — not a shield exactly, something closer to a curved sheet of the air's own moisture drawn taut and made solid, rippling faintly at its edges like a held breath. Aldric's first Mana Bolt struck it and simply vanished into it, absorbed rather than blocked, the way water takes a stone without ever quite breaking.

"That's new," Aldric said, watching a faint cloudiness stir inside the ward where the bolt had gone.

"It holds what it takes. Up to a point." Ardell didn't sound like a man explaining a trick. He sounded like a man stating a fact he'd stated many times before, to many opponents, none of whom had found a use for the information. "I'll release it eventually, on my own schedule, at a moment of my choosing. Until then, everything you spend against it is simply banked."

It was, Aldric realized with a sinking, familiar feeling, exactly the trap Solen had described — a wall that turned every attack into fuel for its own eventual answer, patient past the point where patience should have been possible for anyone his age. Aldric slowed his own pace deliberately, refusing the rhythm Ardell's stillness invited, and for a long, strange stretch of the match neither of them did very much at all — two patient fighters, each waiting for the other to be the one who broke first, while the crowd's noise thinned into something closer to held breath than cheering.

It was Aldric who broke it, eventually, though not the way anyone watching expected. He stopped attacking the ward at all. He simply moved — sideways, low, forcing Ardell to track him rather than absorb him, spending nothing, offering nothing for the ward to bank — and watched, with something that might have been the first real hope of the match, the faint line of concentration deepen at the corner of Ardell's mouth.

*He's holding the ward open longer than he wants to. He doesn't like this. He's used to a fight that feeds it.*

Aldric kept moving. He didn't attack again for nearly a full minute — an eternity, in tournament terms, long enough that a section of the crowd actually started to boo, certain they were watching two frightened boys stall a final neither wanted to lose — and somewhere in that minute, low and insistent, the thing in his chest started testing the door again.

He recognized it instantly this time. There was no surprise left in it, only the same old, tired argument he'd been having with himself since Ashford: *not now, not here, not in front of the entire kingdom.*

And then, for the first time in two years, he heard himself ask a different question.

*Why not?*

He didn't have an answer that satisfied him. He had, instead, a memory — Essa, the day before, in the quiet behind the library: *that was the mistake, as far as this place is concerned. Just showing up looking the way you do.* He had spent his entire life at Greyveil treating the thing in his chest as a mistake to be managed, filed, suppressed before anyone could see it clearly enough to ask what it actually was. He had never once, not in two years, considered simply letting it be what it was, in front of everyone, and finding out what happened.

He stopped fighting it.

It didn't arrive the way it had every previous time — not a burst, not an ambush, not something that happened to him while he stood helplessly at the centre of it. It arrived the way a held breath finally, deliberately released arrives: total, immediate, and — for the first time — chosen. The air around him didn't merely distort. It bent, visibly, a silver-white shimmer rolling off him in a wave that had no clean edge to it, no aimed point, nothing that any registry entry could have called a technique. It was simply him, entirely, arriving all at once instead of leaking through a seam.

Ardell's ward met it the only way it knew how to meet anything — by trying to absorb it, to bank it the way it had banked every ordinary bolt and shell for three long minutes — and for one full second, the water held. Aldric watched, through the strange, cold clarity that always came with this, the cloudiness inside the ward go from faint stirring to something closer to storm, the whole curved sheet of it straining under a weight it had no category for.

Then it didn't shatter, exactly. It simply stopped being water at all, force passing through the place where a wall used to be as though the wall had never fully decided what it was fighting, and Tavish Ardell — controlled, patient, undefeated all tournament on nothing but precision — went down onto the sand without ever having thrown a counter, because there had been nothing left in his plan for an opponent who stopped trying to win.

The silence afterward lasted almost two full seconds before the crowd remembered how to make noise at all.

Aldric came back to himself the way he always did — the silver bleeding slowly out of his vision, the world resolving from something too bright and too still into ordinary color and ordinary sound — and found Tavish Ardell already sitting up, unhurt, staring at the space where his ward had been with an expression Aldric had never once seen on him before: something close to genuine confusion.

"That wasn't a spell," Ardell said. Not accusing. Just accurate, in the same flat register he used for everything.

"No," Aldric agreed, and offered a hand down, the same gesture Torval had given Rynn, the same one Crest had given Bryn Aldous, the small tournament grammar that meant this is over and we're both still here. "It wasn't."

"I don't know what it was."

"Neither do I, most days."

Ardell took the hand, considering him with something that had shed all its earlier certainty and hadn't yet decided what to replace it with. "I built that ward on the principle that force is force. Something to be measured and absorbed and returned." A pause, and for once, something in his flat delivery genuinely faltered. "Whatever that was, it didn't measure."

Aldric didn't have anything true to say to that, so he said nothing at all, and let the crowd's noise — now fully returned, now a solid physical wall of sound crashing down from every tier at once — carry the moment somewhere he didn't have to explain it.

He looked for the King first, out of some instinct he didn't examine closely, and found him on his feet, along with everyone in the royal box, applauding with the specific, slightly startled enthusiasm of a man who'd expected a good match and gotten something considerably stranger. Somewhere below the box, in the faculty tier, Maret sat with his notebook already open on his knee, not writing yet, simply watching, his face giving away exactly nothing. Further along the same tier, Lirien sat very still, hands folded, and did not applaud at all — watching the space where the wave had rolled through with an expression Aldric couldn't read from this distance and suspected he wouldn't have been able to read from any distance.

He looked, finally, for the man in expensive grey — the same unhurried, unreadable presence from the semifinal, seated three rows back from the envoy's box — and found the seat empty. Gone, sometime in the last several minutes, without waiting to see how the match he'd apparently found so interesting actually ended.

Aldric stood with that for a moment, the cheering enormous and disorienting around him, and felt something cold settle in underneath all the noise — a small, specific unease that had nothing to do with winning and everything to do with the particular kind of person who watched a thing that closely and then left before it finished.

He filed it. There was, for the moment, nothing else to do with it.

Rynn's voice found him again, closer this time, already climbing over three separate strangers to reach the rail. "YOU WON. Aldric. You WON the entire — I don't even have words, I've used all of them already, I used them all on the semifinal—"

"You could try new ones."

"There aren't any left. You've used up my whole vocabulary in four days. I'm going to have to learn new words just to keep up with you, and frankly I resent it."

Aldric let himself smile at that, finally, the first real one all match, and turned to meet him, and did not look back toward the empty seat again, though some part of him — the part that had spent two years reading rooms for a living — already knew he was going to think about it long after the cheering stopped.
