# Chapter 4: The Last Ordinary Day

### Saga 1, Arc 1

---

— Aldric Vane —

The letter wasn't there in the morning.

Aldric checked without meaning to — a glance at the small table by the door where Maren left anything that came through the kingdom post, which was almost never anything, which was why he glanced at it, which was why he immediately looked away and went to find his boots.

The morning was the kind that late summer produced when it was being generous: warm light coming in at a low angle, cooler air underneath it that suggested the season knew it was almost done and was making an effort. He pulled the door shut behind him and stood on the step for a moment, for no reason, just standing.

Daran was waiting at the south end of the road.

This was not unusual. Daran was always slightly earlier than agreed and slightly impatient about it, which Aldric had long since stopped apologising for because the impatience never lasted more than thirty seconds and then they were just walking.

"You're late," Daran said.

"I'm exactly on time."

"I've been here for ten minutes."

"That's a you problem."

Daran looked at him with the specific expression he reserved for things that weren't worth responding to, which was one of his more useful expressions because it covered a lot of ground. They fell into step without discussion, the way you do when you have walked the same routes with the same person enough times that your feet remember the direction before your head does.

The errand was nothing — a bundle of dried cuttings Maren had set aside for old Presswick on the eastern row, who had bad knees and didn't come to the market anymore. Aldric had the bundle under his arm. It smelled like the workroom: green and dry and faintly medicinal. He had been smelling it his whole life and had never once thought to name it until this moment, which was strange and which he didn't examine.

They went the long way without deciding to.

The village looked exactly like itself. Wyla had her stall open, the root oil and dry goods arranged the same way they always were, and she gave Aldric a short nod as they passed that meant *tell your mother I'll settle the account Thursday.* He nodded back. He didn't know how he knew that was what the nod meant. He just did.

"Did you look at the positions again last night?" Daran asked.

"The pamphlet positions?"

"No, a different set of positions I invented in my sleep. Yes, the pamphlet."

Aldric considered this. "I looked at them."

"And?"

"And I looked at them."

Daran made a short sound that was not quite a sigh. "The written portion of the formal exam has a theory component. The pamphlet covers two of the seven basic channel-opening frameworks. If you know those two, you know how the other five are structured."

"I know."

"You don't practice."

"I know the positions."

"Knowing and practicing are different."

"Daran."

"What."

"I know."

Daran was quiet for a moment. Then: "Fine."

It wasn't fine in the sense of resolved. It was fine in the sense of *I've made my point and you've acknowledged receiving it and I'll return to this later.* Aldric had learned to distinguish between these early on.

They delivered the cuttings. Old Presswick opened the door approximately six seconds after they knocked, which meant he had been watching the road, and thanked them with the particular thoroughness of someone who did not get many visitors and was going to make the most of the ones he had. Aldric stayed for the full length of it. Daran lasted four minutes before he drifted to the doorstep and stood there doing a very convincing impression of someone simply getting some air.

Walking back, the sun had shifted. Still warm. The air underneath it was cooler than the morning.

"Six weeks," Daran said.

Aldric didn't ask what he meant. "Give or take."

"The assessor said six to eight. Six weeks is the low end. We're at six weeks."

"I know what six weeks means."

"I'm just noting that it could come today."

"It could come today," Aldric agreed.

Daran nodded once, satisfied, and didn't say anything else about it. But Aldric noticed he walked slightly faster on the way back. Not urgently. Just — faster.

---

The letter wasn't there at midday either.

Aldric stopped checking after that. Or he stopped checking intentionally, which wasn't quite the same thing, but was close enough that he could tell himself it was.

Maren was in the workroom. The smell of it reached him before he reached the door — green and dry and warm from the sun coming through the south window. She was at the drying rack, turning stems with the automatic efficiency of someone whose hands knew the task well enough that her attention could be elsewhere.

"Presswick?" she said, without looking up.

"Grateful. Thorough about it."

"He's lonely."

"He mentioned his nephew three times."

"He always mentions the nephew." She turned another stem. "The nephew never comes."

Aldric leaned in the doorway. The workroom was the smallest room in the house and the most full — herbs hanging from the ceiling beams in rough-dried bunches, jars on the lower shelf arranged by frequency of use in an order that looked arbitrary and wasn't, the shallow rack under the window holding whatever was mid-process. He had grown up ducking the ceiling bunches. He did it without thinking.

"Daran thinks the letter comes today," he said.

"Daran has thought that for two weeks."

"He's more confident about it today."

Maren set down the stem she was turning and looked at him. Not with alarm. Not with anything that could be named grief. With the specific quality of attention she gave to things she had already thought through — complete and present and without urgency.

"Are you ready for it?" she asked.

Aldric considered lying. Not from dishonesty — from the old habit of not making things heavier than they needed to be. He looked at the jars on the lower shelf instead of at her.

"I think so," he said.

She nodded once. Picked up the stem again. "Then it doesn't matter when it comes."

He stayed in the doorway another moment. She had already returned her attention to the rack. Both of them understood that the conversation was complete.

---

The letter came in the mid-afternoon.

Two envelopes. Kingdom seal — not red wax, which was for official decrees, but the plain brown that the assessment office used for correspondence. One addressed to Aldric Vane. One addressed to Daran, care of the Vane household, because Daran had been here when the preliminary review dates were filed and Aldric had listed this address without thinking and no one had corrected it.

Josse brought them in from the post. He set them on the table by the door — the same table Aldric had glanced at that morning — and said: "Post came."

Aldric looked at the envelopes.

Daran, who had materialised in the Vane household sometime in the past hour in the way he occasionally did, picked up his envelope before Aldric picked up his. He broke the seal cleanly, read the contents once, and set it down.

"Confirmed," he said.

Aldric opened his.

The language was formal and impersonal — standard kingdom administration, the kind of prose that existed to transmit information without wasting either party's time. His name. The date of the formal examination. The location: Valdris Prime, the Greyveil assessment hall. Reporting instructions. What to bring. A brief line about accommodation for travelling candidates from outer settlements.

He read it twice. Not because he needed to. Because once felt insufficient for something that was apparently real now.

Maren appeared in the doorway from the workroom. She looked at his face, not the letter.

"Good," she said. One word, quiet, certain — not *congratulations,* not *I'm proud,* just *good,* in the tone that meant *this is what was supposed to happen and it has happened and that is enough.*

Josse, who had not left, looked at Aldric with the direct patience that was simply his way of paying attention. He said: "Four days to the capital, if the road's clear."

Aldric looked at him.

"Start thinking about your boots," Josse said. "Yours won't make it."

He went back outside.

Aldric looked at the letter again. He was aware that Daran was already at the table with the pamphlet open, cross-referencing something. He was aware of the specific quality of afternoon light coming through the front window — the way it fell across the table, the envelopes, the ordinary grain of the wood.

He folded the letter and put it in his pocket.

---

They ended up in the field.

Not by decision — it was simply where they went, in the way that a thing you have done enough times stops requiring a decision and becomes the thing you do. The back fence. The open field beyond it going golden in the evening light. The treeline at the far edge, already catching shadow.

Aldric sat on the fence. Daran stood beside him, forearms on the top rail, looking at nothing in particular.

The village behind them was doing what it always did at this hour. Smoke from the evening fires. Someone's dog making a brief complaint about something and stopping. The low movement of ordinary life settling into itself for the night.

"I've been thinking about the combat portion," Daran said.

"Of course you have."

"The preliminary review was just assessment. The formal exam has a combat application component. It's not graded on output — it's graded on technique and adaptability. Which means it's not just about what you can do, it's about whether you can adjust when what you're doing isn't working."

"Mm."

"Which you're better at than me."

Aldric looked at him. This was not the kind of thing Daran said.

Daran was still looking at the field. His expression was the same — level, forward, giving nothing away. "I'm faster on written comprehension. You read situations better than I do. We're different." He said it like a notation. Like something he had assessed and filed. "It's fine."

Aldric was quiet for a moment. "When did you start keeping score like that?"

"I've always kept score like that."

"I know. I just wondered when you'd say it out loud."

Daran looked at him then. A brief, sideways look — the kind that carried more than it showed. Then he looked back at the field.

The light was going. The first stars were not quite visible yet — that threshold moment where the sky had lost the day but not yet committed to the night. The treeline at the far edge of the field was deep shadow now.

"We should go in," Daran said. He didn't move.

"Probably," Aldric agreed. He didn't move either.

They stayed there another few minutes. The village continued its evening around them, indifferent and continuous. Somewhere behind them a window lit up — Maren, probably, starting the lamp in the front room. The smell of the field was different in the evening: cooler, damper, the green smell of it releasing into the air.

Daran pushed off the fence first. "Tomorrow," he said, without specifying what about tomorrow.

"Tomorrow," Aldric said.

They went in.

The letter sat on the Vane table where Aldric had left it. The village outside was quiet and continuous and exactly itself. The last star appeared above the treeline at the edge of the field, and then another, and the night came down over Ashford the same way it always had, over everything unchanged, over a table with a folded letter on it that had made something real that used to be a direction.

Aldric went to sleep.

He did not dream.
