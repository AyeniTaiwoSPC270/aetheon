# Chapter 25: What Aldric Does About It

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"Absolutely not," Nessa said, before Aldric had finished the second sentence of asking. "Not because I don't want to. Because it wouldn't work, and I'd rather tell you that now than let you find out in four days with less time left than you started with."

They were in the corner of the library nobody used for studying, which was where Nessa had suggested they talk once Aldric had made clear this wasn't a question about circuit diagrams. She had listened to the whole thing without interrupting, which he'd come to recognize as her particular courtesy — she let a problem finish being a problem before she started dismantling it.

"Your mother's on the Council," Aldric said. "A word from House Croft about how a review's being scheduled—"

"Would take longer than four days to write, longer than that to be answered, and when it finally landed it would look exactly like what it was — one noble house leaning on a process to protect a commoner, which is a story certain people on that Council have been waiting years to tell about my family specifically." She said it without heat, the way she said most things, a fact laid flat on the table between them. "My mother is a pragmatist. She votes with whoever's winning. If I ask her to spend real capital defending your friend's lamp-bracket log, the very first thing she'll ask is what it costs her, and the honest answer is more than she'll want to pay for six days and two weeks of evening duty. I'm not saying that because I don't care. I'm saying it because you'd be trading a slow problem for a slower one, and you don't have six days to spend finding that out."

"That's bleak."

"That's Thursday," Nessa said, "in my family," and it took Aldric a moment to realize that had been, in its own dry way, a joke — offered flatly enough that he almost missed it entirely, which he suspected was exactly how she preferred them landed.

Doven was the second door he tried, less because he expected it to open and more because it was the kind of door he felt obligated to knock on before admitting he'd have to go somewhere colder. He found the instructor after hours, chalk on both hands as usual, halfway through erasing a diagram nobody but him had asked him to erase.

"I could ask," Doven said, when Aldric had laid it out — the notice, the six days now four, the shape of what he suspected without saying Crest's name aloud, because some instinct told him not to say it aloud even here. "I could absolutely ask. I like Rynn. Anyone who apologizes that loudly has clearly got a good heart working overtime." He set the chalk down, and something in his easy face went, for a moment, genuinely regretful. "But you're not really asking me to ask, are you. You're asking me to make it faculty business. And the second it's faculty business, it's not quiet anymore — it's a formal inquiry, with formal questions, and formal questions have this unfortunate habit of turning around and asking about the person who raised them, not just the person they were raised for."

Aldric said nothing, because there was nothing to say that wouldn't have confirmed exactly what Doven had just, gently, declined to make him confirm out loud.

"I'm not telling you not to fight for him," Doven said. "I'm telling you that some fights get worse the more people are standing in the room when they happen. You strike me as someone who already knows that better than most people your age ought to."

So that was two doors, and Aldric left the second one with four days become three and the uncomfortable, clarifying knowledge that every path running through someone else's authority ran, eventually, back toward his own — toward Bavel's watching, Maret's notebook, the burst he still had no name for and had no intention of explaining to a Disciplinary Review Board that had never once asked him a question he could safely answer. He couldn't fight this with status, because he had none that wasn't borrowed and none he could afford to spend. He couldn't fight it with faculty, because faculty asked questions in both directions. Which left, when he actually sat with it — alone, that evening, turning the problem the way Solen turned a folded page until its corners squared — the one lever he'd been avoiding because it required doing something he didn't especially want to become good at.

He thought about Crest the way he'd once thought about a flawed circuit diagram: not what it looked like from the front, where all the confident lines were drawn, but where it would actually fail if you found the thin place and leaned on it. And he had, without ever intending to collect it, gathered more about that thin place than he'd realized. A slow smile that came only when a plan worked, never when a fight was fair. A refusal, twice now, of a friend's offered hand, because being seen needing help was apparently a worse injury than the one that put him on the ground. A father on the Council who opposed concessions on principle and had, by every account Aldric had ever half-overheard, never once let a result be good enough on its own to need no further explaining.

*Underneath the contempt is a student who is terrified of not being good enough for the name he was born into.* Nobody had told Aldric that in so many words. He'd built it the way he built most things — by watching, filing, saying nothing, until the shape of a person assembled itself whether he wanted it to or not.

He found Crest alone the next evening, which took less effort than he'd expected, because Crest — like Aldric — apparently preferred to do his real thinking somewhere nobody else could watch him do it. The east stair landing, past curfew for anyone official but early enough that no warden would trouble two first-years standing near a window. Aldric had chosen the spot on purpose. No witnesses. Nothing for either of them to perform for.

Crest's expression, on seeing him, went through three separate calculations in under a second — surprise, suspicion, and then the slow, practiced settling into contempt, the register he wore the way other people wore a coat. "Come to appeal, Vane? I hear Doven's already told you how well that goes."

"I'm not here to appeal anything," Aldric said. "I'm here to tell you what happens if the review goes forward."

"I know what happens. That's rather the point."

"Not to Rynn. To you." Aldric kept his voice flat, unhurried, the register he used for explaining a diagram's failure point to a room that expected him to bluff. "Right now, this is invisible. Nobody knows your name is anywhere near it. A steward forgot to sign a page, a junior forgot to cover for him, paperwork did what paperwork does. That's the story, as far as anyone official is concerned. Clean. Untraceable. Very good work, honestly — I mean that."

Something flickered across Crest's face at *very good work*, quick and involuntary, gone before he could smooth it away. Aldric noted it and kept going.

"But it doesn't end quiet. It ends in a room, with a Board, and a steward who already went to the office once to correct the record and will absolutely go a second time, in person, under oath if it comes to that, because he's an honest man who's annoyed someone's making him repeat himself. And when he does, somebody on that Board is going to ask the obvious question — not whether the entry was signed, that's settled, but why a closed matter got reopened at all, and reopened fast enough to beat his correction to the office. That's not a hard question to answer. It just has an answer nobody's going to like."

"You have no proof of anything," Crest said, but the flat certainty from the yard was gone from it now, replaced by something more careful.

"I don't need proof. I need the question asked in front of people. You know how this academy works better than I do — you've had longer to learn it. A rumor that a Halvon spent a week quietly maneuvering a records office against a commoner's lamp-bracket duty doesn't need to be proven. It just needs to exist, in the right rooms, told by people who weren't even trying to hurt you, just repeating what they overheard because it was a good story." Aldric let that sit for a second before he added the part he'd actually come to say. "I keep thinking about what that story sounds like, told badly, at your own table. Not *he beat someone in front of the whole yard.* Not even *he lost, but it was close.* Just — *he found a sick man's forgotten signature and used it against a boy who can't afford a lawyer.* That's not a story about strength. Everyone who hears it is going to know exactly what it's a story about instead."

Crest didn't answer for a long moment. When he did, his voice had lost the practiced edge entirely, and what was underneath it was younger than Aldric had ever heard it before, and angrier for being caught being young. "You don't know anything about what my table sounds like."

"I don't need to," Aldric said. "I only need to know what you're afraid it sounds like. I think you already told me, back in the yard, the first week. *The exception is never a bigger fire. It's a smaller one, in exactly the right place.*" He watched that land — watched Crest hear his own words handed back to him, stripped of whatever cleverness they'd carried the first time. "You built a very small fire, Halvon. Precise. Deniable. I'm telling you, as one favor, unpaid — it's currently burning in exactly the wrong place. Put it out before someone else notices where you lit it."

He didn't wait for an answer. There wasn't going to be one worth hearing, not tonight, not standing on a stair landing with his pride still recalculating in real time — and Aldric had learned, from a lifetime of knowing which fights mattered, that the last word in a conversation like this one was never actually the important part. The important part was what happened after, alone, when nobody was left to perform contempt for.

He got his answer two days later, in the common room, secondhand and exactly the way he'd designed it to arrive — not from Crest, and not to him. Nessa mentioned it first, between bites of something she was pretending was breakfast, in the mildly surprised tone of someone reporting mild weather. Apparently Crest Halvon had gone to the administrative office himself, that morning, and personally cleared up an "unfortunate clerical confusion" regarding a certain steward's conduct log — had, in fact, been quite gracious about it, in front of two separate witnesses, mentioning that he'd "looked into the matter further" and found the steward entirely credible, and didn't the Academy owe men like that more patience than it generally gave.

"Very noble of him," Nessa said, not quite looking at Aldric as she said it, in the specific way of someone who suspected there was a version of this story she wasn't being told and had decided, for now, not to ask for it. "Everyone's saying how decent it was. Halvon, showing mercy. It's going to do wonders for his standing with the second years."

Rynn's notice arrived that afternoon, one line, unsigned: matter resolved, record amended, duty confirmed served. He read it four times in front of Aldric, checking for a trick that wasn't there, and when he finally believed it he didn't ask how. He just put a hand on Aldric's shoulder, briefly, hard, and said nothing at all — which from Rynn, who apologized too loudly for everything, was its own kind of very loud.

Aldric never told him what four days had actually cost, or what he'd said on a dark stair landing to make a very small fire go out somewhere nobody else would ever see it burning. Somewhere in the yard tomorrow, Crest would be receiving quiet compliments on his grace from students who had no idea what they were praising him for, and would have to decide, every single time, whether to let himself believe the version everyone else already had.

Solen, across the table, said nothing at all — just looked at Aldric for exactly as long as it took to confirm something he'd apparently already half-suspected, and went back to his tea without a word.

Only two people in the whole academy would ever know whose idea the mercy had actually been. Neither of them was talking. Aldric intended to make sure it stayed that way.
