# Chapter 47: The Look

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


A guard Aldric didn't recognize caught him by the shoulder before he'd made it ten feet from the dais. "Staff and students, toward the east cloister. That's not a request."

"I'm looking for two people."

"Everyone's looking for two people. Move."

He moved, because arguing with a man holding a spear at a professional angle wasn't a habit worth starting tonight, and because Rynn's voice — pitched somewhere between panic and pure relief — found him almost immediately from the direction he'd been told to walk in anyway.

"You're not dead," Rynn said, arriving at a run and stopping short of actually touching him, as though checking first. "I want that on record before I say anything else. You're not dead, and I've decided that's the headline."

"I'm not dead."

"Good. Great. That's — Solen, he's not dead—"

"I can see that," Solen said, a step behind, already scanning Aldric head to foot with the specific thoroughness of a man counting for injuries rather than reassurance. "You're not hurt."

"I'm not hurt."

"You're shaking again," Solen said, flatly, in the tone of a man reading a fact off an instrument.

"I've been shaking most of the night. It's becoming a personality trait."

Rynn made a small, strangled noise that wasn't quite a laugh. "Somebody attacked Principal Solm. At the *tournament.* At the *closing ceremony.* I keep saying it and it keeps not making sense as a sentence."

"It didn't make much sense while it was happening either."

That, at least, was true, and saying it out loud didn't make it feel any truer. The courtyard around them had the specific, wrong quiet of a place that had been loud a minute ago — overturned tables, a scatter of glass nobody had swept yet, faculty moving in pairs with the clipped economy of people who had a job and weren't going to discuss it with anyone standing nearby. Aldric had wanted, walking away from Solm, exactly one thing: to understand what had just happened. He was beginning to suspect that wanting it and getting it were going to be two very different projects tonight.

He tried the most obvious source first. Bavel was crossing the dais with a length of torn fabric wound around one forearm, moving like a man who intended to keep moving until someone made him stop.

"Instructor."

"Vane." Bavel didn't slow down. "You're supposed to be with your friends."

"I found them. I have a question."

"I have several answers, and none of them are for you." He said it without cruelty — the same flat economy he gave everything — and kept walking, which was somehow worse than if he'd snapped it.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one on offer tonight." Bavel paused, for exactly as long as it took to look at Aldric properly, and something in his face shifted a fraction — not warmth, but its nearest professional cousin. "You did well tonight. Better than well. I'd tell you to be proud of it, except I don't think you've got room left over for pride right now, and I don't blame you." He was already moving again before he finished the sentence. "Go find somewhere to sit down. That's an order, not a suggestion."

"Who were they?"

Bavel didn't answer that at all. He simply kept walking, and the distance did the answering for him.

"That went well," Solen observed.

"He said I did well tonight."

"He didn't say who tried to kill the Principal in front of half the kingdom, though," Solen said, "which was rather the question you asked."

"I noticed."

Rynn, hovering somewhere between the two of them with the specific misery of a man who wanted very badly to be useful and had no idea how, tried a junior administrator next — one of the same functionaries who'd walked Rynn to his own hearing back in his first term, still recognizable by the particular way he clutched his ledger like it might be called as a witness. He got three sentences of hedged, official-sounding nothing and a firm suggestion that the courtyard was for faculty and emergency personnel only, delivered with an apologetic wince that suggested he genuinely wished he had something better to give them.

Nessa Croft, of all people, was the one who finally told him something true, though she clearly hadn't meant to. She found them near the overturned refreshment tables, arms folded, still in her placement sash from the ceremony, watching the faculty work with the flat, assessing look she gave everything that wasn't a spell diagram. "My mother's already sent a runner," she said, to no one in particular, though she was looking at Aldric when she said it. "She wants to know whether the Council was the target or the Principal specifically. Nobody's told her either, which she's taking personally, because people generally do tell her things."

"What do you think?" Aldric asked.

"I think if it were the Council in general, they'd have hit the box, not the dais." She said it the way she said everything — plainly, without performance, already turning to go before she'd fully finished the thought. "Somebody wanted him. Just him. Draw your own conclusions about what that means for the rest of us." She left before Aldric could ask her to draw them for him instead.

"They're not going to tell us anything," Solen said, once she'd gone. "Not tonight. Possibly not ever, if the shape of Solm's own face when he sent you away is any indication."

"He said 'later.'"

"People say 'later' about a great many things they have no intention of ever discussing." Solen's voice was flat, but something in it had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever he'd already worked something out and wasn't sure Aldric would want to hear it yet. "I don't think 'later' means next week."

"You think it means never."

"I think it means whenever somebody with more authority than either of us decides it's convenient, which is a different thing from never, but isn't especially comforting either."

Rynn, who had been quiet for slightly longer than his usual tolerance allowed, finally said the thing he'd clearly been holding since the moment he found them. "I'M SORRY, can we talk about the part where you apparently ran *at* the attack? Because everyone keeps saying it like it's a footnote and I don't think it's a footnote. I think it's the headline underneath the first headline."

"There wasn't time to plan something more sensible."

"That's not — Aldric, that's not a plan, that's a description of the problem."

"I'm aware it's not a plan," Aldric said. "I didn't have one. I had a gap that needed to not be a gap, and I was closer to it than anyone else."

"You could've died."

"I didn't."

"That's not the same as *couldn't have.*" Rynn's volume had climbed without his apparently noticing, the way it always did when he was frightened and trying very hard to sound merely annoyed instead. "You've got to stop doing the thing where something terrifying happens to you and you file it under 'Tuesday.'"

"It wasn't Tuesday."

"You know what I mean."

He did know what Rynn meant, and he didn't have an answer for it that wouldn't have sounded, out loud, like an admission he wasn't ready to make even to himself — that some part of him had moved toward Solm the way his hand had once moved toward Josse on a kitchen floor a lifetime ago, before any part of his mind had finished deciding it was a good idea. He filed that too, the way he filed everything he didn't have room to look at properly, and let Rynn's noise do the work of ending the conversation instead.

It struck him, standing there amid the wreckage of a night that had started with a tournament and a medal and a King's handshake, that he hadn't had one uninterrupted minute yet to feel any of that properly either. He'd won the entire thing. He'd stood in front of the King of Valdenmere and a full arena and made a stranger's dismissal look foolish, exactly as he'd set out to. It should have felt like something. Instead it sat somewhere very far back in his chest, small and strange and entirely eclipsed, the way a candle looks pointless the moment someone lights a house on fire next to it.

"You won today," Solen said, quietly, as though he'd read the shape of the thought off Aldric's face. "I don't think anybody's going to get around to mentioning that again tonight. I thought someone should."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. I'm only saying it because it's true, and because it's the kind of true thing that gets buried under a more urgent true thing and never dug back out." A pause, the smallest concession to something almost gentle. "Somebody should remember it happened, even if tonight isn't the night for celebrating it."

"I'll add it to my own list."

"The list has always been long," Solen said, with the specific, tired weight of a man quoting himself back at his own week. "You're just finally paying attention to how long."

The faculty were working the courtyard in a widening pattern now, faculty and a handful of city watch moving out past the dais toward the treeline that marked the edge of the grounds, torches bobbing in a loose, purposeful line. Somewhere out there, Aldric understood without being told, were the three who'd gotten away — and the two who hadn't, wherever they'd been taken, presumably having a considerably worse night than anyone standing in this courtyard.

He watched the search line for longer than he meant to, the torches moving in and out of the treeline's shadow, and it was there, at the very edge of where the light still reached, that he saw the shape detach itself from the dark.

It wasn't running, not exactly — it moved the way someone moved when running would draw more attention than walking quickly, unhurried in a way that looked deliberately unhurried, which was its own kind of tell. One of the three. Close enough, for one suspended second, that Aldric could make out the ordinary lines of a catering uniform gone wrong in the dark, and far enough that no one else in the courtyard seemed to have noticed him at all.

The figure stopped.

It shouldn't have. Every part of tonight had been built around not stopping, not being seen, getting out before the search line closed the gap — and for one full, deliberate second, the figure stopped anyway, turned, and looked directly across the width of the courtyard.

Not at the dais. Not at the faculty, not at the torches, not at the wreckage of a celebration that had gone wrong an hour into its own applause.

At Aldric.

He felt it the way he felt very few things — a cold, specific certainty that arrived ahead of any thought about it, the same instinct that had once told him a Greyback was already inside the walls before anyone had said the word aloud. This wasn't a stranger's idle curiosity, the way the man in expensive grey had looked at him a week ago, or even the unhurried, unplaceable interest of the watcher in the Council tier. This was recognition. Whoever this was, wearing a stolen uniform and running for his life through the dark, had found the one face in a courtyard full of faces that he'd apparently come here already knowing, and had spent one full second of his own narrowing window to make sure Aldric knew it too.

Then he was gone — back into the treeline, swallowed by shadow and distance, and a shout went up somewhere along the search line a half-second later that suggested someone else had finally caught the movement, too late to matter.

Aldric stood very still, the noise of the courtyard behind him going on exactly as it had, entirely unaware that anything had just happened at all.

"What," Solen said, low, arriving at his shoulder without Aldric having heard him approach, "did you just see?"

"I don't know yet."

"You've got the face," Solen said. "The very specific one."

"I know."

He kept his eyes on the treeline a moment longer, though there was nothing left there to see — no shape, no movement, nothing but ordinary dark closing back over ordinary trees, exactly as if the last second hadn't happened at all. But it had happened, and holding a look that long, in the middle of fleeing for your life, wasn't an accident. It wasn't fear, and it wasn't recognition of Solm's own student body in some general, incidental way.

It was a message. He didn't have the rest of the sentence yet. He understood, standing there with the search line's torches receding into the trees, that he wasn't going to get it tonight — and that whatever it turned out to say, it had already been addressed, specifically and deliberately, to him.
