# Chapter 21: Rynn and Solen

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

"Forty-one," Rynn announces, from the top of the east stair, with the specific pride of a man who has just finished the worst job of his life and intends to be congratulated for it. "Forty-one brackets. Two weeks. I have polished more brass than the entire kingdom's cavalry."

"Forty-three," Solen says, from three steps below, not looking up from the paper he is folding into careful quarters. "You've missed the two behind the pillar again. Both times. It's almost impressive."

"Forty-three, then. Forty-three brackets," Rynn concedes, "is not a number that requires congratulation—"

"It requires," Solen finishes for him, "that you never hit anyone in this building again."

"I would like it noted," Rynn says, "that I do not regret it."

"Nobody asked you to regret it," Aldric says. "We asked you to survive it. There's a difference. One of them gets you free tonight. The other one gets you a plaque."

Rynn descends the last few steps two at a time, buoyant in the way of someone who has been counting down a sentence for fourteen days and can, as of the bell that will ring at midnight, finally stop. "Tonight," he says, with the weight of a declaration, "is a celebration."

"Tonight," Solen says, "is a Tuesday."

"Tonight is the first Tuesday in two weeks I haven't spent talking to a bracket, and I intend to mark the occasion." Rynn spreads his arms to indicate the general scale of the marking he has in mind, which appears, from the gesture alone, to be considerable. "We should do something. Something with a bit of — " he searches for the word — "consequence."

Aldric, who has spent two weeks quietly making sure Rynn's evenings produced no further consequences of any kind, feels the specific dread of a man watching a fire he only just finished putting out reach for a second match. "No."

"You haven't heard the idea."

"I don't need to hear the idea. I can see your face. Your face is already doing the idea."

It is Crest, as it happens, who supplies the actual idea, though not on purpose. He passes them at the base of the stair with Milo a half-step behind and Seya not bothering to hide that she's watching for a reaction, and says, without breaking stride, "Last night, is it? Enjoy it. I hear the honest work suits you." He says *honest work* the way other people say a word they've decided is beneath them, and keeps walking before anyone can answer, which is, Aldric has noticed, becoming Crest's preferred exit whenever he isn't certain the answer will go his way.

Rynn's face does the idea harder.

"Did you hear that," he says. "*Enjoy it.* Like I've been — like this was a *holiday.*"

"It was two weeks of lamp brackets," Solen says. "It was, technically, the opposite of a holiday."

"I want to do something," Rynn says, rounding on both of them with the specific heat of a man who has just been handed a perfectly good grievance and intends to spend it immediately, "that makes it very clear I am not embarrassed. Something with consequence."

"The word you keep using," Aldric says, "is doing a great deal of unpaid labour. What, specifically, is the consequence."

"You could also," Solen adds, not looking up, "consider that the office is already disposed to believe the worst of you specifically. Any celebration involving a bucket will be assumed, correctly, to be yours before anyone has finished asking whose it was."

"That's slander."

"It's a documented pattern. I could show you the ledger."

"Nobody wants to see the ledger, Solen."

"I want to see the ledger," Aldric says. "Purely for research."

Rynn's plan, once extracted, involves the training yard, a bucket, several feet of twine, and enough confidence in his own aim that Aldric can tell — purely from the shape of the explanation — that Solen has already run the numbers and arrived at a mark, a hearing, or both. Solen, in fact, has his arms folded before Rynn finishes the sentence, which is as close to visible alarm as Aldric has ever seen him produce without a stopwatch involved.

"That plan," Solen says, "ends with your name back on that ledger inside a week. I've done the arithmetic. You have not."

"It's a victory lap."

"It's a hearing. This time an actual one, since you'd be doing it on purpose and sober, which the office weighs rather differently than doing it once, badly, in anger." Solen unfolds his page, refolds it the other way, a habit Aldric has come to recognise as Solen buying himself a few seconds to choose the least inflammatory version of a true sentence. "You have two weeks of good behaviour on the record now. That's worth more than a bucket."

"It doesn't *feel* like it's worth more than a bucket."

"Most correct decisions don't," Aldric says. "That's rather the design flaw in them."

Rynn looks between the two of them, betrayed on a scale usually reserved for actual crimes, and says, with real feeling, "I have spent two weeks being *responsible.* I would like, for one evening, to not be."

"You could," Aldric offers, "be irresponsible somewhere that doesn't end in a ledger."

"Such as."

"Such as here. With us. Doing nothing whatsoever that a hearing could describe." Aldric sits down on the step Rynn just vacated, which he intends as a small, undramatic gesture and which Rynn, predictably, receives as an occasion. "We could simply be three people, sitting on a stair, being deeply irresponsible about absolutely nothing."

"That's not a celebration. That's just — the three of us. Existing."

"I'm told," Aldric says, "that some people call that a group."

It lands harder than he means it to. Rynn stops mid-protest, something shifting behind his eyes that has nothing to do with buckets, and even Solen looks up from his page for a fraction of a second longer than the sentence strictly requires. Aldric had meant it as a joke — a small, deflating one, the kind he uses to let air out of a moment before it becomes something he'd have to mean on purpose — and it has, without his permission, become something closer to true. Three people who did not choose each other on any particular day, who have exchanged perhaps two hundred words apiece and a great deal of proximity, sitting on a stair at an hour nobody made them share, none of them elsewhere.

"A group," Rynn repeats, testing the word the way he tests everything, out loud and at volume. "Huh." He sits down on the step below Aldric's, uninvited and entirely certain of his welcome, which turns out to be accurate. "I could live with a group."

"I would prefer," Solen says, sitting on Rynn's other side with the careful economy of someone who has decided a thing and is now merely completing the paperwork of it, "that this group not generate any further ledger entries."

"No promises," Rynn says.

"That was not a real request for promises. That was a hope, stated aloud, which I already know will not survive contact with you."

They sit like that for a while, saying very little, in the specific comfortable silence of people who have run out of things that need to be argued about tonight, and it is Solen — of course it is Solen — who breaks it first, not with a joke but with a fact, delivered in the same flat register he uses for everything from lamp brackets to shell-hold times.

"Second-year," he says. "Third window along, east dormitory face. He's been there eleven minutes."

Aldric follows the angle of Solen's gaze without moving his head, a habit he's picked up without noticing he was picking it up. There is, indeed, a figure at a lit third-floor window across the courtyard — too far to make out a face, close enough to make out that the figure is not doing anything, simply standing, in the specific stillness of someone who has stopped pretending to be doing something else.

"Probably reading," Rynn says, unbothered. "People read near windows. It's a whole thing people do."

"He isn't watching Rynn," Solen says, which is not the sentence anyone expected, and lands with considerably more weight than the observation about the window did on its own. "He was watching Rynn for the first four minutes, while you were shouting about buckets. He's been watching Vane for the other seven."

Rynn stops being unbothered. Aldric does not say anything at all for several seconds, because there is, genuinely, nothing useful to say to a fact like that — a second-year he has never spoken to, watching him from a window, for reasons nobody in this group can begin to guess at.

"Do you know who he is," Aldric asks.

"Not by name. I know the collar — second-year, and not a house I've had reason to learn." Solen says this with the specific dissatisfaction of a man reporting an incomplete answer, which Aldric is beginning to understand is nearly as uncomfortable for Solen as not answering at all. "I'll have a name by the week's end. I don't like not having one."

"Add it to the list," Aldric says, and means it as a joke about Maret, and Doven's warmth, and Bavel's *worse*, and it comes out sounding less like a joke than he intended and more like an actual inventory, which — he realises, saying it — it rather is.

The window goes dark a minute later, all at once, the way a light goes out when someone decides, deliberately, that they are finished being looked at rather than that they are finished with whatever they were doing. Nobody says anything about that either. There isn't, really, anything to say.

It's Solen, once the stair has gone quiet enough that his voice barely needs to work to fill it, who delivers the evening's second piece of news, in exactly the tone he'd use to report a shell-hold time.

"I've decided against the tournament," he says. "I'm taking the alternative assessment track instead."

Rynn sits up so fast he nearly loses his footing on the step. "You're *what.* Solen. You'd win half your bracket without trying."

"Probably," Solen agrees, entirely without false modesty, which is somehow more convincing than modesty would have been. "It counts the same on the record either way, and nobody hits me during a written assessment. I find that arrangement suits me."

"That's not a reason. That's a — that's barely even a sentence."

"It's a perfectly good sentence. It has a subject and a verb and everything a sentence requires." Solen folds his page one final time, into a shape too small to unfold again without effort, and puts it away. "I've made the decision. I'd rather not make it three times over the course of the evening."

Aldric watches him say it — watches the particular economy of it, the sentence built to be complete and unarguable and entirely lacking in the one thing Aldric has come, over fifteen weeks, to expect from Solen whenever he explains himself: the sense that he has told you the true reason and not merely a reason that will hold up under questioning. It is a good answer. It is, Aldric thinks, a little too good, constructed with the specific care of something built in advance rather than arrived at on the stair just now.

He does not ask. Some part of him — the part that reads a hundred small things before anyone else in the room has noticed one — understands that Solen has just closed a door with exactly enough courtesy that opening it again would cost more than either of them currently has to spend.

"Suit yourself," Rynn says, deflated in a way that has nothing to do with buckets or ledgers. "I was looking forward to watching you make somebody's afternoon miserable."

"I still might," Solen says. "Just not somewhere with a bracket attached to it."

The midnight bell goes not long after, somewhere above them, and Rynn stands up like a man being released from something with actual weight to it, stretching until his shoulders crack, grinning in the dark at nobody in particular.

"Free man," he announces, to the empty courtyard, the dark window, the two people who are apparently, as of tonight, officially a group whether any of them planned it or not. "Free man, first day. Somebody should mark the occasion."

"We did," Aldric says. "We sat on a stair and were deeply irresponsible about absolutely nothing. Try to contain your excitement."

"I'm containing it beautifully," Rynn says, and does not, in fact, contain it at all, the whole walk back.
