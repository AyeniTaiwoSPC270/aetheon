# Chapter 16: Bavel's Yard

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

Bavel read the drill order aloud, and Aldric's name came right after Crest's.

Either that was chance, or Instructor Bavel had a sense of humor. Aldric had watched the man through two mornings of stance drills and wall laps and had seen no evidence of one.

"Halvon, post six. Vane, seven." Bavel didn't look up from his slate. "Croft, eight. Solen, nine. Rynn, ten, and you'll stay on your feet or I'll have you carried."

"I'm fine," said Rynn.

He was not fine. He hung at Aldric's shoulder like a wet coat on a hook. Two weeks of evening lamp duty had sanded the edges off him, and he'd told Aldric over breakfast that he was sleeping "in the gaps between brackets."

"You've got that face," Aldric murmured.

"What face?"

"The one that says the post is going to be sorry."

"The post," Rynn said, with great dignity, "has no idea."

Beyond the gate, the yard was flagstone at the threshold and sand past it, with two long rows of timber posts standing in the sand like a very patient audience. Someone had chalked a white square on the face of every one.

Bavel didn't make a speech. That was the first thing Aldric had learned about him. He walked to the nearest post, pointed one hand at it, and said, "Watch."

The air cracked.

A pale blue-white flash, a flat dry snap like a plank breaking over a knee, and the post rocked on its base. When the dust settled, the chalk square had a bare disc knocked out of its middle. It was about the size of a fist.

"That's a bolt," Bavel said. "It's a fist. It doesn't burn, it doesn't freeze, and it doesn't cut. If anyone tells you it's a fireball with the fire taken out, they're selling something. Point. Push. Don't throw."

A boy in the second row raised a hand. "What's the difference between push and throw?"

"You'll find out."

Somebody laughed and then stopped, as though the laugh had been confiscated.

"Pairs go up together and stay up until both have fired three times," said Bavel. "Eleven seconds between casts, not ten. Your hand needs the time even if you don't. Best print counts. Your print goes on the board by the gate, in order, and everyone reads the board. Nobody argues with the board." He looked down the line. "Some of you have been told your whole lives where you stand. The board doesn't know that."

Three places down, Crest's jaw did something small and tight.

Aldric looked at the post and did the sums.

The board was the problem.

He'd spent his life being looked past. It was a skill, and he was good at it. Too low, and Bavel would take an interest in the boy who couldn't do what any village kid with one battered book ought to manage. Too high, and he thought of a small notebook, and a pen that had stopped at his desk for exactly four seconds.

Middle, then. The exact, dull, forgettable middle.

He had never aimed anything at anything. What had happened in Ashford hadn't been aimed. He rolled his shoulders, told himself it was a fist, and decided he'd be a slightly disappointing one.

---

"Halvon. Vane. Up."

Crest went first, left post before right, as the order said.

He raised his hand with a flourish, like a man greeting a crowd. The post stood three paces off. The bolt left his fingers a fist and arrived as a slap, a wide, weak splash of light that smeared chalk across half the post and knocked nothing loose at all.

The silence lasted about a second.

"Post's damp," said Crest.

"It's chalk," said Bavel. "It doesn't get damp. Again."

The second was better and still wide. On the third, Crest shoved with his whole body, and the print came out the size of a dinner plate. Any of his classmates would have been proud of it. Crest looked at it as if it had said something about his mother.

Then he turned his head and looked at Aldric.

It wasn't anger, exactly. It was the look of someone who has already decided how a thing will go and is waiting for it to start. Since the pamphlet business, Crest had stopped talking to him and started watching instead.

Aldric preferred the talking.

"Vane," said Bavel.

"Fist," hissed Rynn, from post ten. "You've got it. Fist, fist, fist."

"Are you coaching me or praying?" Aldric asked.

"*Both,*" said Rynn.

Aldric raised his hand.

He'd planned it. He would let the hand drop half an inch. He would let the thread go thick and lazy, the way Rynn's did, and push. Sloppy, but not stupid.

He pushed.

Pale blue-white. A flat, dry crack.

In the middle of the chalk square was a mark the size of a thumb. Clean-edged. Neat as a stamp.

Nobody said anything. Somewhere down the line, someone let out a breath they'd been saving.

"That's not supposed to do that," said Rynn.

"It's a fist," Aldric said. "It hit the post."

"It hit the post *politely,*" Rynn said.

"Tighter than the demonstration," said Solen from post nine, without looking up from his own hand. "By a margin."

Aldric looked at the print. Bavel's had been the size of a fist and had rocked the post on its base. This one hadn't even shivered it. It was the difference between a slap and a signature.

He had tried to be sloppy and had arrived, from the wrong direction, at good.

That was, he supposed, a talent.

"Two more, Vane," said Bavel. His face gave away nothing whatsoever.

---

Crest had crossed the sand. Nobody had called him.

He stood at post seven with his hands behind his back like a man at a museum, studying the print with great courtesy.

"Lucky," he said.

"Probably," said Aldric.

"Beginner's luck is very common. In the self-taught." He said the word gently, as if handling something that might stain. "You wouldn't know the difference." He leaned in. "It's so small. It's almost, ah, delicate."

"I'm told it's fragile," Aldric agreed. "Luck usually is."

Crest lifted one finger toward the chalk.

"Bavel's already marked it," said Solen mildly, not turning around.

He had. Aldric hadn't seen it happen. Bavel stood forty feet away with the slate against his chest, and the chalk in his other hand hadn't stopped moving.

"Halvon," Bavel said, without raising his voice. "Post."

Crest's hand dropped. He went back to six. He walked like a man who'd won an argument nobody else had noticed, which was a lie, and the whole line knew it.

At post eight, Nessa Croft glanced at Aldric's print, then at her own, then at Aldric again. Her expression was pleasant and unbothered, and it was exactly the expression of someone taking notes.

Aldric fired twice more.

He did his level best.

The second, he flinched on purpose, jerking his hand like a man swatting a wasp. He let his stance sag. He thought about lamp brackets, about pamphlets, about anything at all that might make him lose the thread.

The print was the size of a thumb. Very slightly off-centre, in a generous sort of way.

The third, he fired with his eyes half closed, in a spirit of pure sabotage.

It landed in the same spot. Marginally neater.

*You can't,* he thought, with real dismay. *You can't un-learn it on request.* He had taught himself to hold a thread thin, in a village where the only teacher was one book. He'd assumed the habit was something he owned.

It turned out it owned him. It had come to the academy in his bag.

"I hate this," he said quietly.

"It's going badly?" Rynn asked hopefully.

"It's going *well.* In the wrong direction."

---

The rest of the line took its turns, pair by pair, and the yard filled with the flat dry cracks of a whole cohort learning to punch the air with their minds.

Pell's print came out small and tidy, and he huffed through his nose at it as if it had disappointed him personally. Lira Voss fired with the expression of someone who'd been asked to represent all elves to a target. Milo's bolt scattered, and Milo looked instantly, visibly relieved that Crest was busy looking elsewhere.

Rynn's first shot was so wide it clipped post eleven and took the chalk square clean off some poor boy's post.

"SORRY!"

"Volume," said Bavel.

"*Sorry,*" said Rynn, in a whisper that could still be heard from the gate.

By his second shot, Rynn's hands were shaking. He braced his wrist with his free hand like a man holding a hot cup. His bolt came out pale blue-white, like everyone's, with no fire in it at all. He looked faintly betrayed by this.

"Nine seconds between casts," Solen murmured. "You're rushing. Bavel said eleven."

"I'm tired, not rushing," Rynn muttered.

"You're tired *and* rushing. Eleven," said Solen.

Rynn glared at him. "Do you count *everything?*"

"Yes."

Rynn's third bolt was a lopsided fist. It was still a fist. He made a noise of quiet triumph, and Aldric found he was grinning, which was inconvenient, because he had a reputation to not-have.

Then his eyes drifted down the far row, and the grin slipped.

At the end of the far row, one shot at a time, a girl was firing.

She stood a little apart from the rest. The yard had arranged itself around her without anyone quite deciding to. There was a gap on either side of her post, the sort of gap that opens when people know who's standing there.

She raised a hand. A pale flash, a flat crack.

The chalk took one clean mark, dead centre.

A pause. Another mark, in exactly the same place. Then a third.

She never looked at her post. She never looked at the yard. She never looked at Aldric, and in a yard that had spent ten minutes looking at nothing else, that was almost a kind of statement.

"Lirien," Solen said quietly, following his eyes. "Eleven seconds between each. I checked twice."

"You timed her?" Aldric asked.

"I time everyone," said Solen. A beat. "She's the only one who hasn't noticed."

---

When it was over, Bavel wrote the board up on the wall by the gate, names in chalk, in order of print. The cohort drifted past it in a slow, pretending-not-to-look tide.

Aldric read it, because there was no way not to.

His name was at the top.

He read it again in case it was a different name. It was not.

One line under it, in the same squared chalk, was the name of the girl who hadn't looked at him. Crest was in the middle. Rynn was near the bottom, with a small tick beside him that might have been mercy.

"Near the bottom?" said Rynn, reading it. "That's — Solen, is that *good?*"

"You hit two posts," Solen said. "You're on the board."

"That's not the same as good!" Rynn protested.

"It's not the same as bad," said Solen.

Aldric had aimed for the middle and had, by some cruelty of arithmetic, hit the top. The middle would've been so much nicer. You could stand in the middle for a year without anyone turning you into anything.

Crest stood in front of the board a long moment. Seya, beside him, read it twice. Then, for the first time all morning, she looked at Aldric as if he'd changed shape. Milo stood a step behind them both and had the good sense to study his boots.

"Enjoy it," Crest said, quietly, to the board.

"I'm trying to be forgettable," said Aldric. "It isn't working."

A few paces off, Lirien read the board once. Top to bottom, at the pace of someone reading a receipt. Nothing moved in her face. Then she turned and left the yard the way she'd entered it, and the gap around her went with her.

Aldric couldn't tell what she'd concluded. He was annoyed at how much he wanted to know.

"Dismissed," said Bavel. "Vane. Seven."

Rynn mouthed *run.* Solen hesitated, squared nothing, and left. The cohort trickled out through the gate in twos and threes, talking in low voices, and did not look back with great effort.

Then the yard was empty, except for one post and two people.

---

Bavel walked to post seven. He took a rag from his belt and wiped the print away, slowly, with the flat of his hand, until the chalk square was blank. Then he re-chalked it himself, one careful stroke at a time, until it was white and new.

He stepped back.

"Again," he said.

"I've had my three," said Aldric.

"Again," said Bavel.

Aldric raised his hand.

"Worse," said Bavel.

Aldric's hand stopped in the air.

"Sir?" Aldric said.

"Worse," Bavel said again, quietly, the way you'd tell someone their shoelace was undone. "Go on."

He didn't explain. His face didn't do anything at all. He stood there with the rag in his hand and waited, and Aldric understood two things at once, and neither was comfortable.

The first was that Bavel had watched every single shot.

The second was that he had watched the two Aldric had tried to ruin.

It wasn't a pause, like the one in the lecture hall. It wasn't four seconds of a pen. It was a decision. Bavel had already made up his mind about something and was only waiting to see what Aldric would do next.

Aldric had tried to be worse twice already. He knew exactly how that went.

Bavel waited.

Aldric raised his hand.
