# Chapter 40: Crest's Match

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


Aldric wanted to want Crest to lose. He'd wanted it for two years, reliably, the way you want rain to stay away from a picnic — a small, standing preference that required no daily maintenance. Standing in the stands an hour before Crest's own quarterfinal, watching him warm up alone at the edge of the third ring with the same economical precision he brought to everything, Aldric discovered, with some irritation, that the preference wasn't holding.

"You've gone quiet," Solen observed.

"I'm thinking."

"About?"

"About how annoying it is that I don't currently want him to lose."

"That is annoying," Solen agreed, with the particular flatness that meant he found this observation genuinely interesting rather than merely conversational. "When did that start?"

"I don't know. Somewhere around the east stair, probably." Aldric didn't elaborate on which particular conversation on which particular east stair he meant, and Solen, mercifully, didn't ask — though the look he gave suggested he had a fairly good idea already, the way he had a fairly good idea about most things nobody had told him directly.

Milo and Seya had claimed a spot two rows down, loud in a way neither of them usually allowed themselves to be in public. "Two bolts, clean, that's all he needs," Seya was saying, to nobody in particular, the specific confidence of someone who had actually done the arithmetic rather than merely hoping. "He's better prepared than the seeding suggests."

"He's *always* better prepared than the seeding suggests," Milo said. "That's the entire Halvon strategy. Look unbothered. Be extremely bothered in private, at length, for weeks."

"That's not a strategy, that's just anxiety with good posture."

"I didn't say it wasn't both."

"You two do this for every match?" Rynn asked, dropping into the seat beside them without waiting to be invited, forearm still faintly pink from that morning's cooling charm. "Sit here running numbers like a betting shop?"

"We ran the numbers on your match too," Seya said. "They weren't kind."

"I don't want to hear it."

"You lasted four minutes against Torval, which is genuinely respectable, considering he's spent a year quietly being the single most dangerous person in the entire year and nobody noticed because he's polite about it."

"See, when *you* say it, it sounds like an insult wrapped in a compliment. When Aldric says something like that it just sounds like Aldric."

"That's because Aldric means it kindly and I mean it as information," Seya said, entirely unbothered, and returned her attention to the ring, where Crest had finished his warm-up and was standing very still, watching the entrance the crowd's noise indicated his opponent would come through.

Crest's opponent turned out to be a broad-shouldered noble from a House Aldric didn't recognize — Bryn Aldous, according to the murmur working through the stands, seeded well enough to be a genuine threat and confident enough, in the opening minute, to make that seeding look accurate. He came in hard and fast, three bolts inside the first thirty seconds, testing Crest's guard the way you test a door you expect to give.

It didn't give. Crest's shell went up clean, held clean, and something in the way he reset his footing after absorbing the third bolt looked less like the boy who'd once needed six weeks of engineered institutional cruelty to feel secure and more like someone who had simply decided, at some point since the east stair, that being good at something didn't require making someone else feel small first.

"He's not talking," Aldric said, quietly. "Usually he talks. Says something. Gets under a person before he needs to hit them."

"He hasn't said a word," Solen confirmed. "Not once. Not to Aldous, not to the crowd, not to himself."

It went on like that for three full minutes — clean, precise, almost quiet by tournament standards, the kind of match that would read as boring to anyone who didn't understand what they were watching. Aldous, frustrated by a silence he'd clearly expected to be filled with exactly the kind of courteous cruelty Crest was famous for, started pressing harder, sloppier, throwing bolts from angles that gave up accuracy for speed, clearly hoping a louder, faster Crest would emerge somewhere in the noise and hand him an opening the quiet one wasn't giving.

None came. Crest absorbed the fourth exchange the same way he'd absorbed the first three, unbothered, and something in his stillness seemed to unsettle Aldous more than any provocation could have — the specific unease of a man realizing, mid-fight, that the opponent he'd prepared for wasn't the one actually standing across from him.

The turn, when it came, wasn't dramatic. Crest simply stopped absorbing and started reading — catching the rhythm of Aldous's increasingly frantic pattern, sidestepping a bolt by exactly the width it needed, and answering with a single, precise shot that caught Aldous flat-footed mid-reset. It wasn't cruel. It wasn't showy. It was, Aldric realized with something that felt uncomfortably close to respect, simply *good* — the kind of win that didn't need an audience to feel earned, delivered by someone who, two years ago, would have needed the audience more than the win itself.

Aldous went down clean, yielded before the judge even finished the count, and Crest didn't gloat. He offered a hand down — the same gesture Torval had offered Rynn, unhurried, without spectacle — and said something too quiet for the stands to catch, that made Aldous laugh despite himself and take the hand.

Aldric found himself clapping before he'd decided to. It surprised him more than it should have.

"You're clapping," Rynn said, arriving beside him at exactly the wrong moment, as always. "For *Crest.* Did something happen to your head during your own match that I should know about?"

"I don't know," Aldric admitted. "He won it clean. That's — that's just true. I can clap for something being true."

"That's an extremely diplomatic way of saying you like him now."

"I don't like him. I've simply run out of specific reasons not to clap."

"That's the same thing with extra words."

"It really isn't."

"It really is, and you know it, and that's why you've gone that specific color you go when you've caught yourself doing something you didn't plan on."

"I haven't gone any color."

"You've gone the color. I've catalogued your colors extensively, this is a documented one." Rynn grinned, entirely too pleased with himself, and Solen, on Aldric's other side, made a small sound that might, on a more demonstrative man, have qualified as a laugh.

"He's not wrong," Solen said. "You have a face for this. It's subtle. Most people wouldn't catch it. I count things for a living, so I catch it."

"I hate that you two have a shared hobby now."

"We've had it for a while," Rynn said. "You're just the subject of it, so you notice it more."

Crest walked off the sand, and for a moment — just a moment, barely long enough to register before it was gone — his eyes moved across the crowd, past Milo's grin, past Seya's already-calculating nod, past the noble box where his own family sat with the particular stillness of people who expected excellence and rarely troubled themselves to reward it out loud, and found Aldric's face specifically, in a crowd of several hundred people, in less time than it should have taken to find anyone.

He didn't smile. He didn't gloat. He looked, for exactly as long as it takes to confirm something, and then looked away again, already turning toward Milo's approaching shoulder-clap and Seya's already-arriving commentary on his footwork in the third exchange.

Aldric stood very still for a moment, turning that over.

It wasn't triumph. It wasn't even acknowledgment, exactly — there'd been no nod, no gesture, nothing a stranger watching would have caught at all. It was something closer to checking. As though some part of Crest had wanted, specifically, for Aldric to have seen this — not Milo's approval, not Seya's arithmetic, not his own House's cool, expected pride. His.

"That was strange," Aldric said, mostly to himself.

"What was?" Rynn had already half-turned toward the refreshment stall two rows back, distracted by the promise of something involving honey that a second-year was selling out of a covered basket, and hadn't caught it at all.

"Nothing. Never mind."

Solen, though, had caught it. Solen caught most things. He didn't say anything about it directly — just filed a small, unhurried glance between Crest's retreating back and Aldric's face, the specific kind of look that meant a note had gone into some private ledger Aldric would probably never be shown, filed alongside the four-second pause and the eleven-second thread and every other thing Solen had ever counted without being asked.

Aldric didn't have a way to explain what he'd felt that wouldn't sound like more than it was, and wasn't entirely sure, standing there in the noise and the settling dust, that it *wasn't* more than it looked. Two years of a rivalry with a shape he'd understood perfectly well — Crest cruel, Aldric enduring, both of them keeping score in a ledger that never balanced — and now, apparently, something had shifted its terms without telling him, and he had no idea, yet, what the new ones were going to cost.

He didn't know what to do with that. So, for now, he did the only thing that had ever reliably worked for him: he filed it, carefully, in the place he kept things he wasn't ready to look at directly, and let Rynn's noise — now fully committed to the question of whether the honey stall took barter — carry him toward the next match instead.
