# Chapter 43: Essa

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


The problem with winning your semifinal in front of the entire kingdom, Aldric had discovered within about an hour of it happening, was that everyone considered this an invitation to talk to you.

"You're entitled to four," a boy he'd never met told him gravely, outside the dormitory, holding a wax-sealed form like it might combust. "Visitor tickets. For the final. I need to know now, because the seating chart closes at noon, and if you don't use all four, they go to the waiting list, and the waiting list is — Vane, the waiting list is *considerable.*"

"I don't have any family coming."

"None? Not even a—" The boy consulted the form again, as though it might contain an answer Aldric himself didn't have. "There's a line here for 'guardian or sponsoring household.' Do you have one of those?"

"Give the tickets to the waiting list."

"That feels like it should require more paperwork than it apparently does," the boy said, faintly betrayed, and left him alone at last.

He'd tried the common room next, which had emptied the moment he walked in — a courtesy, he understood, and one that felt, in practice, exactly like being watched by an empty room. He ended up, finally, in the one place at Greyveil that had never once in two years contained another living person when he wanted it to: the narrow gap behind the library's east wall, the cut-through nobody used for studying. Someone else had apparently had the same idea first.

Essa didn't look up when he arrived. She was sitting on the low wall with a book she wasn't reading, the specific stillness of someone who'd chosen this spot for the same reason he had and had simply gotten there first.

"Sorry," Aldric said. "I'll find somewhere else."

"You don't have to." She said it the way she said most things — quiet enough that he had to actually attend to catch it, not looking at him, a door held slightly open rather than an invitation through it. "There's room."

He hadn't spoken to her properly since Doven's class in his first term — six words exchanged, and Rynn had spent a week afterward speculating about what she'd meant by them, before losing interest the way Rynn lost interest in most mysteries that didn't resolve within a reasonable window. Aldric had never lost interest, exactly. He'd just filed it, the way he filed everything he couldn't do anything about.

He sat, keeping a careful distance he suspected she'd have found strange if he'd sat any closer.

"You won," Essa said, after a silence long enough that he'd stopped expecting her to say anything at all.

"I did."

"Everyone's talking about it. The whole year. Some of the second-years too." A pause, her eyes still on the unread page. "I don't think anyone's talked about a first-year match this much since—" She stopped herself, the sentence trailing into something she'd apparently decided not to finish.

"Since what?"

"Since you arrived." She turned a page she hadn't actually been reading. "Your first week. Everyone talked about that too. Differently. But a lot."

Aldric didn't need her to elaborate on what, specifically, everyone had talked about. He'd lived inside the specific quality of that attention since his second day at Greyveil — the white hair, the red eyes, the unplaceable bloodline that gave every noble student a puzzle to work at and no commoner student anything they could ask him about directly. He'd gotten good at not noticing it. He hadn't gotten good at it ever actually stopping.

"I remember what you said to me," he said. "That first term. Doven's class. 'Nobody here forgets a first mistake.' I've thought about it more than I probably should have, given it was six words and you wouldn't explain any of them."

"I know you have." Something in her voice shifted — not warmer, exactly, just more present, the way a person sounds when they've decided a conversation is finally worth actually having. "I watched you turn it over for weeks afterward. You're not subtle about thinking, even when you think you are."

"That's alarming information to receive this late."

"It's not a criticism. I do the same thing. I just do it quieter, because nobody's watching me do it." She closed the book, finally, without marking her place, which told him more clearly than anything she'd said so far that she hadn't actually been reading it at all. "I meant myself, that day. My first mistake. I answered a question in my first week here — an easy one, an obvious one — and got it wrong, out loud, in front of the entire room, and two years later people I've never spoken to still bring it up like it happened yesterday. That's what I meant. Nobody here forgets. Not really. Not ever."

"I'm sorry that happened to you."

"I didn't tell you so you'd be sorry." She looked at him directly for the first time since he'd arrived — the first time, he realized, she'd ever looked at him directly at all, in two years of overlapping the same rooms. "I told you because I don't think you understand yet that it happened to you too. On your first day. Before you'd said a single word wrong, before you'd done anything at all except exist in a room with that hair and those eyes and no story anyone could attach to either one. That was the mistake, as far as this place is concerned. Just showing up looking like a question nobody could answer."

Aldric sat with that longer than he meant to, the noise of the arena crowd two courtyards away arriving faint and indistinct, like weather happening to someone else.

"That's not a mistake," he said, finally. "That's just — how I look."

"It doesn't matter what it actually is. It matters what people decided it was, the first week, before you'd earned anything of your own to be known for. I got mine wrong on a diagram. You got yours wrong just by being born looking the way you do, in a room that had never had to make room for it before." She stood, unhurried, tucking the unread book under one arm. "I've watched them fail to stop wondering about it for two years now. I don't think winning tomorrow makes that go away. I think it just gives them a second thing to wonder about, on top of the first."

"That's not encouraging."

"I didn't come here to encourage you. I came here because it's quiet, and you found it too, and I thought — since we're apparently the only two people at this academy who understand what it's like to be defined by something that happened before we had any say in it — that you should hear it from someone who actually knows, instead of guessing at it alone for another two years."

She was already moving, unhurried, toward the gap in the wall that led back out into the noise, and Aldric found himself standing before he'd decided to, some old instinct insisting the conversation wasn't finished even though every signal from her said it was.

"Essa."

She paused, half-turned, the closest to fully facing him she'd allowed herself all conversation.

"I wanted to ask—"

"Nobody forgot yours," she said, before he'd finished the sentence, in the specific tone of someone who'd already known exactly what he was going to ask and had decided to answer it before he could waste breath asking it. "Whatever you're about to say. I already know the question. Nobody forgot yours. Neither did I."

And then she was gone, through the gap and back into the ordinary noise of an ordinary afternoon before an extraordinary final, leaving Aldric alone with a version of himself he hadn't examined this closely in two years, and a book she hadn't read a single page of.

He stayed a while longer, turning it over the way he turned everything over eventually, whether he wanted to or not. Two years of assuming the hair and the eyes were simply weather he had to walk through — background noise other people made about him that had nothing to do with anything he actually did — and it had apparently never once occurred to him that the noise itself was the story, as far as Greyveil was concerned, whether he ever gave it a better one or not.

It was a strange thing to be handed, an hour before the biggest match of his life, by someone who'd never once tried to be his friend and clearly hadn't started today either. Not comfort. Not a warning, the way Sable's had been, or a threat dressed as an observation, the way half of Crest's first year had been. Just a fact, delivered flatly by someone who'd apparently been carrying it, unshared, for exactly as long as he'd been carrying his own — two years of watching people build a story around him out of nothing but hair and eyes and silence, and never once mentioning that she recognized the shape of it, because recognizing it out loud would have cost her something she hadn't been willing to spend on a stranger.

She'd spent it today. He didn't know why now, specifically, out of all the days she could have chosen, and suspected — with the same instinct that had never once failed to notice the wrong thing at the right moment — that the timing wasn't an accident either. Tomorrow he'd stand in front of the entire kingdom again, and whatever he did there, win or lose, would only ever be the second thing people remembered about him. The first thing had already happened, two years ago, before he'd said a word.

He didn't have an answer for that. He filed it, the way he filed everything, and told himself there'd be time to look at it properly once the final was behind him.

There wasn't, in the end. There wasn't time for a great many things he'd told himself he'd get to eventually.
