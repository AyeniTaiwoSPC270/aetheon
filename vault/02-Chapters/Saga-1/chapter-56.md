# Chapter 56: The Seat With No Name

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"You're late," said the clerk.

"The notice said by the end of the week."

"The notice said *by the end of the week*," the clerk agreed, in the voice of a man who had been waiting to be contradicted since breakfast, "and it is, as you'll observe, the end of the week." He pointed at the clock with a stamp. It was the only weapon he seemed to own, and he held it like a man who knew what it could do. "Sixteen minutes. I've had to enter it as a *tardiness.*"

"Is that a real category?"

"It is now."

He took out a fresh ledger sheet, ruled a line across it with a steel edge, and wrote the word *TARDINESS* at the top in careful capitals. Aldric had the distinct impression of watching a man found a small nation.

"Do I get a certificate?"

"You get a *record,*" the clerk said, with something close to pride. "Certificates are for people who do things right."

Aldric set the box on the counter. It held everything the Grand Tournament had lent him and now wanted back: a competitor's sash, a badge with a number on it that had, at one point, been the most important number in his life, a pouch of unused visitor tickets nobody had ever come to claim, and a sheaf of printed sheets with his own name on them, in several typefaces, none of which he'd been asked about. He'd been told to return it to the records office by noon. He had, in fact, been told this on three separate occasions, by three separate people, each of whom had used a slightly different tone of voice to imply that he would forget.

He had forgotten. Ordinarily this would have bothered him.

This morning, with the yard raked and the sand gone and a face he still couldn't put down, it felt like a mercy. A small, dull errand. A counter, a clerk, a stamp. Nothing on the other side of it that could possibly turn out to matter.

"Sash," the clerk said, and took it. "Badge." He weighed it in one palm like a man testing fruit. "Tickets. All four unused?" A pause, and a quick, involuntary look up. "Nobody came?"

"It was a short guest list."

"Mm." The clerk wrote something. It was probably not unkind. "Sheets. I'll need you to check these against the master before I can sign you off. Standard procedure, in case the printers made an error." He slid the sheaf back across the counter and flipped open a great slab of a ledger with the other hand. "Read them. Tell me if anything's wrong. If nothing's wrong, say *nothing's wrong* and sign. If something's wrong, say *something's wrong,* and I will be forced to think about it."

"I see the appeal of the first option."

"Everyone does."

"Is there a third option?"

"There is no third option. I've checked. I checked at length. It's why the chairs are hard."

---

There were eleven sheets. Aldric went through them at the speed of a man who wants to be finished: his bracket, his match order, his badge number, the competitor list with his name lodged awkwardly among a great many better-bred ones, a sheet for the Final that had the wrong hour on it in one corner and had been corrected in ink by someone with a very steady hand. Everything was in order. He was reaching for the pen when his eye slid off the bottom of the ninth sheet and caught on a table he hadn't expected.

*NOBLE COUNCIL TIER: SEATING (SEMIFINAL).*

It had ended up in his set by accident, he assumed. A stray. Someone had filed the wrong tier's chart in with a competitor's packet, and it was going to become somebody's entire afternoon.

He should have turned the page. It was, quite precisely, none of his business. He held it, for a moment, in the deliberate, careful way of a person handling something they had already decided not to read.

Then he read it.

It was tidy work. A grid, five rows deep, the seats numbered left to right across the curve of the tier. Beside every number, a name and a House in the same square, upright print. *A delegate of the river trade. The steward's deputy and guest. The envoy's secretary.* Every seat a person, every person a seat, the whole tier laid out like a very expensive game of draughts.

He was not looking for anything. He wanted that clear, later, when he tried to explain it to himself. He was doing what he always did with any room: counting. Rows, seats, exits, who sat beside whom. It was a nervous habit, the sort of thing a man did with his hands. And at some point during the counting, quite without permission, his eyes went to the third row back from the envoy's box.

He had spent two matches looking at that row. He knew, without needing to check, which seat it was: the ninth from the aisle, three rows behind the box. He'd learned it the way you learn the location of a sore tooth.

He found the ninth seat on the chart.

There was a number. There was a small square beside it.

The square was empty.

Not blank, in the way a printer's error is blank, a gap or a smudge or a place where the ink ran dry. It had a border, like every other square. Inside it, set in the same neat type as everything around it, was a single dash. A small, deliberate horizontal line. The kind of mark a person makes when they mean *nothing goes here*, and want everyone to know that they knew.

Aldric read it again.

He turned to the tenth sheet. It was the Final's chart, printed on the same grid, in the same hand. The ninth seat, third row back, carried the same border and the same dash.

*Clerical,* he told himself. *A seat that was cancelled. A guest who never came.*

But a cancelled seat would have said so. It would have said *withdrawn*, or *released*, or carried a line through a name. There was no name to strike. This seat had not been given up, or reassigned, or lost. It had been printed, twice, on the official record of an event a king had attended, and both times it had been very carefully, very officially, given to no one.

And someone had sat in it.

He had seen it. A man in expensive grey, unhurried and unreadable, watching the yard the way no one else in that tier was watching it. Watching *him.* Aldric had felt the attention like a hand between his shoulder blades. He had looked up in the semifinal and found the seat occupied, and looked up in the Final and found it occupied, until it wasn't, and in every case been so thoroughly convinced of the seat's reality that it hadn't occurred to him to wonder whether it existed.

There was one more detail, and it arrived a moment late, the way the last stair in a dark house does. Every other seat in that tier had carried a cushion. Council blue, tasseled, rather too plump for a stone bench. He'd noticed them in passing during the semifinal and thought only that the nobility took its comfort seriously. He had never once noticed that the ninth seat, three rows back, had not had one.

The man in grey had sat on bare stone, perfectly upright, for the length of a match. And Aldric hadn't seen it, because the whole of his attention had been on the man's face.

*Ask,* said a small, cold voice at the back of his mind, and it sounded suspiciously like Solen.

*Don't,* said another, and that was his own.

"Something wrong?" the clerk said, without looking up.

"I don't think so." He was pleased to find his voice entirely steady. "There's a chart in here that belongs to the Council tier. I don't think it's meant for me."

"Ah." The clerk held out his hand for it, then paused, and squinted, and took the top sheet back from Aldric with a small sigh. "Yes, the printers do that. The Steward's office sends everything in one lump, and they feed it through as one lump, and I have been *telling* them." He glanced at the chart, briefly and without interest, in the manner of a man who has looked at a great many charts. "Seating's issued against slips. Every seat. If no slip, no name, and if no name, the seat doesn't print. Steward's rule. Otherwise the cushions aren't paid for."

"The cushions."

"They're expensive cushions. It's a Council tier." He tapped the chart, above the ninth seat, without seeming to see it. "Nothing to it. Every square's a slip."

"And a square with nothing in it?"

"That would be a slip that was never written." The clerk looked at him, as though only now noticing he was there. "Which can't happen. Nobody sits without a slip. If anyone had been sitting there they'd have been turned away. I'd have heard about it. The Steward's office reports every seat problem to me, in writing, at length, three times."

"Of course," Aldric said. "That's very thorough."

"Who signs off on those? The Steward's office?"

"A deputy. A very thorough man. Do not go asking the Steward's office things, Vane." The clerk lowered his voice, as though the wall might be taking notes. "They'll send you a form to ask the question. Then they'll send you another form for the answer. I once waited a full term for a receipt for a receipt."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's a nightmare." The clerk stamped the ninth sheet with something like tenderness. "There. That's you finished. You may be released into the world."

Aldric looked at the stamp, and then at the small initials beside the dash, which he had not noticed before. There were two of them, in a firm, unremarkable hand: not the printer's, and not the clerk's.

The Steward's office had reviewed the seating chart. It had approved the empty square.

He signed the receipt. He said *thank you*. He walked out of the records office, and down a flight of stairs, and across a lower court, at the pace of a person with nowhere in particular to be, and only when he had come to the end of the colonnade, where the light fell across the flagstones in long, ordinary bars, did he stop.

"You've got a face," Solen said.

Aldric didn't jump. He'd been aware of Solen for the last several paces, in the way you're aware of a clock. Solen was standing in the shade at the end of the colonnade with a stack of returned library slips, looking as if he'd happened to be there for some time.

"It's paperwork," Aldric said.

"The face for paperwork is different. It has more contempt in it." Solen tilted his head, a fraction. "That one's for something with a name."

"It hasn't got a name yet."

"Then I'll be somewhere with a door that closes." He shifted the slips to his other arm and went on past, unhurried, and did not look back, which was the kindest thing he could have done.

Aldric watched him go. He would tell him. He was nearly certain he would. But not yet, not until the thing had a shape that would hold, and he had, at present, a dash in a square and a stack of cushions to go on.

He was thinking, quite calmly, about the cushions.

Somebody had sat in a seat the academy had never issued. Somebody had watched him across two matches from a place that did not exist on paper, and the paper, when asked, had been very careful to say that nobody had.

It hadn't said *I don't know who that was.*

It had said, in a firm and unremarkable hand, *there was no one there.*
