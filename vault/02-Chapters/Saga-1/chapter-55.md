# Chapter 55: Two Things

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"You look like a man with something to think about," Bavel said. He held out a rake. "Think it with this."

Aldric looked at the rake. It was a very good rake, iron-toothed and long-handled, and it had the air of an instrument that had ended more than one difficult conversation.

"Instructor, I don't recall volunteering."

"You didn't. The yard's closed for resurfacing. Somebody has to pull up the old sand before the crew comes at noon, and you were standing in my doorway at first light looking for a place nobody would find you." Bavel's face gave away nothing at all. "This is a place nobody will find you. Congratulations."

"That's very generous."

"It's very practical. Rake it into rows. Keep the rows straight. Don't leave anything under the surface I'd have to explain to the crew." He was already walking away, wiping his hands once on his rag, which Aldric noticed and chose not to think about. "Noon, Vane."

The yard was empty and enormous and smelled of cold stone and old scorch. Aldric took the rake, and the sand began to argue with him.

He raked for a while in respectable silence. The sand had been walked into that yard by a great many frightened first-years and had absorbed, in return, a great many frightened first-year secrets. It gave none of them back. It was, he decided, the most discreet thing at Greyveil, and he'd have hired it.

---

It turned out that raking was very good for thinking, in the same way that being hit by a cart was very good for clarifying your priorities.

The trouble was the pattern of it. Pull, step, pull, step, a long grey ridge of sand curling up behind the tines, and the yard laid itself out in front of him in orderly rows while his mind, unattended, went straight back to the hill.

*Two things,* he thought. It was a habit he'd learned from Maret, over a diagram with a missing return path: when a mess wouldn't resolve, stop looking at the mess. Find the two smallest pieces and set them side by side. See which one refuses to fit.

He had two pieces.

The first was the man. Not his crime, or his name, which Aldric didn't have. Just his face, kneeling in the trampled grass. Tired, and thin in the specific way of someone who has been walking for too long, and looking at the soldiers with something that was not defiance and had stopped, somewhere underneath, being fear. It had taken Aldric most of a day to put a word to it. *Relieved.* The man had looked like someone who had finally been allowed to put a heavy thing down.

The second was the armor.

White, with a grey trim the color of a blade left out in the rain. A figure standing apart from the rest, not calling orders, not moving, and yet somehow the axis on which the whole valley turned. A ring of soldiers, trained and armed and plainly dangerous, and every one of them had kept a little of their attention pointed at him, the way iron filings keep a little of themselves pointed at a lodestone.

He hadn't cast anything. Aldric had watched for it, out of habit and something like hunger. Not a flicker. He had simply stood there with a plain sword on his hip, wearing the kind of authority that didn't need a spell to hold it up.

Pull, step. The rake caught on something and he freed it. A ridge of sand, straight as a ruler.

He set the two pieces side by side, as Maret would have. And he waited, with the specific patience of a person who has been told that the answer will come eventually and suspects it will arrive at the worst possible time.

---

"You're doing it wrong," Rynn said.

Aldric didn't jump. He'd been aware of Rynn for the last forty paces, in the way you're aware of a wagon rattling up a hill. "You can't see the rows from there."

"I can see that they're tidy. It's suspicious. Nobody rakes that tidily when they're happy." Rynn perched on the low wall that ringed the yard, holding two rolls in a cloth, one of which he was clearly planning to eat regardless of what happened next. "Solen told me not to ask. I want it on record I'm not asking. I'm bringing bread. It's a completely different act."

"That's very legalistic."

"I'm learning from the best." He tossed a roll. Aldric caught it left-handed without breaking the rhythm of his rake, and Rynn made a sound of profound wounded pride. "Do you know how irritating you are? I brought you *food* and you've made me feel like the clumsy one."

"You are the clumsy one."

"I'm the *loud* one. It's a completely different qualification." Rynn bit into his roll, chewed, swallowed, and said, much more quietly than usual, "Whatever it is, you don't have to have it worked out before you say it. I'd like you to know that. I'm not smart, but I've got a very large pair of ears and nothing much in them."

Aldric stopped raking.

It was such a Rynn thing to say that for a moment he simply stood there, the rake propped in the sand, holding a roll and a stone in his chest that had suddenly gotten a great deal lighter and a great deal heavier at once.

"I know," he said. "Thank you."

"Good. Enough emotion. I have to go be somewhere else." Rynn slid off the wall. "Bavel's crew is coming at noon and I heard they've got a *cart.* I want to see the cart." He went, loudly, in the wrong direction, and corrected himself two turns later without any sign of embarrassment.

Aldric picked the rake back up.

Pull, step.

---

The answer came without any drama, the way a splinter finally works itself out of a thumb.

It was not that the two pieces didn't fit. It was that they fit *backwards*. Each one pointed the opposite way from the other, and he had spent a day and most of a night trying to make them point in the same direction, out of a stubborn conviction that pieces of the same event were supposed to agree.

The man's face was a warning.

He could see it now, quite plainly, laid out like a diagram. A man with a gift that answered to somebody else's will. A man who had been used and used, and had walked into a valley and felt, when it was over, the first honest thing in months. Nobody had asked him to. Nobody had needed to. The world was simply a place where a person could be turned into a tool, and hollowed out, and put down when he stopped being useful, and the only thing anyone would say about it afterward was that the investigation had concluded.

That was the shape of the thing. A face at the end of a rope somebody else was holding.

And the armor was a permission.

He stopped raking. This one arrived slowly, like a light being brought into a room, and it took him a moment to recognize what it was he was looking at.

The figure in white had not needed a spell. That didn't prove he couldn't cast. But he hadn't needed to, in a valley full of people who could, and that was the part that mattered. Aldric had spent a year assuming that the world ran on power in the way he understood it: circuits, channels, output, the hair-thin threads he'd been drilled to hold. And the world had shown him, from a hillside, at a very great distance, that it didn't only run on that. It had room in it for other kinds of strength. It had a place, a very large one, for the person who stood up straight with a plain blade and let the work speak.

*Daran.*

He said it in his head, carefully, the way you set down something you've been afraid to drop.

Daran had stood in a hall in Valdris Prime and heard a list read that did not include him, and had walked out before Aldric could turn around. Aldric had spent every day since carrying that as a debt. He'd rehearsed a dozen versions of it. *I should have looked for him. I should have said something. I got the door, and he got the wall, and I let it happen.* He'd assumed, in the flat, unexamined way you assume the ground is solid, that Daran had walked into a dead end, and that every day since had only made the end longer.

But Daran had always, ever since they were children, given his wanting one shape. A blade. A yard. A person who stood up straight and let the work speak. If he was anywhere, Aldric would have bet the last of his coin on steel in his hands, becoming the sort of person the world made room for.

It wasn't a dead end. It had never been a dead end. It was just a different road, and Aldric had been so busy feeling guilty about the fork that he'd never once looked to see whether the other branch went anywhere.

He breathed out. It felt as if he'd been holding it since the exam.

*He's all right,* he thought, and did not know whether he believed it or only wanted to. *He's not all right, probably. But there's a place for him. There was always going to be a place.*

He didn't examine the relief. He'd learned, this year, that examining a good feeling too closely was an excellent way to lose it, and he had very few of them.

Pull, step. Sand curled behind the tines. Somewhere behind the stable wall a bell was ringing the hour, and a small, dry, entirely reasonable voice in the back of his head observed that he was two-thirds done and that the crew would be here at noon, and he set his shoulders and picked up the pace.

---

He was nearly at the far wall when the other half of the thought caught up with him.

It came from the same direction as the good one. That was the worst of it. It arrived on the heels of the relief, the way the second bolt of lightning follows the first, and it was made out of exactly the same material.

*The world has room for people who can't cast.*

*The world has room for people who can be used.*

The same fact. The same door. He had just spent an hour convincing himself that there was a wide, generous space out there for a boy with the wrong kind of hands, and it hadn't occurred to him until this moment that a space that size had to be shaped by something. That the room the world made for Daran was made, in some very real way, by the same world that had walked a man into a valley and hollowed out his choices. That wide roads had wide ditches. That any place with room for a Daran also had room for a Drel Voss, whoever he'd been, and a shelf for the people nobody had looked for afterward.

And Aldric had been placed, on the day of the exam, in a very particular slot in that machinery. The examiners had doubled back. Maret had written something in a notebook. Somebody had sent five people in tournament uniforms to a courtyard, and one of them had looked at him on the way out.

He had assumed, all year, that he was on the outside of the thing, looking in. A witness on a hill.

It occurred to him, standing in a half-raked yard with an iron rake in his hand and the sun just clearing the wall, that he had absolutely no way of knowing how close to the machinery he already was. Or whether he'd been standing inside it since before he could walk.

At the gate, the crew's cart rattled into view, loud and cheerful and entirely unaware of him.

Aldric finished the last row. It was perfectly straight.

It didn't help at all.
