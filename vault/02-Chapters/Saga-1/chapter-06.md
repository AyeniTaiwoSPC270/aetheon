# Chapter 6 — What He Carries
### Saga 1, Arc 2

---

— Aldric Vane —

The cart was not fast. This was the first problem.

The second problem was that Daran had noticed it too and was now sitting beside Aldric on the flatboard with the expression of a person doing arithmetic in his head that he had not been asked to do.

"We're losing time," Daran said.

"We're gaining rest," Aldric said. "These are different things. Both true simultaneously."

"The exam—"

"Is in four days. We have been on this cart for forty minutes. The exam remains in four days."

Daran looked at him. Then he looked at the road. Then he looked at Bren's horse, which was moving at the specific pace of an animal that had made a private agreement with itself about how much effort the day was going to require.

"I worked out the exam weighting last night," Daran said.

"Of course you did."

"The practical component is worth more than the written."

"I know."

"You don't study for practical."

"I also don't stay up working out weighting percentages the night before a four-day road trip."

Daran was quiet for a moment. On the box ahead of them, Bren worked the reins with the particular unhurried patience of a man who had been making this run for twenty years and had long since stopped being surprised by anything the road offered him, including two boys arguing about exam methodology on his flatboard.

The morning was clear. The tree line on either side of the south road was that specific autumn green — not fading yet, just thinking about it. Ashford had rounded its bend and disappeared a while back, somewhere in the middle of a sentence about circuit efficiency that Aldric could no longer remember the beginning of, and the world had become road and trees and the slow creak of Bren's cart.

Aldric stretched his legs out and looked at the sky.

This was, he thought, the part they had not talked about when they talked about leaving. The part that was just — being on the road. Moving toward something that had been theoretical for long enough that the actual movement felt strange. Not bad. Just strange.

He had not looked back when the village rounded the bend. He had noticed not looking back. He had filed that and kept walking.

Daran was still doing the arithmetic.

"If the practical component is weighted at forty percent and the combat application at thirty—"

"Then the written is thirty percent, yes, I can also do subtraction—"

The horse stopped.

Not slowing — stopped. A single hard pull-up, the animal's front legs bracing in the packed road, and Bren was already saying something low and calm that was directed at the horse and not at them. Aldric grabbed the side board. Daran, to his credit, had already braced.

"Easy," Bren said, to the horse. "Easy, easy."

The horse did not move. Its right foreleg was lifted, barely — not dramatically, just enough. Bren climbed down from the box with the slow economy of a man who had done this before and was already diagnosing before his boots hit the road.

They watched him crouch. Pick up the hoof. Look.

"Stone," he said. "Lodged."

He worked at it for a moment. The horse stood and allowed this. The stone came free. Bren examined the hoof, pressed gently in two places, and the horse shifted its weight and did not want to take it.

Bren stood. He looked at the boys with the expression of a man who was genuinely sorry about something he could not change.

"She'll need to rest it," he said. "Can't push her on that. Not a long road." He looked at the distance behind them — Ashford, not yet far. "You're closer to back than you are to forward, lads. I'm sorry for it."

Aldric looked at Daran.

Daran looked at the road behind them, then the road ahead, then performed a brief internal calculation that his face made visible.

"Right," Daran said, and climbed off the cart.

---

They had been walking back for twenty minutes when Daran heard it.

Aldric knew he heard it before Aldric did because Daran stopped mid-sentence — something about the written component, the thread Aldric had lost track of entirely — and turned his head toward Ashford in the specific way of someone whose brain had received information that did not fit what it expected.

Aldric stopped beside him. Listened.

Noise. The wrong kind of noise — not the market's ordinary current but something with edges on it, something that had direction and urgency and underneath it a sound that Aldric did not have a name for but his body apparently did, because his body was already moving.

They ran.

---

The Greyback was enormous.

That was the thing Aldric had not been prepared for, though he could not have said what he had expected instead. Every piece of information he had about monsters was secondhand — stories from travellers, the assessor's mentions, the broad general knowledge that Ashford's distance from the northern forests was the closest thing the village had to protection. None of it had prepared him for the specific physical fact of something that size moving through the market end of the village as though the stalls and the posts and the people attempting to stop it were obstacles of roughly equal inconvenience.

It was low and broad, the way large working animals were broad — mass concentrated in the chest and shoulders, the hide scarred and thick in a way that read as simply old, simply a creature that had been everything's problem long enough that the world had stopped being able to mark it cleanly. It moved without urgency. That was the worst thing. It did not appear to be hunting. It appeared to be passing through, and the things in its way were simply in its way.

Wyla was there. Aldric registered this with a specific jolt — Wyla, the village trader, broad and efficient and furious, driving at the Greyback's flank with a cart pole and putting real weight behind it. The pole connected with the creature's shoulder. The creature turned its head and looked at her with an expression that was not anger, not threat assessment, simply the mild attention of something that had registered an input. Then it moved on.

Two village guards — Josse's occasional colleagues, men Aldric had seen his entire life carrying the light polework of a village that never needed serious guarding — were circling from the other side. Their weapons were agricultural-weight at best. This was visible from thirty feet away. They knew it. They were circling anyway.

Josse was in front of them.

He had his woodcutting axe. He was standing at the edge of the market's open space at the specific angle of a man who has positioned himself between something and something else, and who has no intention of moving, and who is also completely aware that he is not large enough, not fast enough, not armed correctly for what he is doing, and has decided that this is not actually the point.

Aldric knew that posture. He had seen it aimed at things considerably smaller than a Greyback.

The creature had a villager cornered — a woman Aldric half-recognised, pressed against the grain store wall, entirely still in the way that prey goes still, both hands over her mouth. The Greyback was not looking at her. It was looking at something past her, or at nothing. But it was between her and every exit.

Josse moved.

Not a charge — a deliberate placement, getting into the creature's eyeline, dragging its attention from the woman with the specific focused intention of a man who has decided that the problem in front of him is one he is going to be the problem's problem. He hit it across the snout with the flat of the axe. Not a wounding blow — a direction. Here. This way. Not her.

The creature looked at him.

Then it hit him.

Not its full weight — a sweep, one foreleg in a movement that was more dismissal than attack, the way something very large removes a smaller obstruction — but it caught Josse across his side and the sound was wrong and Josse went down in a way that legs do not go down when they choose to.

Aldric was already running.

---

Daran reached Josse first.

He was faster than Aldric over short ground — always had been, would be for a long time yet — and he dropped to his knees at Josse's side in one movement, hands already checking, the kind of efficient motion that had nothing in it except function.

"He's breathing," Daran said. Not to Aldric. To the air, or to himself. "He's—"

The Greyback turned.

Aldric arrived at a dead run to find his father on the ground and his best friend crouched over him and the Greyback between them and everything else, its attention coming around with the slow inevitability of something that could not be reasoned with or aimed or redirected — something that was simply present, and large, and here.

He did not decide anything.

---

— Daran —

The air changed first.

That was the thing Daran would not be able to explain later, even to himself — not the force itself, not the sound, but the moment before, when the space around Aldric Vane did something that had no mechanism Daran could point to. A shimmer. That was the closest word, and it was not close enough — silver-white, like heat haze but running the wrong direction, colder than the air around it, there and gone in the same breath.

Then it happened.

He had no word for it. He would have no word for it for years. What he saw was Aldric's hand — slightly extended, no real intention in the gesture, more like something had moved through him than out from him — and then something that was not visible but was absolutely present moved across the space between Aldric and the Greyback like a translation of force that did not need the air to carry it. Where it hit the creature, the Greyback's hide lit briefly — pale white-silver, a cold flash at the point of contact, there for less than a second and then gone — and the full weight and momentum of something that size simply ended.

Not stumbled. Not retreated. Stopped. It was on the ground and then it was not a problem and Daran understood from the absolute cessation of its movement that it would not be a problem again.

One second of total silence in Daran's head.

He looked at Aldric.

Aldric's hand was still slightly raised. His nose was bleeding — a thin line, not dramatic — and he appeared not to know it yet. His eyes were wrong. That was the second thing, arriving a half-beat after the shimmer: Aldric's red eyes had shifted, briefly, to something silver and deep and luminous that had no business being in a fifteen-year-old boy's face, and even as Daran registered it the colour was already bleeding back to red, the way embers cool when you stop looking directly at them.

Aldric's face was not triumphant, not frightened, not anything Daran had a clean name for. It was the face of someone who had just heard a sound they did not know they were capable of making, and had not yet decided what to do with that information.

Daran held the image for one full second.

Then the village noise crashed back in — voices, movement, someone calling for Maren — and Daran looked down at Josse and moved his hands back to checking and the image was filed in a place that Daran did not examine, not then, not immediately, but that would not stop working regardless.

---

— Aldric Vane —

The noise came back all at once.

He had not noticed it leaving. One moment the world had contracted to a single point — his father on the ground, Daran's hands, the creature turning — and the next it was all back, every sound Ashford contained arriving simultaneously: voices, feet on packed earth, the grain store woman somewhere behind him making a sound that was relief and shock in the same breath.

His nose was bleeding. He discovered this when the warm line reached his upper lip.

He stood where he was. The post of the nearest market stall was at his back and he put his hand against it without meaning to — not for support exactly, more like confirmation. The disorientation was not what he had expected. It was not dizziness. It was the sensation of something having moved through him that his body had not been designed to carry yet. Every circuit he had fired once at full output and was still vibrating with the resonance of it, like a bell that had been struck too hard. He waited for it to settle.

It settled, gradually.

Maren arrived.

He watched her cross the market space at a pace he recognised — not running, but the fast walk that covered ground faster than running and left both hands free — and she went to Josse without looking at Aldric first, which was correct, and crouched at his side, and Daran made room without being asked, and her hands began their work.

Daran moved to the edge of the space. He was nearby. He was not close. He had not looked at Aldric again since — since. He was watching Josse being moved, watching Maren's hands, and his face was the controlled face he put on when he was processing something he had not finished processing. Aldric noticed this. He did not process it yet either. They were even, in that way.

The gossip began before Josse was carried inside.

Aldric had expected it in the abstract. He had not expected the specific texture of it — two separate conversations happening simultaneously about twenty feet away from him, one with wonder in it and one without.

Wyla's voice, carrying because it always did: *did you see what he did, did you actually see it, I felt it from here—*

A voice he did not immediately identify, lower: *the Vane boy, the white hair one, what did he do, that was not — that was not anything I've seen from a trained mage—*

Then another, somewhere in the middle distance, the practical anger of someone who had been frightened and needed somewhere to put it: *we should have had a ward crystal, any decent village has a ward crystal, this wouldn't have reached us if we'd had one—*

And quieter, further, not meant for him but reaching anyway: *what is he, does anyone know, does Maren know what she took in—*

He stood with his back against the post and let it pass over him. Filed it the way he filed everything. The wonder and the unease going into the same drawer, neither of them processed, because processing would require hands he did not have available yet.

The post was solid. That was enough for the moment.

---

The house was quieter than the village outside.

Josse was on the cot they had moved into the main room — the bedroom was further from Maren's workroom than she wanted it tonight. Two cracked ribs, she said, in the specific tone she used for diagnoses, which stripped everything personal out and left only information. His side was dressed. He was awake. He had looked at Aldric once, from the cot, with an expression that contained several things and then closed into its usual steadiness, and he had not said anything. This was correct. This was Josse.

Aldric stood in the doorway between the main room and the workroom. The village outside was still talking — he could hear the shape of it through the walls, the story already passing between houses, already changing as it went. By tomorrow it would have details he had not noticed. By next week it would have details that had not happened.

Maren came to the doorway.

She looked at him. The full look — the one she had been giving him occasionally since he was very small, the one that felt like a calculation started years ago and still running. He had never known exactly what the calculation was. He stood still and let her do it.

She reached up and pressed two fingers briefly to the side of his face, just below the dried blood from the nosebleed. Not wiping it. Just — placing her hand there, for a moment, in the specific way of someone confirming something they could feel more reliably than they could see.

Then she dropped her hand.

"Eat something," she said. "Before it gets cold."

She went back to Josse.

Aldric stood in the doorway for one more moment. Outside the village was still talking. Inside the house it was quiet and warm and Josse was breathing and Maren's hands were doing what they did.

He went to the table. He sat down. The bowl of food was there, pushed to the side of the table by whoever had put it out — he had not seen who — and it was still warm.

He ate.

He did not think about what had come out of him, or what it meant, or what Daran's face had looked like in the one second before the world had come back. He put it in the drawer with everything else. He ate his food and he listened to Ashford talk through the walls and he did not look at his hand.

Not yet.
