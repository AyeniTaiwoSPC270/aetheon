# Chapter 18: The Pressure Distortion

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

Crest had been arguing for eleven minutes that every rule holds, and Aldric was beginning to suspect he meant it personally.

"Water puts out fire," said Crest. He sat straight-backed on the bench with his hands folded, like a man dictating his will. "It always has. Nobody has ever found the exception. That is what makes it a rule."

"Nobody's looked hard," said Aldric.

"That's your side. Argue it properly." Crest smiled. "Doven's orders."

Doven stood at the far end of the long table, holding a book he was not reading. It was upside down. Aldric had begun to suspect that was a technique.

He tried again. "Take a forge. Throw a cup of water on it. The forge doesn't go out. The cup does."

"That isn't beating the rule," said Crest. "That's arithmetic."

"Most rules are arithmetic in a good coat."

Somebody two benches over choked on a laugh. Crest's smile stayed exactly where it was. It had been built to.

"Swap," called Doven, still studying the underside of his book. "Halvon, the exception. Vane, defend the water."

Aldric took the side he was handed. "Water puts out fire. It has for as long as there's been anyone to complain about it. If that changes, we'll all hear."

"Thin," said Crest pleasantly. He leaned forward on his elbows. "The exception is never a bigger fire, Vane. A bigger fire is only arithmetic, as you say. The exception is a smaller one, put in exactly the right place."

His voice dropped, courteous, almost kind.

"Push less. Then watch where it goes."

The room went on around them. Aldric did not hear it.

He had given those words to Nessa Croft for free. He had not thought about who else was sitting in the room.

"Bavel does shells on Thursday," said Crest, in the same gentle voice. "Do try to hold."

"Time," said Doven, and turned his book the right way up with a flourish. "A draw. You both lost to the clock. Beautifully, though."

---

Bavel raised a shell without a word, and the whole yard stopped talking.

It stood a long stride out from him in the flat morning light, faint and glassy, like a bubble blown from a window. He did not lean on it. He did not look at it. He turned to the cohort with his slate against his chest.

"Shell. Stand inside it. Don't lean on it." He let it drop, and the air went back to being air. "Your partner fires three, eleven seconds apart. Then you swap. If it cracks, you'll feel it before you see it. If it goes, it goes." His eyes went along the line. "Same pairs. Same posts."

Aldric looked at post six.

Crest looked back, all courtesy, and inclined his head an inch.

"Same pairs," Rynn breathed beside him. "That's the universe being personal."

"It's a slate," said Aldric.

"It's *personal*." Rynn jerked his chin at post eleven. The boy there had come with his hood up and was watching Rynn's hands like weather. "He asked to be moved. Bavel said the board had heard it before."

"Thirty-three seconds of holding," said Solen from post nine, squaring nothing. "Plus the raise. Call it forty."

"Comforting," said Aldric.

"It wasn't meant to be."

At the far post, Lirien had raised her shell before Bavel finished the word *go*. It stood round her without a seam. Her partner fired the first bolt, watched it slide off, and looked at her own hand as though it had let her down personally.

Aldric looked away. It was a habit he was developing. It was not working either.

Aldric had a plan, and for once it was a good one. Nothing showy. A fat, sensible, ordinary shell, held for forty seconds, and a line on Bavel's slate that said *held* and nothing else. He already had a board and a notebook with his name on them. He was not collecting a third.

"Halvon fires," said Bavel. "Vane holds. Go."

---

Aldric raised the shell.

It came out the way everything came out of him. Fine, bright and stretched, a skin instead of a wall. At an arm's length it was like blowing a bubble around himself with one long, careful breath. He could feel the seams. Mana leaked through them like water through a sieve, and the pressure of it sat behind his teeth.

It shimmered, faintly, glassily. A window someone had talked into being round.

*Thicker,* he told it.

His hand did what it had always done. Thin, tight, small. The habit stood between him and the fix like a man guarding a door he'd been told to leave.

Crest raised his hand.

Aldric braced for a splash, the wide weak slap from the last drill. He'd have taken a dinner plate.

The bolt was the size of a coin.

It struck the shell at chest height with a flat, dry crack, and the whole skin shivered. A line ran out from the point like a scratch on glass. Aldric felt it in his teeth.

Crest lowered his hand. His face gave nothing away. He began, very visibly, to count.

"Eleven," said Solen, holding his own shell perfectly, to nobody at all.

*He practised,* Aldric thought. *He heard the answer, and he took it home, and he practised until his hand stopped shaking.*

Somewhere in there was the worst part. Crest had built a weapon out of something Aldric had given away, and it was a good weapon. Aldric would have been proud of it if it had been anyone else's.

Crest's second bolt hit the same spot.

The line branched. Two hairlines crept off it, then a third, spidering across the shimmer. The shell wasn't holding now so much as arguing.

Aldric reached for thick a second time and got the same answer.

*Pressure doesn't go to the nearest wall. It goes to the weakest one.*

He had said that to an entire room. Crest had been in that room.

"Your shell's going, Vane," Crest said, conversationally, across six paces of sand. "You can feel it, can't you? That's the sort of thing they tell you about."

"Ten seconds," said Solen.

"Nine," said Crest.

"You're supposed to be counting eleven," said Aldric.

"I'm supposed to be thorough."

Bavel, forty feet off, was watching the shell. Not the bolt. The shell.

Crest raised his hand.

Aldric knew where it would go. He knew the way you know which step is missing on a dark stair. It would go where the lines met, into the widest crack, and it would go through.

Something arrived behind his ribs.

It didn't come like the old pressure, quiet and patient, at the edge of sleep. It came like a hand between his shoulder blades. It came like being shoved, and it had a shape, and the shape was *no.*

"Sorry," said Crest gently, and pushed.

The air around Aldric went wrong.

It bent, all at once, like heat haze that had learned to be cold. A shimmer of silver-white ran out from his feet in every direction. A low note filled the yard, felt in the chest more than heard, like a breath held until it hurt.

Crest's bolt came in bright and went flat. It smeared against nothing, a stroke of pale light pressed thin as a coin on a road, and was gone.

The sand leaped away from Aldric in one clean pulse.

It ran outward and flattened. It smoothed itself into a ring four paces wide, its edge as crisp as a line drawn with a compass. The posts groaned on their bases. Dust that should have risen lay down instead.

Crest's boots went out from under him. He sat in the sand with his mouth slightly open.

Four seconds. Perhaps five.

Then it stopped, the way a hand lets go.

The shimmer was gone. So was the shell. The yard was silent. Aldric stood in the centre of a perfect ring, and his hands were shaking so badly he put them behind his back.

His mouth was dry as chalk.

He knew the shape of it. He had felt it once before, in Ashford, with a Greyback turning toward his father. That time, it had killed something. This time it had only moved sand.

That was smaller. It was not nearly small enough.

*That's not supposed to do that.* It was what Rynn had said about a bolt, days ago, and it had been funny then.

---

"Nobody move."

Bavel crossed the sand at an ordinary walk. He reached the edge of the ring and stopped with his toes on the line, like a man at a chalk mark. He looked at the flattened sand. He looked at Crest, sitting in it. He looked at Aldric.

He took the rag from his belt and wiped his hands. Then he wiped them again. The second time, there was nothing on them.

"Ambient discharge," Bavel told the yard, in the voice he used for reading the drill order. "Shell overload against old sand. It happens in this yard. Nobody touches the ring until it settles. The rest of you, continue."

Nobody continued. The whole cohort stood exactly where it was.

"*Continue,*" said Bavel.

They did, badly.

"Sir," said Aldric. "I—"

"Bench." Bavel had already turned his back. "Water."

Aldric sat. It was a long bench, and nobody else was on it.

Rynn arrived with a tin cup and a face like a man who had run through a fire. "I brought you water," he whispered, at a volume that carried to the gate. "It might be damp."

"Rynn," said Aldric.

"I'm *whispering.*"

"You're not."

Rynn tried again, visibly pulling the sound down through his chest. "Was it the sand? Bavel says it was the sand."

"I'd like to blame the sand," said Aldric.

"It's damp," Rynn offered.

"It's sand. It doesn't get damp."

"Then it's the yard," said Rynn.

"Then it's the yard," Aldric agreed. He drank. His hands shook so hard that the water shivered.

Solen, whose partner had stopped firing some time ago, sat down at the far end of the bench. He had a page in his hand, folded, and didn't square it.

"Four seconds," he said quietly. "The sand took eleven to stop moving."

"You counted."

"It's a habit." A pause that wasn't quite his usual pause. "I would not tell anyone what I counted."

He left before Aldric could reply.

Crest was getting up. He did it alone, because Milo had held out a hand and Crest had looked at it as if it were an insult. He brushed sand from his knees. He looked at the ring, and at his own hand, and at Aldric.

He wasn't smiling. Aldric almost wished he were.

Seya wasn't looking at Aldric at all. She was looking at the ring, and her eyes went across it edge to edge, measuring.

At post eight, Nessa Croft had lowered her hand mid-cast and not raised it again. She studied the ring the way she had studied the diagram: as a mildly inconvenient fence.

Aldric put the cup down. Nobody was looking at him, and that lasted about a second. Then he saw why.

Every face in the yard was turned to the ring. The posts, the sand, the ragged tide of first-years pretending to drill. Every one of them.

Except one.

At the far post, Lirien stood inside a shell that hadn't a single crack in it. She was not looking at the ring. She was not looking at Aldric. She had turned her head toward the gate, and she held it there, perfectly still, at the exact angle of someone who has already seen what she needed to.

Aldric followed her eyes.

Instructor Maret stood inside the gate.

He had not been there at the start. Aldric was certain of it. He would have catalogued a man in a coat like that. He had come in during the shell, unannounced, and nobody had looked up.

The big ledger was tucked under his arm.

The small notebook was open in his hand, and the pen had already begun to move.
