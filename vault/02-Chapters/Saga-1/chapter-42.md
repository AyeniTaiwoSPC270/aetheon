# Chapter 42: The Semifinal

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


The draw for the semifinal went up before Aldric had finished chewing his breakfast, which meant he heard the result twice — once from the board, and once from Rynn, who arrived at a dead sprint with his mouth already three words ahead of his feet.

"You've got Torval," Rynn said, skidding to a stop hard enough that he had to catch himself on the table. "You've got — Aldric, you've got *Torval.*"

"I heard the first time."

"You didn't hear it from *me* the first time, and I ran the whole way, so you're going to let me have this." Rynn dropped into the seat across from him, still catching his breath, and reached for a piece of toast off Aldric's plate with the casual entitlement of two years' friendship. "This is the one. This is the real one. Everybody's saying it. Even Bavel said something that might have been approval, which for Bavel is basically a parade."

"What did he say?"

"He said 'Hn.' But it was a specific kind of 'Hn.' I've catalogued his Hns. This was the good one."

Solen arrived a beat later with none of Rynn's velocity and all of his information, and set down a folded paper beside Aldric's cup without comment. "Half the bracket held the way I said it would. Ardell's through on the other side — beat Milne in four exchanges, none of them close. Crest lost his semifinal an hour ago." A pause, the smallest concession to something like regret. "Cleanly. No shame in it. He'll tell you himself if you ask, though I wouldn't recommend asking today."

Aldric turned that over — Crest, out, an hour before he'd even known his own draw — and found, to his mild surprise, that the flat coldness he'd have expected from himself two years ago didn't arrive. What arrived instead was something closer to sympathy, awkward and unfamiliar, like a coat that fit someone else better.

"That's..." he started, and didn't finish it, because he didn't actually know what he meant to say.

"Unexpected," Solen supplied. "I know. I've stopped trying to predict how you'll feel about Halvon. It's the one variable I can't model."

Rynn, mouth full of stolen toast, pointed the crust at Aldric with the gravity of a man delivering a verdict. "That leaves you and Ardell in the final, if you get through today. Which — no offense — is going to be considerably harder than not offending you about it."

"None taken. I'm aware."

"Are you, though. Because you've got the face. The one where you're doing arithmetic and don't like the number."

He was. He didn't say so.

The walk to the arena took them past the same stretch of tiered stone where, a week ago, a man in expensive grey had told a woman beside him that Aldric's seed was somebody's idea of a story. Aldric spotted him again by accident, further along the terrace, mid-conversation with a different companion, his voice pitched lower than it had been the first time.

"—admit I hadn't accounted for the boy having any actual output," the man was saying, not quite loud enough to carry, though it carried anyway, the way things did in a crowd this attentive. "One good match against a nobody with a grudge doesn't—"

"He beat Bale outright," his companion said. "That's not nothing."

"No," the man admitted, with the particular reluctance of someone revising an opinion he'd stated too confidently in public the first time. "It isn't."

Aldric didn't slow down to hear more. He didn't need to. It was, he thought, walking on, a small and entirely unglamorous kind of victory — not proving the man definitively wrong yet, just watching him stop being entirely certain — and it would have to be enough for today.

Torval was already in the yard when Aldric arrived, standing at the ring's edge with the same unhurried stillness he brought to everything, watching the crowd fill in without appearing to watch it at all. He didn't warm up. He never did. Aldric had come to understand, across two terms, that whatever Torval spent in a fight, none of it was spent early.

"You're not nervous," Torval said, when Aldric reached the ring. Not a question.

"I'm extremely nervous. I'm just quiet about it."

"Good. Nervous is honest." Torval considered him for a moment with the specific weight he gave everything. "I watched your match against Bale. You won by making yourself unreadable. That won't work today."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm not reading you the way he was. I'm not reading anything. I just wait, and answer what actually happens." A pause, unhurried as ever. "You can't out-think a strategy that doesn't require thinking about you specifically."

It was, Aldric thought, possibly the most words Torval had ever said to him in one sitting, and every one of them landed like a small, correctly placed stone. He didn't have an answer for it. The judge called the ring open before he needed one.

Torval opened with nothing — no bolt, no shell, just a step forward that closed distance Aldric hadn't accounted for, and Aldric's first Mana Bolt went wide because his feet were still deciding whether the match had actually started. Torval didn't press the advantage. He simply reset, unhurried, the way he reset everything, and waited for Aldric to catch up to his own reflexes.

"You're better than that," Torval said. It wasn't an insult. It barely sounded like commentary at all — more like a man reading a gauge.

"Give me a moment. I was still hoping you'd go easy on me."

"I don't do that anymore. You'll understand why by the end of this."

He meant Rynn. Aldric understood that immediately and filed the shape of it — Torval's genuine respect expressed as its opposite, a full effort instead of a kindness — and used the half-second of thinking about it to actually set his stance properly, which was, he suspected, exactly the gift Torval had just handed him disguised as a threat.

The middle of the match went the way Aldric had expected, in the sense that it was long, and the way he hadn't, in the sense that it never once felt like it was going anywhere. Torval absorbed two clean bolts without so much as resetting his footing differently, moved through a shell hold at an angle that made it look less like defense and more like an inconvenience he was choosing to acknowledge, and answered every third or fourth exchange with something precise and economical that Aldric only avoided because he'd started, somewhere around the fourth minute, to genuinely stop thinking and start reacting.

"I hate this," Aldric said, mostly to himself, ducking under a return he hadn't quite seen coming. "It's going *well.* In the wrong direction."

Torval didn't answer that. Torval rarely answered things that didn't require an answer.

That was when it started to slip.

He felt it low first — not the drawer, not the pressure building toward a decision, just a kind of heat behind his sternum that had nothing to do with exertion, arriving the way it always arrived, uninvited and entirely indifferent to whether this was a convenient moment. He knew this feeling now. He'd spent two years learning its exact texture, the specific quality of wrongness that meant something in him was about to happen whether he agreed to it or not.

*Not now. Not in front of the King's envoy. Not in front of anyone.*

He set it down the way he always set it down — both hands, deliberately, the old discipline from Ashford dressed up in academy clothes — except this time it didn't go quietly. This time it pushed back, low and insistent, like a held breath that had decided two years was long enough to wait for permission.

Torval's next bolt caught him a half-second late, glancing off a shell that had gone thin without his noticing, and the impact rattled up his arm and into the same place the heat was already sitting, and for one full second — long enough that he heard, distantly, someone in the crowd say something sharp and worried — Aldric's vision narrowed to a single silver-white edge at the border of everything he could see.

He did not let it go.

It cost him. It cost him more than Bale had, more than the half-second of stillness Solen had clocked in the quarterfinal — this was longer, deeper, a full-body effort of *no* directed at something that had no ears to hear it with, and by the time he'd forced it back down into whatever place it lived when it wasn't testing the door, his hands were shaking and his shell had failed completely and Torval was standing three feet away, having chosen, very deliberately, not to close the distance while Aldric was somewhere else.

"You went somewhere," Torval said. Not urgent. Just accurate.

"I'm back now."

"I noticed." A pause, weighing something. "I'm not going to ask what that was. I don't think you'd tell me, and I don't think it's mine to ask."

It was, from Torval, an almost extravagant kindness, and Aldric didn't have room in the moment to properly receive it, because the match wasn't over and his legs were unsteady and the crowd's noise had gone from background to something with edges.

He won it the only way he had left — not by out-thinking Torval, who wasn't playing a game that could be out-thought, and not by out-lasting him, which the last minute had already proven he couldn't do. He won it by refusing, one more time, to let the thing in his chest make the decision for him, and putting everything that was left — actual mana, actual effort, nothing borrowed from the place he wasn't looking at — into a single Mana Bolt thrown not at Torval's guard but at the ground three feet in front of him, at an angle Torval's unhurried economy hadn't accounted for because nobody sane aimed for the ground on purpose. The impact threw sand and force sideways instead of forward, caught Torval's ankle at an angle his footing hadn't planned for, and put him down — not hurt, not humiliated, just briefly, genuinely surprised, which was, Aldric suspected, rarer for Torval than losing.

The judge called it before Torval had finished getting back to one knee.

"That," Torval said, once he was upright and the crowd noise had started to settle into something recognizable as cheering, "was not a technique I've seen before."

"It's not a technique. It's a man running out of better ideas."

"Running out of better ideas and still winning is its own kind of skill." Torval offered a hand — not to help him up, since Aldric was already standing, but the same gesture he gave everyone, an acknowledgment rather than a courtesy. "You went somewhere in the middle of that. I meant what I said. I'm not going to ask."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me for minding my own business. It's not generosity. It's just correct." Torval's mouth did the small, private thing it did instead of smiling, and he turned to go, then paused. "Ardell, in the final. You'll want to actually rest before that one. I mean that as information, not concern."

"I'll take it as both."

"That's your business."

Rynn arrived at a dead run, as he did after every match Aldric had ever won, already loud, already three sentences into a thought Aldric had missed the beginning of — "—and I don't even care that I lost to him now, because if *that's* what he had left over after beating me, I feel considerably better about my whole week—" — and Solen arrived a half-step behind him, quieter, watching Aldric's hands with the specific attention of a man counting something he wasn't going to mention today.

"You're shaking," Solen said, low, under Rynn's noise.

"I know."

"I'm not going to ask what happened either. Everyone's decided that's the game today, apparently." Something in Solen's flat register shifted, just slightly — not warmth, exactly, but its nearest functional cousin. "You're through to the final. Ardell tomorrow. I'd suggest sleeping, if your body will let you, which given the last five minutes I have some doubts about."

"I'm always pleased with myself," Aldric said. "It's a low bar and I clear it constantly. Today it's a lower bar than usual."

"That's the most honest thing you've said all week."

Aldric let Rynn's noise and Solen's quiet count carry him toward the edge of the yard, away from the crowd still settling back into its seats for the next match, and it was there, at the rail, catching his breath properly for the first time since the bolt had left his hand, that he noticed the man.

He was seated in the tier reserved for the Noble Council, three rows back from the royal envoy's box, dressed in the kind of understated grey that cost considerably more than it looked like it did, and he was not watching the yard the way everyone else in that tier was watching the yard — not tracking the next match, not murmuring to the woman beside him about odds or Houses. He was watching Aldric. Directly, openly, with an expression that held no hostility in it at all, and no warmth either — something closer to a man reading a book he'd been looking forward to for a long time and had just reached an interesting page in.

Aldric held the look for exactly as long as it took to be certain it wasn't accidental, and the man did not look away first. He simply inclined his head, very slightly, the way one might acknowledge a stranger across a crowded room without any particular claim on the gesture meaning anything at all, and returned his attention to the yard as though nothing had happened.

Aldric didn't know him. He was fairly certain, standing there with his pulse still not entirely settled, that he'd have remembered if he did.

He filed it — not urgently, not yet — and let Rynn's noise close back over the moment, and told himself, without quite believing it, that a stranger's interest in the tournament's surprising fourth seed was, after everything else this week had already thrown at him, the least strange thing that had happened today.
