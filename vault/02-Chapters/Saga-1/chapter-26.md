# Chapter 26: Ambient

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"You look pleased with yourself," Solen said, which from Solen was practically an accusation.

"I'm always pleased with myself. It's a low bar and I clear it constantly."

"You're pleased with yourself in a specific way. The way where something worked and you're not going to say what."

"That's most of my pleased," Aldric said, and let it stay there, because four days had passed since the stair landing and he still hadn't told anyone — not Rynn, who'd stopped asking out of what Aldric suspected was genuine, hard-won restraint, and not Solen, who hadn't stopped asking so much as switched to asking sideways, in the hope Aldric would trip over an answer without noticing he'd given one. He hadn't yet. It felt, four days on, like something he'd built and didn't want handled — not out of secrecy for its own sake, but because saying it out loud might make it smaller than it had felt at the time, on a dark landing, watching a very careful boy's face come apart by degrees.

They walked to class together, the three of them, in the loose formation that had stopped needing discussion weeks ago — Rynn in the middle doing most of the talking, Solen at the edge doing most of the listening, Aldric somewhere between the two depending on the hour. Rynn was mid-complaint about a second-year who'd apparently claimed the good practice post before dawn out of what he insisted was pure spite and Solen insisted was pure scheduling, and Aldric let the argument wash past him the way he let most of Rynn's arguments wash past him, content, for once, to simply be walked alongside rather than watched.

"You've been easier to be around," Rynn said, apropos of nothing, breaking off his own complaint mid-sentence the way he sometimes did when a truer thought elbowed a lesser one out of the way. "Since — whatever it was. Last week."

"I'm always easy to be around."

"You're really not," Solen said. "You're precise to be around. It's adjacent to easy but costs more attention."

"High praise."

"I don't do low praise. It's inefficient." Solen adjusted the strap of his bag with the same exactness he did everything, and added, in the tone of a man closing a ledger entry, "Whatever it was, it's holding. You've stopped doing the thing where you map every room before you sit down in it."

"I still map every room."

"You map them slower now. That's the improvement." Solen let that sit, then delivered the last of it with no visible change in delivery at all, which was how Aldric had learned to recognize when he meant something. "I'd like it to keep holding."

Aldric didn't have an answer for that one that didn't risk saying more than he wanted to say in a corridor with witnesses, so he let it go unanswered, and the three of them reached the lecture room's steep half-bowl of benches in the same comfortable quiet that had, somewhere in the last four days, stopped feeling like something he had to manage and started feeling like something he simply got to have.

Mana Theory that morning had the particular hush of a room settling into its own routine. Maret's ledger was already open on the front desk when they filed in, his small notebook nowhere visible, which Aldric had learned meant nothing — the notebook came out when it came out, on its own schedule, for its own reasons, and speculating about when only wasted attention better spent elsewhere. Lira Voss took her usual second-row seat and corrected nobody today, for lack of anything yet worth correcting. Pell settled by the window. Two rows ahead and one seat over, in the periphery Aldric had stopped pretending he didn't track, Lirien sat with her usual stillness, already working before the bell had properly finished.

"Today," Maret said, without preamble, because Maret had never once opened a class with anything resembling one, "a sustained hold. Not the thread — you've done the thread. A weight, this time. Small, constant, held without flinching for the length of the exercise." He didn't explain further. He never did. He simply produced, from beneath the desk, a tray of smooth grey stones the size of a knuckle, and began setting one before each student with the flat efficiency of a man dealing cards he found personally uninteresting. "Hold it aloft with a bare thread of output. Not lifted — held. There is a difference, and by the end of the hour you will each have discovered it the hard way."

Aldric's stone sat in front of him, unremarkable, grey, entirely unaware of the several dozen students about to resent it. He caught Rynn's expression beside him — the particular dread of someone who had already, this term, discovered the hard way that his own output did not believe in *bare threads* of anything — and offered, low enough not to carry, "Think of it as a very small, very judgmental pet."

"It's already judging me," Rynn muttered back. "I can feel it."

"Everyone can feel it. That's the exercise."

Solen, on Aldric's other side, didn't look up from settling his own stone with the exact, unhurried care of a man defusing something. "The exercise is watching Rynn's face while he tries not to think about brackets."

"I resent that."

"You resent it accurately," Solen said, "which is the best kind of resentment to have," and there — Aldric felt it land even in Solen's flat register, the small private amusement of a joke that cost nothing and meant everything to the three of them specifically, the kind that had started to make this room, this hour, feel less like an obstacle and more like something close to easy.

He was, he realized, settling into the exercise the way he hadn't quite settled into anything at the academy before — not watching the door, not counting exits, not holding some part of himself in reserve against whatever the next six weeks intended to do to him. Four days since the stair. A stone the weight of a large coin, hovering a careful two inches above his desk on the thinnest output he could manage, steady as anything. He'd solved a problem that should have been beyond him, quietly, without a mark on him anywhere. For the length of one held breath, in a room full of other people's small grey stones wobbling and dipping and occasionally clattering back to their desks in defeat, Aldric let himself believe, fully and without qualification, that he was finally, actually, in control of something.

The stone did not move. Nothing about the stone changed at all.

The air around it did.

It wasn't like the yard. He understood that instantly, in the half-second before understanding became useless — the yard had come with a shape he could point to afterward, a shell cracking, a bolt landing, a *because*. This came from nothing. No provocation. No Crest timing a third shot for the widest gap. No memory of a Greyback bearing down on his father. He was sitting perfectly still, holding a stone above a desk in a room that had, until one breath ago, been the safest hour of his week, and the pressure simply *arrived* — the same silver-white distortion, the same bending of the air at its edges, except smaller this time, and worse for being smaller, because smaller meant it hadn't needed a reason at all.

The stone shot upward and cracked against the ceiling. A gasp went round — not a scream, nothing so dramatic, just the collective intake of thirty people registering, in real time, that something had gone visibly and inexplicably wrong two feet from where they sat. Aldric's ears rang faintly, the specific dull pressure of a held breath released too fast, and his hands, on the desk in front of him, were shaking in a way he had no order to give them that would make them stop.

He looked for the cause on pure reflex — the way he'd have looked for a door, an exit, a reason. There wasn't one. No Crest smiling somewhere in his peripheral vision. No memory surfacing hard enough to explain it. Just his own body, doing the thing it did, apparently now on no schedule and no provocation he could name, and that absence — the sheer, featureless absence of anything to blame it on — frightened him more than the burst itself had.

*If it doesn't need a reason,* he thought, with a clarity that felt almost separate from him, watching itself arrive, *then there's no version of careful that keeps it from happening again.*

Rynn's hand found his shoulder, the same instinct as always, half a beat too late to matter and entirely sincere for it. "You alright?"

"Fine," Aldric said, which was becoming, he noticed distantly, the single least true word in his vocabulary and the one he reached for the most.

Lira Voss had turned fully around in her seat, more startled by the ceiling than by him. Pell had stood halfway up before catching himself. And two rows ahead, Lirien had not turned at all — had, if anything, gone even more still than her usual stillness, the particular concentrated quiet of someone doing careful arithmetic behind an unmoved face, though whether the arithmetic was about the cracked plaster or about him, Aldric had no way to tell and told himself, firmly, that it didn't matter which.

Maret did not rise from behind his desk. He did not cross the room, did not ask a question, did not so much as look, for more than the length of a single unhurried glance, at either the small scorch of dust drifting down from the ceiling or the white-knuckled boy sitting directly beneath where it had struck. He simply reached for his ledger, made a note — one line, unhurried, in the same flat hand he used for every answer given aloud in seating order — and said, to the room at large, in a voice that hadn't changed pitch at all, "Continue. You have forty minutes remaining."

That was all. No pause in the schedule. No excuse offered to the room, the way Bavel had offered one in the yard — *ambient discharge, old sand* — no cover story at all, because apparently none was needed for an instructor who simply declined to react. The other students returned, with visible relief, to their stones. Rynn's hovered and dipped and clattered down twice more before the hour ended, and each time he swore under his breath in a register Aldric found, despite everything, distantly funny.

Aldric did not manage his stone again. He sat with his hands flat on the desk instead, feeling the aftertremor work itself slowly out of his fingers, and watched Maret's pen move twice more before the bell — small, unhurried marks in a ledger Aldric would never be shown, about an event Maret had just watched happen from twelve feet away and declined, entirely, to acknowledge.

It was the silence that stayed with him longest, walking out at the end of the hour with Rynn already loudly reconstructing the incident for anyone who'd listen and Solen quiet at his other side, watching him with the specific attention of someone doing his own private ledger work. Bavel's cover story had been a kind of shelter, however thin — a lie built to keep the yard from asking questions Aldric couldn't answer. Maret hadn't bothered building anything. He had simply looked, noted, and moved on, as though the event required no explanation because he'd already decided, privately, exactly what it was.

That was worse, Aldric thought, than any reaction would have been. A reaction would have told him something — surprise, alarm, even the small performed calm Bavel had managed in the yard, all of it information he could have used. The silence told him only that Maret already knew enough not to need to react at all — that whatever the pattern was, this was simply the newest data point in it, expected, unremarkable, already accounted for somewhere in a ledger Aldric would never be shown — and gave him no way of finding out what that meant, or how long Maret had known it was coming before it came.

Four days ago he'd stood on a dark stair landing and made a very careful, very frightened boy back down without ever raising his voice, and he'd walked away from it believing, for the length of exactly one good week, that carefulness was a thing he could keep building — that if he just stayed patient enough, watched closely enough, he could out-think anything the academy put in front of him, including the parts of himself he didn't yet understand. The ceiling still had a faint grey mark on it where the stone had struck. Nobody had cleaned it. It would still be there tomorrow, and the day after, small and unexplained and entirely his, a permanent little proof that some things did not ask permission before they arrived.
