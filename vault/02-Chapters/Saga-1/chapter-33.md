# Chapter 33: Dross

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

Three days after a girl he'd known for four minutes told him a stranger's interest in him "had a cost," Aldric had developed a working theory of his own: if you sat still long enough in a room, the room eventually decided to talk about you anyway.

He'd tested it twice already this week. Both times, correct.

"You're doing the face," Rynn said, not quite under his breath, as they crossed the courtyard toward the theory wing.

"I have several faces."

"The arithmetic one. Solen's face." Rynn said it like an accusation. "It's contagious. I caught it off him in October and I still haven't fully recovered."

"You're describing a personality trait like a rash."

"It *is* a rash. A very precise, very smug rash." Rynn glanced sideways, checking whether that had landed. It hadn't, not visibly, which only made him try harder. "Are you going to tell me what's actually wrong, or are we doing the thing where you carry it around for a week and then mention it once, sideways, like it's not important?"

"That's usually the plan, yes."

"I hate the plan."

"Everyone hates the plan. The plan persists anyway." Aldric had told them both a flattened, edgeless version of Sable's warning three nights ago — *someone's worried about someone else's interest in me, no further detail available* — and Solen, walking a half-step behind them now with a folded page whose corners he'd already squared twice, had not pressed for more. Solen always pressed. The not-pressing was its own small weight, sitting exactly where the questions should have been.

"I did look into it a little," Solen said, unprompted, in the tone he used for information he was fairly sure Aldric didn't want yet. "The name you wouldn't give me. I have three candidates and no confidence in any of them."

"I'd like it to stay that way for now."

"Noted. I'll keep guessing badly in silence, then." Solen said this without any evident distress about it, which was somehow worse than if he'd complained.

The theory wing swallowed them the way it swallowed everyone at this hour — a corridor, a stair, the specific hush of forty people arranging themselves into the same half-bowl of benches they arranged themselves into three times a week. Instructor Maret was already at the board when they filed in, setting a fresh diagram with the flat, unhurried economy that Aldric had stopped finding remarkable somewhere around the fourth week of term. No greeting. There never was one.

Aldric copied the diagram out of habit rather than need. His actual attention was three days old and standing behind the library, and it had not moved since.

*Not to him. To you.* He'd turned the sentence over enough times now that it had started to lose its shape, the way any word did if you repeated it into a mirror too long. He went back to the diagram instead, which at least had the decency to stay the same shape every time he looked at it.

"Croft," Maret said, and Nessa answered without standing, crisp and correct, and the review ground forward the way it always did — a name, an answer, the pen moving or not moving, no one ever told which. The year-end assessment was close now — close enough that most of the benches had stopped joking loudly about it, not close enough that anyone had actually stopped thinking about it, Aldric included, for reasons the room didn't share and never would.

"My cousin failed his second year in the review, two years back," said a boy near the front — one of the ones who'd decided academic dread was a subject worth performing at volume. "Nobody would tell him why. He just — didn't clear it. Repeating everything now."

"Comforting," Rynn said. "Truly. Thank you for that. I'll sleep beautifully tonight."

"I'm just saying it happens."

"It happens to people who don't study," Seya said, from two rows over, in the register of someone laying down a fact rather than offering an opinion. "It isn't a lottery. It's arithmetic with consequences."

"That's the least comforting sentence I've heard today," Rynn muttered, "and I was in the room for the cousin story."

Aldric didn't join it. He had his own private, unfinished arithmetic running underneath the diagram — about what a review that compared recorded answers without correcting any of them might actually be *for* — and none of the numbers in it were friendly. He kept it to himself and let his pen move.

"Halvon."

Crest answered cleanly — return continuity, correct branch, no embellishment laid over the top. He'd been like that for a while now: precise, economical, no performance in it at all. Since the stair. Aldric watched him sit back down without so much as a glance sideways and thought, not for the first time, that a person could win an argument so completely that the other person simply stopped being interesting to watch lose.

Milo, beside Crest, glanced back once toward the last row — the row nobody in the first year sat in, by an unspoken zoning that had existed before Aldric arrived and would presumably outlive him — and then didn't look again. Aldric followed the glance out of pure old habit, the room-mapping reflex that had, admittedly, been running a little slower this term than it used to.

Four second-years sat back there, repeating core modules alongside a cohort that had already overtaken them once. It was, Aldric understood distantly, not a subject anyone discussed. You didn't ask why someone was sitting through your year's class for the second time. You noted it the way you noted everything that didn't concern you, and moved on, and the review moved on with you.

One of the four — thin, dark-haired, a jaw set permanently against something that had nothing to do with the lecture — had not said a single word in any class Aldric had shared with him since the term began. Aldric knew his face and had never once heard his voice. He hadn't even had a name for him until Solen supplied one weeks ago, in passing, with the flatness Solen reserved for information he'd catalogued but hadn't yet found a use for: *Dross. Repeating.* Nothing further offered. Nothing further asked.

"Vane."

Aldric answered — return path, weak branch, the pressure finding the thinnest wall rather than the nearest one, the same answer he'd given in October and still believed — and Maret's pen moved the ordinary amount, which these days felt like a small mercy rather than the insult it once had.

The review ground on. Aldric let it, and went back to not thinking about the library cut-through, and failed at that too.

He was still turning Sable's sentence over — *I don't know yet. That's not me being careful. That's me being honest* — when Nessa, three benches down, asked the question that cracked the hour open without meaning to.

"What happens," she said, plainly, the way she asked everything, "if you fail it? The review. Specifically. Not the rumor version."

The room's attention, which had been diffuse a moment before, found a single point and stayed there. Even Maret's pen paused — not for two seconds this time, for one, and Aldric noticed only because he'd learned, over the term, exactly what to notice.

"You repeat the modules you failed," Maret said, without inflection, as though reading it off a card he'd read a hundred times before. "The record notes it. Nothing else happens."

"That's the official version," Crest said — not a challenge, just an observation, delivered in his newly economical register. "Is there an unofficial one?"

"There's a version students tell each other in corridors," Maret said. "I don't correct rumors. I correct diagrams." He tapped the board, once. "Continue."

It was, by any reasonable measure, a small moment. Nobody laughed. Nobody flinched outright. The class moved on to the next name within four seconds, the way classes always did, closing back over the gap like water over a dropped stone.

Except that in the back row, for the first time all term, somebody spoke without being called on.

"That's not true."

Every head in the room turned at once — not toward Maret, toward the voice, because nobody in living memory of this term had heard it before. Dross hadn't raised his hand. He hadn't stood. He'd simply said it, flat and quiet, into a silence that had already been closing, and the effect was somehow louder than shouting would have been.

Maret looked at him for a long moment. Whatever calculation happened behind that look resolved into something Aldric couldn't read from four rows away.

"Go on, then," Maret said.

Dross didn't look at Maret when he answered. He looked, very briefly, at Aldric — long enough that Aldric felt it land somewhere just under his sternum, the way a look did when it wasn't asking anything, only confirming something the speaker had already decided — and then he looked back down at the desk in front of him, finished with the room entirely.

"They fail the ones who notice," he said. "I noticed."

Nobody asked him what he meant. Nobody, Aldric understood with a cold, settling certainty, was going to. Maret said "Continue" a half-beat too evenly, and the review moved on to the next name as if nothing at all had happened, and the second-year at the back of the room had already gone back to being someone nobody looked at.

---

"That was," Rynn said afterward, in the corridor, at a volume that suggested he hadn't fully worked out how loud the sentence deserved to be, "extremely not normal."

"No," Aldric agreed.

"Do we know him? Does anyone know him?"

"I know his name," Solen said. "I don't know anything that would make the name useful."

"That's the least useful sentence you've said all week, and you told me yesterday that Crest's boots are new because his old ones would've cracked at the toe by now."

"That was useful. This is a gap. There's a difference between not having information and having decided not to give it to me." Solen said it without heat, the way he said most true things about himself. "He's said nothing all term. Today he said one sentence, to Vane specifically, in front of forty people, at the exact moment the review came up. That's not a boy losing his temper. That's a boy who's been waiting for an opening."

"Or," Rynn offered, with the air of a man laying down his single remaining card, "he's just strange, and it's a coincidence, and we're building a whole conspiracy out of one weird sentence from a boy who eats lunch alone."

"That is also possible," Solen said.

"You don't sound like you believe it."

"I don't. But I'd like to note, for the record, that I considered it." Solen squared the corners of his folded page, once, out of habit rather than need. "Two candidates for the strange-coincidence theory. One for the other one."

"What's the other one?"

"That he's telling the truth, and it isn't about him at all."

Nobody had anything to say to that. Rynn opened his mouth twice and closed it again both times, which for Rynn constituted a full minute of visible internal debate, and then gave up and changed the subject to whether the kitchens would still have bread by the time they got there, which Aldric understood, correctly, to be Rynn's version of setting something down gently rather than dropping it.

Aldric thought about the rest of it the whole way back across the courtyard — about a boy who'd sat silent for a full term through a class he was already failing once, holding one sentence in reserve for the moment it would land hardest, and choosing, of everyone in the room, to spend it looking at him.

He didn't have a shape for what it meant yet. That night, lying with the ceiling doing nothing in particular above him, he ran the two conversations against each other the way Solen would have — one cost with no shape, promised by a girl who watched Varen and clearly, generally didn't misspeak; one accusation with no evidence, delivered by a boy who apparently hadn't spoken since the term began and had chosen, out of every possible audience, him. Different people. Different rooms. No thread he could point to that ran between them, only the same low, cold note underneath both — that Greyveil had a way of dealing with people who saw more than they were meant to, and that whatever it was, it had already started paying attention to him twice this week, from two directions that didn't know about each other.

He filed it anyway, on top of a stack that was getting harder to close the drawer on, and did not mention to either of them that it was the second time in three days someone had told him, in almost so many words, that noticing things at this academy came with a bill nobody had shown him yet.
