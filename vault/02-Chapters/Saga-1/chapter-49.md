# Chapter 49: Drel Voss Before

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Drel Voss —

The goat was the first thing to notice the riders, which meant Drel noticed them roughly four seconds later, which was still — by the standards of the last six weeks — a personal best.

He was elbow-deep in a lambing pen that wasn't his, on a farm whose owner believed his name was Corran, holding a breech kid the wrong way round and not caring, because the ewe was frightened and Drel's hands had that effect on frightened things whether he asked them to or not. The kid stopped struggling the moment he touched it. It always did. That was the whole trouble with him, distilled into one small, wet, ungrateful animal: things stopped struggling around Drel Voss whether they should or not.

The goat, tethered by the fence line, had gone rigid three fields off, ears forward, and Drel had learned six weeks ago to trust a goat over his own ears every single time.

He finished the birth. That part, at least, was still entirely his — hands, breath, the particular patience it took to turn a stuck kid without breaking its neck or the ewe's. Nobody had ever needed to make him good at this. He'd been good at it since before he had a word for what he was, back when it was just a boy in a village who could calm a kicking mule with a look, and the village had called it luck, and later a gift, and later still — once the wrong people noticed — an asset.

He wiped his hands on straw, told the boy who owned the farm that the ewe would live and the kid would too, collected the two coins he was owed and didn't especially want, and walked, without hurrying, toward the tree line the goat had already told him about.

Not hurrying was its own kind of discipline. Six weeks ago he'd have run. He didn't run anymore, and it had taken him most of those six weeks to understand why, which was the part he genuinely couldn't tell anyone, not because he was being close-mouthed about it, but because he didn't have the words either.

He walked into the trees and kept walking until the farm was gone behind him, and only then let his knees remember they were allowed to shake.

*They're close,* he thought, and felt the thought arrive with its usual companion — a second, colder awareness sliding in half a step behind the first, checking it, weighing it, deciding what to do with it before Drel himself got a vote. He had stopped trying to name that second awareness months ago. Naming it made it sound like a person, and he did not want to spend what might be his last free hour being polite to the thing that had been wearing his hands like a glove.

He kept walking. East, toward denser country, away from the farm and the boy and the goat that had, in its blunt animal way, just saved his life for one more afternoon.

The trouble with running for six weeks on an ability like his was that the ability didn't stop working just because he needed it to be quiet. He had learned, from a bitter distance, exactly which of the local farms had a sick mule, a colicking horse, a ewe in trouble — learned it the way he learned everything now, as a low, constant hum at the edge of every field he crossed, animals turning their heads to watch him pass whether he wanted them to or not. Trying not to be noticed, with a gift built entirely out of being noticed by every living thing within half a mile, was a joke so thorough he'd stopped finding it funny sometime around week three. Somewhere around week five he'd started finding it funny again, in the specific, brittle way a man finds anything funny once he's run out of other things to feel about it.

He crested a low rise and saw the smoke before he saw the village — not fire-smoke, just the ordinary grey thread of a dozen hearths going about a dozen ordinary evenings, and something in his chest did the thing it always did at a sight like that, which was want, with an intensity that had nothing to do with hunger or shelter, to walk down into it and tell someone.

*Don't.*

He didn't know if the word came from him or from the other thing, and that not-knowing was, more than the running, more than the six weeks, more than anything else that had happened to him since Ashford, the actual wound. He used to be able to tell. In the beginning — and he made himself think the word *beginning* precisely, because pretending it had always been this way was its own kind of surrender — there had been a clean line between what Drel Voss wanted and what Drel Voss did, the same line every honest man walks around inside every day without noticing it's there. Somewhere in the months after that, the line had stopped being reliable. He would reach for a thought and find it already half-formed by someone else's hand. He would open his mouth to say a true thing and find the true thing rerouted, on its way out, into something smaller and safer and utterly beside the point.

He stood at the top of the rise, looking down at the smoke, and thought — deliberately, testing it the way a man tests a loose tooth with his tongue — *I should walk down there and tell them a search party is three fields behind me, so they lock their doors and keep their children in tonight, because whatever comes to collect me may not be gentle about how it does it.*

It was a good thought. It was, he was fairly sure, entirely his own — decent, practical, the kind of thing the man he used to be would simply have done without weighing it first.

His feet did not move toward the village. His mouth did not open. Something in his chest closed instead, smooth and total and entirely without malice, the way a door closes when a draft finds it rather than when a hand pushes it, and the good, decent, practical thought simply stopped being a thing he was doing anything about.

He stood there a while longer, not moving, testing the door the way he tested it most days now, out of something between curiosity and grief. It held. It always held. He had never once, in six weeks, gotten a warning past it to anyone who might have used it — not the boy with the ewe, not the innkeeper who'd fed him on credit outside Corvane, not, worst of all and longest ago, anyone in a village called Ashford that he had never set foot in and had ruined all the same, from a comfortable distance, with a creature that had never once, in its miserable animal life, wanted to hurt anyone until something reached into it and made it want to.

He had not chosen that either. He wanted, badly, for that to matter to somebody besides himself.

Gallows humor, he'd found, was the only kind that survived the trip. *A monster tamer,* he thought, starting back down the far side of the rise, angling away from the smoke, *who cannot warn a single living soul about anything, including the monster currently wearing him.* He had told that joke to precisely no one, for the excellent reason that there was no one left to tell it to, and it was, he privately thought, wasted on an audience of one goat and a lambing pen, but a man kept the muscle exercised regardless, in case anyone was ever again in a position to laugh at it with him.

Night found him in a dry culvert half a mile on, chewing the last of the bread he'd been given instead of proper payment, listening to the particular quiet that meant the countryside around him had noticed something and gone still about it. He did not need the goat this time. He could feel it the way he felt everything now — a spreading awareness of two dozen small, wary creatures further out doing exactly what the goat had done that afternoon, ears up, backs to the wind, watching something approach that they didn't have a name for either.

He thought, with the flat clarity of a man who has done the arithmetic too many times to flinch from the answer, *tonight, then.*

He did not run.

He had asked himself why, more than once, in the six weeks it had taken him to arrive at this culvert with soldiers somewhere in the dark beyond it, and the honest answer — the one that belonged entirely to him, untouched by any other hand — was that running had stopped being about escape somewhere around week four and had started being, quietly, unbearably, about how many more villages he would pass through before whatever was inside him decided to use him again. Every day he stayed free was a day closer to some farmer's ewe, some child's pony, some stranger's dog turning traitor in his hands the way the Greyback had, because whoever held the leash had shown, once, exactly what they were willing to spend him on, and Drel Voss did not believe for one instant that it had been a single, isolated expense.

He set the bread down. He did not hide, particularly. He sat in the culvert with his back against the cool stone and let his hands go still, for the first time in six weeks, entirely on their own instruction rather than anyone else's.

The soldiers found him within the hour — quiet, professional, more of them than the occasion strictly required, which told him something about how the kingdom had come to weigh the danger of one tired man who talked to animals. He did not resist. He did not call anything to his defense, though he could feel, distantly, half a dozen creatures in the surrounding dark straining toward the sound of trouble, ready without being asked, and it took every remaining scrap of will he owned to hold that door shut himself, on purpose, the one time in six weeks he actually wanted it closed.

A young officer said something to him that he didn't fully hear, about rights and procedure and a name he no longer used. He looked past her, at the treeline, at the ordinary dark that held two dozen animals he had just spent the last effort of his freedom keeping out of a fight that wasn't theirs, and felt something move through his chest that was not the smooth, total closing-off he'd grown used to, but something else — older, plainer, entirely his own.

Relief did not feel the way he'd expected, when it finally arrived. It felt like the first honest thing he'd been allowed to feel in six weeks, sitting quietly beside the much larger, much colder feeling of knowing exactly how little of what came next would be his to choose either.

They put him in irons that did nothing at all to the part of him that actually mattered, because the part of him that actually mattered had never once, in six weeks, needed his hands free to be dangerous, and somewhere behind his sternum, patient and total and entirely without a name he could give anyone, the thing that had been wearing him went very quiet, as if it, too, understood that its work here was finished for now.

He did not know who it was. He never had. That was the part he would carry into whatever came next more heavily than the irons — not fear of what they'd do to him, and not even the weight of Ashford, though that would never entirely leave him either, but the specific, grinding indignity of having been used by something he couldn't even properly hate, because hating a thing requires knowing its shape, and this had only ever shown him its hand.

He looked up at the officer, at the torches, at the ordinary tired soldiers doing an ordinary tired job, and said the only true thing he had left that hadn't been touched by anyone else's hand.

"I'm glad it's over."

She misheard it as confession. He let her.
