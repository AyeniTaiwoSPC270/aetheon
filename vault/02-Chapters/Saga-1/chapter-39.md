# Chapter 39: Rynn's Match

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


Rynn had been talking about this fight for six days, which was, everyone agreed, at least four days longer than was healthy.

"I'm not nervous," he said, for what Aldric was fairly sure was the ninth time that morning. "I want that on the record. Whatever happens today, I want it noted that I was calm."

"You've said the word 'calm' eleven times in the last ten minutes," Solen said. "That's not what calm looks like from the outside."

"How would you know? You've never been calm about anything in your life, you just look like you have."

"That's the definition of the skill."

"I beat Nessa Croft yesterday," Rynn added, apropos of nothing, the way he did when he needed to remind himself of something true. "I want that remembered too. In case today goes badly. I want there to be at least one good sentence in the official record before the bad one."

"You didn't beat her," Solen said. "She yielded when your shield caught her third bolt clean. That's not the same as beating someone."

"It's *exactly* the same as beating someone, Solen, that's what winning a fight *is*—"

"It's winning a fight with slightly worse posture about it."

Rynn opened his mouth to argue further, thought better of it, and settled instead for a long, aggrieved sigh directed at the ceiling of the practice tent, as though appealing to a higher authority that had, so far, declined to intervene on his behalf in any meaningful way.

"For what it's worth," Aldric said, "I think you'll do fine."

"You have to say that. You're my friend."

"I'm your friend, which means I'm also allowed to say you looked genuinely terrible against Nessa's third bolt and got lucky she yielded before the fourth."

"That's not encouragement, that's a *eulogy.*"

"It's both. I contain multitudes."

"You stole that from Solen."

"Solen stole it from somewhere first. Everything's borrowed eventually."

Aldric said nothing, mostly because he was watching the far end of the ring, where Torval had just arrived, unhurried, saying nothing to anyone, the way Torval arrived everywhere. Somewhere behind them, a cluster of second-years Aldric didn't recognize had already started a quiet, running commentary about how the quarterfinal draw had turned out — Torval against Rynn, which apparently meant something to people who followed this more closely than Aldric did, because more than one of them had used the word *rematch,* and none of them had bothered to lower their voices while saying it.

"They fought before," Solen said, following the look on Aldric's face without needing to be asked. "Supervised bout. Arc five. You were there."

"I remember. He let Rynn keep his footing instead of ending it."

"He's not going to do that today." Solen's voice didn't change register, but something in it settled, the way it did when he was stating a fact rather than an opinion. "Different stakes. He won't insult Rynn by holding back in front of the whole tournament. That would be worse than losing."

Rynn, three feet away, had clearly heard all of this and decided, with visible effort, to treat it as encouragement.

The judge called the ring open, and for the first two minutes, it looked almost even — Rynn's Ignis Lance came in fast and hot, exactly the way it always did, a thin white-orange line that Torval avoided by the smallest possible margin each time, moving with an economy that read less like defense and more like a man conserving something. Rynn pressed harder. Torval let him.

"He's not casting anything," Aldric said, low, to Solen. "Not once. Not even a shell."

"No."

"That's not normal for a quarterfinal."

"No," Solen agreed. "It isn't."

It became, over the next minute, the single strangest thing Aldric had watched all tournament — not because Torval was losing, but because of *how* he wasn't casting anything at all, and the crowd's murmur had started to shift with it, half of them convinced Torval was simply hoarding his mana for one decisive cast.

"He's not hoarding it," Solen said, low, working through it out loud the way he did when a thing had just clicked into place. "Look at his footwork on the third dodge — that wasn't free. Something paid for it. It's just not showing."

"Paid for it how?"

"Circuit density. If his channels run dense enough, a cast that would shimmer and drain for you or me barely registers as a draw at all — same cost, spent so efficiently there's nothing left over for a Mana Sense reading to catch. It's rare. I've read about it. I've never actually seen it." Solen's eyes didn't leave the ring. "I don't think anyone's ever caught him at more than a first-year drill, which means nobody's ever had a number to compare it against. He's not hiding a lack of anything. He's hiding how much there actually is."

It wasn't luck, then. Torval moved economical, unhurried, spending exactly what a moment needed and nothing more — the kind of control that came from a well so deep the drawing from it left no visible ripple at all.

Rynn didn't know any of that. Rynn kept pressing the way you press an opponent you believe is running low on something, throwing a third Lance, a fourth, each one costing him more visibly than the last — a fine sheen of sweat, a slight tremor working into his aim by the third minute, the cost of Ignis Lance's own held-beam limit starting to show in the way his shoulder dropped a half-inch lower with each cast. Torval read every one of them the same unhurried way, sidestepping, never once retreating further than the width of the ring demanded, conserving whatever it was he was conserving for the moment it would actually matter.

The moment came at the start of the fifth minute. Rynn, chasing an opening that had looked real for exactly as long as it took Torval to let him believe in it, held the beam a half-second past where his own control could carry it cleanly — flare-back licking up his forearm the way it always did when his aim outran his patience — and in the gap that opened, the half-second where Rynn's whole attention went to his own burned hand instead of the man in front of him, Torval closed the last three feet between them in a single motion that looked, from the stands, almost gentle, and put him down onto the sand with an economy that would have been humiliating from anyone else and read, from Torval, simply as correct.

Rynn hit the ground, and for one long second, didn't move.

Then he laughed. Out loud, flat on his back, one arm thrown across his own face.

"Okay," he said, to nobody, to the sky, to the entire arena. "*Okay.* That's the strangest way anyone's ever beaten me."

Torval offered a hand down. Rynn took it.

"You never cast," Rynn said, once he was upright, breathing hard, grinning despite the arm that was going to bruise badly by evening. "The whole match. Not once. I kept waiting for it."

"I cast the whole match," Torval said. "You just didn't see it."

Rynn blinked at him. "That's not possible. There was no shimmer. No drain. Nothing."

"There was. It just didn't cost enough to show." Torval considered him for a moment, the same unhurried weight he gave everything, and offered, for once, more than his usual currency of silence. "Dense circuits. Rare enough that most people never meet one. Doesn't feel like an advantage from the inside — mostly it just means nobody's ever been able to tell me how strong I actually am, including me. You lost to something you had no way of reading. That's not the same as losing badly."

"That's a very kind way of saying I lost."

"It's an accurate way of saying it. Kindness wasn't the point." Torval's mouth moved, very slightly, in something that might, on a less unhurried man, have qualified as amusement. "You'll be sore tomorrow. Ice it tonight, not tomorrow morning. You'll forget, and then you'll be sore for a week instead of two days."

"Is that concern? Are you being *nice* to me?"

"I'm being useful. There's a difference, and you'd know it if you paid better attention."

It wasn't the kind of thing that made a crowd cheer, exactly, but something in it landed anyway — a small, genuine ripple of respect moving through the stands the way it hadn't for either of the last two matches, because everyone had just watched a boy lose and stand up laughing about it, and there was something in that people wanted to believe was still possible, even here, even with House names stitched into every third collar in the crowd.

Aldric watched Rynn walk off the sand under his own power, waving away Solen's offered arm with theatrical outrage — *"I'm bruised, not broken, Solen, there's a difference"* — and felt something settle low and cold in his chest that had nothing to do with pride at all.

"You're doing the face," Rynn said, arriving beside him, still catching his breath, forearm already reddening under a hastily applied cooling charm someone from the medical tent had pressed on him. "The arithmetic one. What's wrong, I just lost, you're supposed to be comforting me right now, not doing *maths* at the air."

"I'm not doing maths."

"You're doing maths *specifically at Torval,* which is worse, because now I feel like I lost to a word problem."

Solen, arriving a beat later with the particular expression of a man who had also been doing the maths, and for considerably longer, said nothing at all — which was, Aldric had learned by now, exactly the kind of silence that meant Solen had already reached the same place he had, and was simply waiting to see whether Aldric would get there on his own.

Torval had held nothing back today. Torval, by his own admission, fought like a man who'd spent years learning to spend exactly as much as a situation required and not one measure more. And he'd needed the entire five minutes, patient and economical and clearly capable of far more than he'd shown, just to put down a first-year who overextended once, against an opponent who wasn't even trying to hurt him.

If that was what an actual quarterfinal cost the strongest fighter in the year, Aldric thought, watching the noble boxes settle back into their seats, already murmuring about the semifinal draw — what would something *real* cost the rest of them? Something that didn't fight fair, didn't wait for an opening, didn't care whether the crowd found it respectable, didn't stop the moment a boy's guard dropped for half a second.

He didn't have an answer. He filed the question anyway, the way he filed everything, and told himself, without quite believing it, that it was just tournament nerves talking.
