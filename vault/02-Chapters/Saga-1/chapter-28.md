# Chapter 28: Sharp

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"You're doing it again," Crest said, not unkindly, which was somehow worse than the usual register. "Looking at the ceiling like it owes you an apology."

"It does," Aldric said. "It cracked. That's practically an admission."

They were three days past the stone, and the mark was still there — a small grey bruise on the plaster that the maintenance staff had apparently filed under matters no one had died from yet. Aldric had made the mistake, twice now, of glancing up at it during a lull, and both times someone had caught him doing it.

Bavel's yard didn't allow for many lulls. Post six, post seven, the same posts as always, and today's drill was a plain refresher — three bolts each, eleven seconds apart, the kind of exercise that existed to remind a cohort it hadn't forgotten anything over a slow week.

"Cold makes eleven feel like fourteen," Rynn announced from post ten, blowing on his hands between casts. "That's a fact. I read it somewhere."

"You didn't read it anywhere," Solen said, from post nine, not looking up. "You invented it four seconds ago because your hands are cold and you wanted a reason that wasn't you."

"It could be true."

"It could also be that you're cold."

"Both things can be true, Solen."

"One of them is true. The other is an excuse dressed up as weather."

Aldric's first bolt landed where it always landed: tight, small, dead centre, a habit he'd stopped fighting weeks ago. Crest's had gotten better. Not tight — Crest would never be tight, his output ran wide and hot by nature — but controlled now, deliberate, the visible product of practising something on purpose instead of performing it.

"You've been better," Aldric said, because it was true and because saying true things to Crest lately produced interesting results.

"I've been focused," Crest said, which was as close as he'd come, in three days, to admitting anything had changed between them at all. He didn't smile when he said it. He hadn't smiled at Aldric since the stair.

Bavel called the drill to a close with his usual economy — one word, "Done," and a wave toward the gate — and the yard began its slow drain, students peeling off in twos and threes, Rynn already halfway to the gate arguing with Solen about whether eleven seconds felt longer on a cold day. Aldric started after them and got as far as the threshold before Bavel's voice found him.

"Vane. Post."

He went back. Bavel didn't explain — Bavel never explained — and handed him a rag and pointed at post seven, which had taken a smear of chalk dust across its face that offended some private, unstated standard. Aldric wiped it down while Bavel watched him do it, said nothing about the ceiling, the stone, any of it, and eventually grunted something that might have been dismissal.

By the time Aldric reached the gate, the yard had mostly emptied.

Lirien hadn't.

She stood at her usual post at the far end of the row, alone, finishing a hold she hadn't needed to finish — the drill was over, nobody was scoring it, and she was still standing there with a small shell drawn tight and seamless around her hand, watching it the way she watched everything, as though it might eventually confess to something. Aldric had never once seen her linger. Lirien read the board and left. That was the whole of what he knew her to do.

He should have kept walking. He knew that even as he stopped.

"You're not leaving," he said, which was not a question and not, on reflection, a particularly good opening.

"Neither are you," Lirien said, without looking up from her hand.

It was the first time she had said anything to him at all. Weeks now, across a yard and a lecture room, watching her not-look at him, and the first thing out of her was a correction.

He let the shell hold for another second before she dropped it, clean, no residue, no shimmer left behind to mark where it had been. Then she looked at him — properly, for the first time he could remember, not past him or around him or through the specific gap the yard always left around her — and something in her expression had already decided this conversation, whatever it was going to be, was going to be short.

"You've wanted to ask me something for weeks now," she said. "Ask it or don't. I have somewhere to be."

He hadn't expected the door to open at all, let alone this fast, and it cost him half a second he'd have preferred not to spend recalibrating in front of her.

"The yard," he said. "The burst. You watched the gate instead of the ring."

"I know what I watched."

"I'd like to know what you saw."

"You'd like to know what I *concluded*," Lirien said. "Those aren't the same request, and you know it, which is why you phrased it the soft way." She studied him for a moment with the flat, assessing patience she'd have given a diagram. "Ask the real one."

Aldric had spent his whole time at Greyveil reading rooms for a living. He had never once had a room read him back this precisely, this fast, by someone who hadn't even bothered to raise her voice doing it.

"Fine," he said. "What's wrong with my control."

"Everything," Lirien said, and for a moment he thought that was going to be the entire answer — one word, delivered flat, the conversational equivalent of a door closing. Then she went on, and he understood she'd only been deciding, in that half-second, whether he'd actually earned the rest of it.

"Your shell doesn't fail at the seams," she said. "That's what you tell yourself, because it's tidy, and because it means the fault is structural — something built in, something you can't help. It isn't true. It fails at the parts you stopped paying attention to." She nodded, once, toward the post he'd just wiped clean. "Your bolt is the tightest thing in this yard because you never once, not for a second, stop watching it. You watch it the way you watch everything — like it might turn on you if you look away. Your shell is the opposite. You raise it and then you relax, because you think holding it is the hard part and maintaining it is the easy part. It's backwards. The hold is instinct. The maintenance is where you actually have to be present, and you aren't. You're already thinking about the next thing."

"That's — " Aldric started, and stopped, because the honest end of that sentence was *accurate*, and he didn't especially want to hand her that for free.

"I've watched you three times now," Lirien said, before he could find a version of the sentence he liked better. "The first, in the yard, your bolt came out tighter than you meant it to — twice — no matter how hard you tried to ruin it on purpose. Your attention was already fixed on being small. It wouldn't move even when you ordered it to. The second, your shell cracked twice before it broke. Both cracks came from the same direction, on the same seam, because you'd stopped watching that side the instant you'd decided the other side was the interesting one. The third—" a pause, brief, the first hesitation he'd seen from her, gone as fast as it arrived, "—there was no shell. There was nothing to watch fail, because you weren't holding anything at all. You were holding a *stone*, and it still found the one place you weren't looking. That's not a flaw in your shell. That's a flaw in your attention. It goes wherever you've stopped pointing it, and it does that reliably enough that I could tell you, before it happens, roughly where."

She had just described the yard, and the sustained-hold exercise, in the same breath, without naming either of them, and Aldric felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the specific stillness of being seen by someone who had clearly been keeping better notes than he had.

"You've been watching that closely," he said, "for weeks now. And you never once looked like you were watching anything."

"I never look like I'm doing anything," Lirien said. "That's the entire skill. You'd think, given your own habits, you'd have recognised it sooner."

It was, he realised, the closest thing to a compliment she was capable of giving — an accusation shaped exactly like one, aimed at the part of him that filed and suppressed and gave nothing away, delivered by someone who apparently did the same thing better than he did and had simply never needed anyone to notice.

"Why tell me now," he said. "You had weeks."

"Because you finally asked instead of staring," Lirien said. "I don't answer staring. Staring is a request with no shape to it. I answer questions." She reached down for her bag, and something in the movement told him the conversation had already ended, was ending now, would be over before he found a way to make it longer. "And because it's going to happen again, and next time it might not be a stone."

"That's not comforting."

"I wasn't offering comfort," Lirien said. "I was offering an accurate assessment. You asked for the second, not the first. Learn the difference, it'll save you time." She slung the bag over one shoulder and started for the gate, unhurried, the same pace she'd used the day the board first told the whole yard what he was.

He let her get three steps before he found the only honest thing left to say.

"Thank you," he said, "for saying it plainly."

She stopped. Didn't turn round. For a moment he thought that was going to be the whole of her answer — silence, the gate, the usual exit — and he was already composing the version of this conversation he'd tell no one, when she spoke again, quieter, to the gate rather than to him.

"You held the stone for eleven seconds before it went," she said. "Everyone else in that room was still counting the crack in the ceiling. I was counting the eleven." A pause, brief, deliberate. "You should ask yourself what changes, between second ten and second eleven, that you can't feel happening. I don't think it's random. I think it's a threshold, and I think you're the only person in this academy who's had three separate chances to notice that and hasn't."

She left before he could answer that either — out through the gate at her own unhurried pace, the gap the yard always made for her closing up behind her the moment she was gone, and Aldric stood alone by post seven with a rag still balled in one hand, turning over the fact that she hadn't just watched the burst.

She'd timed it. Independently. To the second. Same as Solen, who'd refused to say what he'd counted, except Lirien had just told him outright — handed it over, unasked for, at the exact moment she'd decided he'd finally earned it.

Eleven seconds. Not four, the way Solen's had run in the yard. A different number, from a different exercise, and she'd noticed the shape of it anyway, closely enough to have already built a theory he didn't yet have the pieces to test.

He was still standing there when he understood, slowly, what actually unsettled him about the conversation. It wasn't that she'd been right about his control, though she had been, more precisely than he'd have managed about himself. It was that she'd clearly been keeping her own private ledger this whole time, the same as Maret, the same as Bavel, the same as Solen with his declined counts and his careful silences — except Lirien's ledger didn't stop at *something is wrong with him.* Hers had a number in it. A threshold. A second, eleven, that meant something she hadn't finished explaining and didn't seem inclined to finish explaining today.

He looked up at the ceiling one more time on his way out. The mark was still there, small and grey and unbothered by any of it.

He had the uncomfortable, settling feeling that it wasn't the only thing in this academy that had been quietly counting toward eleven.

Rynn and Solen were waiting by the gate, in the specific posture of two people who had been arguing about something for several minutes and had run out of new ground to cover.

"You were ages," Rynn said. "What happened? Did Bavel finally explain himself? Did the ceiling apologise?"

"I had a conversation," Aldric said.

"With Bavel."

"With a post."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer you're getting," Aldric said, and Rynn made a noise of profound betrayal that carried most of the way back to the common room, which Aldric judged, on balance, to be a fair price for not saying the rest of it out loud. Solen didn't ask. Solen only glanced at him once, the particular glance that meant he'd noticed something had changed and had decided, for now, to let Aldric keep it.

Eleven seconds. He turned it over the whole walk back, and did not arrive anywhere that felt like an answer.
