# Chapter 34: Daran Alone

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Daran —

Three hundred and twelve.

He didn't say it aloud. He never said the number aloud, on principle — a number said aloud started to sound like an excuse the moment it left your mouth, and he had promised himself, somewhere around day forty, that he would not become a person who needed excuses ready in advance. But it sat behind his eyes anyway, the way it sat there every morning before the light was worth anything: three hundred and twelve days since a hall in Valdris Prime had read his name and then not read it again, and fifty-three left until the year he'd given himself ran out.

He drew the sword — a plain one, cheap steel, the kind a farrier's second cousin sold to men who couldn't afford better and didn't ask why they wanted it — and started the first form before his hands had finished being cold.

The yard behind Hask's stable smelled the way it always smelled at this hour: manure, wet straw, the particular mineral sharpness of a well nobody had cleaned the moss out of in years. Hask paid him in a cot above the tack room and two meals and didn't ask questions, which made him, by a wide margin, the most useful person Daran had met since Ashford. Not a friend. Daran had stopped sorting people into friend and not-friend somewhere around month four; it turned out to be a category that cost more to maintain than it returned.

Form one. Weight forward, blade low, the turn that opened into form two before the wrist had finished committing. He'd drilled it enough thousand times now that his body ran it without asking his permission, which he understood, intellectually, to be the entire point of drilling anything — and which still, some mornings, made him feel less like a swordsman and more like a bell somebody had rung once, a very long time ago, and left ringing.

The book he'd learned it from — bought off a retired Sword Unit quartermaster for three weeks' wages and a lie about who it was for — had a diagram for this form with the weight distribution marked in careful, tidy percentages, as if a person's feet could be instructed in fractions. Daran had spent the first two months following the fractions exactly and falling down exactly as often as the diagram implied he shouldn't. It was only around month three that he'd understood the actual lesson buried underneath the numbers: the diagram was for a body that didn't yet know what it was doing. His job was to become the kind of body that didn't need the diagram, and then burn the diagram, metaphorically, since actually burning it would have wasted three weeks' wages twice.

Form two failed him on the turn, same as it had failed him yesterday and the day before that and, if he was honest — which he tried to be, in the mornings, before the day gave him reasons not to be — probably every day back to whenever he'd started counting.

He reset. Did it again.

His hands had changed more than any other part of him this year. He hadn't expected that — had expected the shoulders, the legs, the specific ache that lived now in a lower back that used to belong to a boy who thought a full day's farm work was hard labor. The hands had surprised him: a permanent thickening across the palm where the grip sat, two knuckles that no longer closed all the way flat, a scar along the outside of his left thumb from a training blade with a burr he hadn't caught until it had already opened him up. He looked at them sometimes, in the grey light before the sun cleared the stable roof, the way a stranger might look at another man's hands and wonder what they'd been used for. His own answer would have taken longer to give than anyone asking would have wanted to hear.

Hask came out once, midway through the fifth form, to throw slops to the hogs on the far side of the yard, and lifted two fingers at Daran without breaking stride — the whole of their morning conversation, most mornings, and more than sufficient. Daran lifted two fingers back. Neither of them had ever once discussed what he was doing out here before the stable woke, or why, and Daran had come to value the arrangement precisely because it required nothing further of him. A question would have meant an answer, and an answer would have meant deciding, out loud, what any of this was for — a decision he preferred to keep making silently, one form at a time, where it couldn't be argued with.

There was a version of this story, he thought, where a man appeared at the edge of a clearing at exactly this point — grizzled, silent, unimpressed, the kind of figure every training-montage instinct in him had been braced for since he was old enough to want one — and told him everything he was doing wrong in nine words, and the nine words fixed it. He had been braced for that man for three hundred and twelve mornings. The man had not shown up. Daran had started to suspect, somewhere around day two hundred, that the man did not exist, that swordsmanship at this level was not secretly handed down by grizzled figures in clearings but built, slowly and without witnesses, out of exactly this: get up, be wrong, get up again, in a stable yard that smelled like a well nobody had cleaned.

It would have been a good joke, if there had been anyone to tell it to. *I trained myself into a sword school. The alternative was waiting for someone to hand me one, and I've noticed that tends to happen to other people.* He didn't finish the thought all the way to a name he'd stopped saying even to himself. He'd gotten better at that too — stopping a thought two words short of where it wanted to go, the same discipline as the footwork, applied somewhere he couldn't see it working.

Form two, again. The turn came a half-beat early this time, which was wrong in a new and interesting way, and he filed the wrongness for tomorrow and reset a fourth time.

Fifty-three days. He had built the number the way he built everything now — deliberately, with a reason he could defend if anyone had been present to ask him for one. A year, exactly, from the day the hall had not read his name, to the day he judged himself ready to walk into a sword school on his own two feet and be taken for what he could do, not what anyone owed him for trying. Not Ironmark specifically. He had crossed Ironmark off the list somewhere around month six, once he'd learned enough about how sword schools actually assessed a walk-in applicant to understand that arriving as a complete unknown, with no lineage and no letter, would get him a polite refusal and a recommendation to try somewhere smaller. He needed somewhere smaller. He needed, specifically, somewhere that would look at what his hands could already do and not ask first what his name used to mean in a village nobody there had heard of.

He had a name for that place now, three towns south, gathered the slow way — a rumor from a cart driver, confirmed by a letter he'd paid a scribe to write on his behalf, confirmed again by the scribe's own cousin, who'd trained there for two years before deciding he preferred farming. A yard, not a school exactly — a master-at-arms who took six students at a time and turned away everyone he didn't like the look of, no exceptions made for coin. Daran intended to be someone he liked the look of. Fifty-three days to make that true, or close enough to true that a demonstration would carry the rest.

Form three. Clean. Form four, into the turn — and here, for the first time in three hundred and twelve mornings, something happened that Daran did not immediately have a name for.

It landed. All of it — the weight, the turn, the follow-through that form four demanded and that he had never once, not in three hundred and eleven previous attempts, gotten all the way through without a hitch somewhere in the last third — landed clean, in one motion, with no seam in it anywhere he could find by feeling for one afterward.

He stood there a moment, sword still extended, waiting for the feeling that was supposed to arrive when a thing you'd worked at for a year finally worked.

Nothing arrived. Not triumph. Not even relief, exactly — relief implied there had been a weight to set down, and what he felt instead was closer to nothing at all, a form completed the same way a form was supposed to be completed, unremarkable, correct, already halfway to ordinary. It should have felt like something. He had built three hundred and twelve mornings toward the specific idea that it would feel like something, and instead it felt like putting down a cup he'd finished drinking from — a small, complete, entirely forgettable act that his body would now simply expect of itself going forward, the bar quietly and permanently raised without ceremony.

That unsettled him considerably more than failing form four for the three hundred and twelfth time would have. Failure, at least, he understood. Failure told him exactly where he stood. This told him nothing except that the ground had moved under him while he wasn't looking, and that tomorrow the plain, unremarkable correctness of form four would simply be the new floor, and he would have to find some new failure to measure himself against or the whole apparatus of getting up every morning would stop making sense.

He lowered the sword. Somewhere in the stable behind him a horse shifted its weight and blew out through its nose, disgusted by something only it could smell, and the sound was almost enough like a laugh that Daran nearly answered it.

He thought, with the specific, flat clarity that came only in the half-second after a body finally did something right without asking permission: *he isn't in this anymore.*

The thought arrived sideways, the way the true ones always did, and took him a moment to recognize for what it was. He'd spent three hundred and twelve mornings measuring himself against a hall that hadn't read his name, and against the shape of a boy in it who had — Aldric's face at the exam, unbothered and unwatched by the same instrument that had doubled back on Daran twice; Aldric's silhouette in a field at dusk saying *we'll both get in* like a fact rather than a hope. He had assumed, without ever checking the assumption, that every morning's count was in some sense a count against that. That form four, when it finally landed, would land as a point scored somewhere Aldric could theoretically see it, even if Aldric never actually would.

It wasn't. It hadn't been, he realized, for longer than he could pin down — weeks, maybe, the measuring quietly replaced by something with no name attached to anyone else at all, just his own hands, his own footwork, his own fifty-three days ticking down toward a yard three towns south that had never heard of the village he was from or the boy he used to know in it. He tried to find the exact morning the substitution had happened and couldn't. It hadn't been a morning. It had been all of them, a little at a time, the way water found its way under a door without ever announcing the specific drop that finally got through.

He didn't examine that too closely. Examining it too closely felt like the kind of thing that could undo a year's work in an afternoon, and he had fifty-three days left and no intention of spending any of them on a feeling.

He sheathed the sword — cheap steel, plain hilt, wrapped twice more than it needed to be because the wrap was the one part of this whole endeavor he could actually afford to do well — and stood a moment in the grey stable-yard light, working the stiffness out of a shoulder that would ache by midday regardless of what he did about it now.

The road east ran toward Valdris Prime, toward the academy, toward everything he had already decided did not need thinking about this morning or most others. He didn't look that way.

He looked south instead — past the tree line, past where the road he actually cared about bent out of sight toward a yard he hadn't seen yet and a master-at-arms who might, in fifty-three days, decide to look at him twice — and found himself wondering, with no particular urgency and no answer waiting for him, what exactly he meant to do first once he got there.

He didn't know yet. For the first time in three hundred and twelve mornings, that didn't feel like a problem.
