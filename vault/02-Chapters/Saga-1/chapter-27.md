# Chapter 27: What Maret Does Not Say

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Instructor Maret —

The mark on the ceiling was still there at the end of the day, which meant either the maintenance staff hadn't been informed or had been informed and had decided, wisely, that a plaster scuff two feet above an ordinary desk did not constitute an emergency. Maret had looked at it four times since the incident — once during the class itself, briefly, the way he looked at everything; once while the room emptied; once alone, standing on a bench with a candle, close enough to see that the scorch pattern radiated outward from a single point rather than spreading from an impact, which told him things an impact mark would not have. And once now, at the end of the day, from his desk, out of habit rather than need, because he had already recorded everything the mark had to tell him and was looking at it the way a man looks at a locked door he has already tried the handle on.

He opened the small notebook and read back through it, which he did rarely and only when the ledger's own flat chronology stopped being sufficient on its own.

*First entry — four seconds at his desk. Longer than any other pause given that hour. The thread held thin without visible effort — not caution, habit. Compared against the room's average hold, an outlier by a margin worth noting.*

*Second — the yard. Arrived unannounced, timing coincidental only in the sense that all useful timing looks coincidental until examined. Four-pace ring, flattened sand, consistent radiating pattern. Bavel's explanation offered before being asked, which is itself worth noting — a man does not rehearse an excuse for something ordinary.*

*Third — the file. Examiner's doubled reading, closed by the Bureau as instrument variance. Closed too cleanly, by a hand that did not want the matter reopened, which is a different thing entirely from a hand that had simply finished with it.*

And now, beneath that, in the same flat unhurried script he used for everything, the fourth line, written not twenty minutes after the class had ended: *Today — no provocation. No partner, no bolt, no memory to point to. A held stone struck the ceiling from a seated position, a radiating pattern matching the yard's, smaller in scale, and the boy's face, when I allowed myself one look at it, wore the particular blankness of someone who had genuinely expected nothing to happen and had no ready story prepared for when it did. That absence of a prepared story is, on its own, more informative than the event.*

He set the pen down and considered, for a moment, the specific shape of what he now had. A colleague more inclined toward humor than he was — Doven, say, or Bavel on a rare unguarded day — might have observed that a plaster ceiling now bore roughly the same expression Maret's own face did most mornings: cracked in one small, precise place, and giving nothing else away. Maret permitted himself the thought once, privately, and did not write it down. Some observations were not evidence. They were simply true, and true was not, on its own, useful to him.

Three data points, as of the Records office and the closed file, had already been more than instrument variance could plausibly explain twice over — he had told himself as much, alone, with the year-end review still comfortably distant, and had spent every week since watching that distance close with the particular grim satisfaction of a man watching an hourglass he had personally set. Considerably less distant now. He had allowed himself no illusions about how quickly a term moved once it had somewhere specific to go, and he permitted himself, briefly, the mildly unwelcome observation that a shrinking clock had a way of feeling both very long and entirely insufficient at once, depending on which part of the problem he was looking at when he thought about it.

A fourth point changed the shape of the problem in a way that troubled him more than he expected it to. Three data points described a pattern. A pattern could, if one wished to be uncharitable to one's own conclusions, still be argued as coincidence stacked upon coincidence — a doubled reading here, an unusual pause there, a training accident somewhere else, each individually explicable by someone determined enough to explain it away, the way the Bureau's own hand had explained away the first of them with four words about a Q3 recalibration error. But a pattern with no external trigger on its fourth occurrence was not a pattern anymore. It was a mechanism. Something with its own internal schedule, running whether or not the boy provoked it, whether or not anyone in the room provoked him. That was not a student with an unusual gift he hadn't yet learned to manage. That was something operating on him, independent of intention, and Maret had spent enough years watching students fail to control ordinary spells to know the difference between *has not yet learned* and *cannot yet be reached.*

He thought, briefly and without much hope, of simply walking to Bavel's office and laying all four points on the man's desk together — the pause, the yard, the file, the stone — and asking, plainly, whether two independent instructors comparing four independent observations amounted, finally, to something worth taking upstairs. He had considered this before, in the quieter hours after he had first confronted Bavel over the file, and had talked himself out of it each time for the same reason, which he now turned over again to be sure it still held.

A single report, closed once, could be dismissed as an overcautious examiner double-checking a calibrated instrument. That was what the Bureau had done, cleanly, with a stamp and four words, and the file had gone back on its shelf no heavier than it had come off. But a *second* report — two instructors, independently arrived at the same conclusion, formally submitted together, four data points instead of one anomalous reading — that was not the kind of thing a Q3 recalibration note could plausibly explain a second time. Whoever had signed the first closure would have to either let this one stand, which meant an actual, unavoidable investigation with the boy's name at the center of it, or close it too, which would require a signature bold enough to ignore two faculty members' formal, joint observation of a repeated, unprovoked, physically destructive event. Maret did not believe, on balance, that the second closure was less likely than the first. He believed, if anything, that it was more likely — because whoever had closed the file the first time had shown, by doing so, exactly how much authority they were prepared to spend keeping this quiet, and a joint report from two instructors was precisely the sort of escalation that authority existed to be spent against.

And if it were closed a second time, formally, in writing, with both his name and Bavel's attached to the request that got buried — then the two of them would no longer be two quiet men independently watching. They would be two men on record as having asked the wrong question of the wrong office, which was, Maret had come to understand over a long enough career, the single fastest way to ensure that neither of them would ever be permitted to ask it again. A door closed once could, in principle, be reopened by someone with better proof and better timing. A door closed twice, on the same evidence, by the same two hands, tended to stay closed for good — not because the matter inside it had been resolved, but because the people asking about it had been quietly filed, themselves, under *handled.*

He would not hand anyone that satisfaction. Not yet. Not on four data points that, however compelling to him personally, still amounted to nothing he could put in front of a reasonable stranger and expect to be believed without an argument.

So: not Bavel. Not the Bureau, not yet, not with anything less than proof strong enough to survive being closed a second time. That left him precisely where he had left himself when the clock had still felt generous — alone, watching, writing lines in a notebook that answered no one and would answer no one until he decided what, exactly, he intended it to answer.

He thought, unbidden, of the conversation he had had with Bavel in the yard, some while past now, the two of them standing over the same flattened ring of sand with their two private accounts contradicting their two public ones. *Write what you saw,* he had said. *I wrote what I needed the yard to believe. Those don't have to match, Instructor. Yet.* The word had stayed with him longer than he cared to admit — that single, deliberate *yet*, offered by a man who did not waste words on hope he didn't privately hold. Bavel believed, evidently, that a day was coming when the two documents would have to match, and had said as much without saying it, in the careful, sideways way faculty who had both learned the same lesson about the Bureau tended to speak to one another. Maret was not certain he shared the belief. He was not certain he didn't. That uncertainty, more than any of his four recorded points, was the thing that kept him at his desk longer than the hour required.

*Compare,* he had written, weeks ago now, in the hour after he'd first confirmed the Bureau's closure note, without knowing yet what he meant to compare it to. He allowed himself, now, the small private indulgence of noting that the word had aged rather well for something written in a moment of genuine uncertainty — the kind of accidental precision a man got perhaps once a decade, and was entitled to be quietly pleased about, provided he told no one and let the pleasure cost him nothing in vigilance.

The real question — the one his four points did not answer, and could not answer on their own, however carefully arranged beside one another — was simpler to state than to solve. Data described a pattern. It did not constitute proof. Proof required something a closed file and a stamped note could not simply wave away with four words about a recalibrated instrument: a demonstration, witnessed by someone with the standing to make it matter, of the thing itself, on the record, undeniable, in a form specific enough that no authority above him could close it a second time without the closing itself becoming the story.

He did not yet know what such a demonstration would look like. He did not know whether it could be engineered, or would have to be waited for, the way this fourth point had simply arrived, uninvited, on an ordinary morning over a grey stone and thirty students who would talk about the ceiling for a week and forget it within a month. He did not know how many more data points he would be permitted to collect, quietly, before something forced his hand — the boy's control failing worse, in a room with fewer sympathetic witnesses; the Bureau's original signatory noticing a second set of eyes had started watching the same file; the year-end review, closer than it had been, which would compare every recorded answer in his ledger against every other and ask him, formally, to explain patterns he had spent a term deliberately not explaining to anyone at all.

He closed the notebook. He did not write *Compare* again — that word had done its work, and repeating it now would be sentiment, not method, and he had no patience for sentiment in his own hand.

Instead, beneath the fourth entry, in the same unhurried script, he wrote a single line and underlined it once, which he almost never did.

*What would proof look like, for a thing with no name?*

He did not know. He put the notebook away, banked the candle, and left the mark on the ceiling for the maintenance staff to find in their own time, thinking that whatever time remained was not very much of it at all, and that he had, as of this morning, precisely one thing he had not had yesterday, which was a question worth spending it on.

It was not much. A single underlined line, unanswered, in a notebook no one but him would ever read. But he had built entire terms of careful, patient teaching out of less than that, and he saw no particular reason a term of careful, patient watching should require any more.
