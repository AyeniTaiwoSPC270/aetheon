# Chapter 20: The Training Yard at Night

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

The trick to breaking curfew is to walk like you have every right to be doing it, and Aldric has been walking like that since he was nine years old and stealing apples from Presswick's tree, so the first two corridors are almost boring.

It is the gate that worries him. Not because it is guarded — it is always guarded, that is the point of it — but because he has never once, in fifteen weeks, needed to find out what happens to a first-year who gets caught on the wrong side of it after block curfew. He would rather not learn tonight. He would rather, more specifically, spend the next hour finding out whether the thing that put four paces of flattened sand between him and Crest Halvon four nights ago can be made to happen again, on purpose, with nobody watching and nobody to protect.

It cannot, as it turns out. But it takes him most of the hour to be sure.

There had been, before he left, a brief and unglamorous negotiation with himself about whether this qualified as a plan or simply as the thing he was going to do regardless of whether it qualified. Rynn would have called it a plan and volunteered to come along and be caught with him, on principle. Solen would have asked three questions Aldric had no answer for and then, very probably, come along anyway, silently, to time it. Aldric had told neither of them. This felt, obscurely, like something that needed to happen without an audience of people who would worry about him doing it — which was, he was aware even as he thought it, exactly the kind of decision that got a person a written reprimand, or worse.

The yard at night is not the yard he knows. The posts are the same posts, chalk-white under starlight instead of morning sun, and the sand holds the day's warmth for exactly as long as it takes his own breath to stop sounding loud in his ears. He picks post seven — Bavel's post, the one that got wiped and re-chalked and told to be worse — because it feels correct in a way he does not examine too closely.

He starts with what he knows. Mana Bolt, three casts, evenly spaced, the way Bavel taught it: point, push, don't throw. Each one lands exactly where he puts it, tight and small and entirely unremarkable, the same habit that made his first bolt the talk of the yard for one uncomfortable afternoon. It tells him nothing. Of course it tells him nothing — a bolt is a bolt, drawn out of the ordinary part of him that everyone else in his year also has, just less of, and the thing he is looking for did not come from that part.

"Come on, then," he says to the dark, out loud, which is a foolish thing to say to a phenomenon with no ears, and he knows it is foolish even as he says it, and says it again anyway.

Nothing comes on, then.

He tries what he remembers from the shell drill — the leak at the seams, the strain of holding something too thin for too long — and raises a shell until his arms ache with it, past the point any sensible person would keep going, waiting for that particular give, that particular *no* he felt with Crest's third bolt already cracking the air. His shell holds. It leaks a little at the edges, same as always, same familiar failure, and gives him nothing he did not already have four days ago. He drops it and swears, quietly, with real feeling, in a way Maren would have had opinions about.

He tries, briefly and with no dignity whatsoever, simply glaring at his own hand, as though it might be persuaded by eye contact alone to remember what it did four nights ago without being asked twice. His hand does not dignify this with a response. He decides, standing alone in a dark yard interrogating his own fingers, that this is possibly the single least impressive thing he has done since arriving at Greyveil, which is a genuinely competitive field, given that the field includes losing an argument with a lot-draw jar.

He thinks about the Greyback. He has spent four days not thinking about the Greyback, filing it in the same drawer as everything else that happens to him without his permission, and now, deliberately, alone in the dark, he opens that drawer and looks at it directly for the first time since Ashford — Josse down, the axe useless in his hands, the specific, total certainty that something was about to end that could not be allowed to end. He holds that memory the way he held the shell. He waits for it to answer.

It doesn't. It just makes him feel sick, and cold, and sixteen years old and very far from a village that no longer entirely exists for him except as something he is countable distance from.

That is the reckoning, and it arrives the way most of his reckonings do — quietly, without ceremony, while he is failing at something else. There is no Daran here to notice the shimmer and say something flat and devastating about it on the walk home. There is no one at this academy who knew him before any of this, who could tell him whether the thing in the yard four nights ago looked like anything, meant anything, was building toward anything at all. Rynn would ask questions Aldric doesn't have answers for. Solen would count something and refuse to say what. Neither of them was there in Ashford. Neither of them has ever seen his hand move on its own in the dark and understood exactly what that costs a person to carry alone.

He is standing in the middle of an empty yard at an hour he is not supposed to be awake, trying to summon a thing that nearly killed a boy he does not like very much, and there is not one living person within four days' ride who would know what any of it meant even if he explained it perfectly. There had been a promise once, made in a field at dusk by two boys who believed it completely — *we'll both get in* — and whatever that promise had been worth, it had at least meant there was one other person on the planet who would have stood in this yard with him and not asked him to explain himself first. That person is somewhere else now, holding a sword instead of a promise, and would not recognise this version of the story if Aldric wrote it out for him in full.

He sits down in the sand, which is still faintly warm, and allows himself exactly one full minute of feeling that as completely as it deserves.

Then he stands up, because a minute is what he allows himself, and goes to post seven again.

He does not hear the warden until the warden wants to be heard.

"Vane."

Aldric does not jump, which he will later consider a genuine personal achievement, given that his entire body had spent the last hour assuming this exact sentence was coming from somewhere. He turns. The night warden is an old man, broad through the shoulders in the way of someone who used to be broader everywhere else too, carrying a lantern he has not bothered to light, which means he has been standing in the dark watching Aldric fail at something for longer than Aldric would like to think about.

"I can explain," Aldric says, in the tone of someone who has never once had a good explanation ready when he said that sentence.

"Don't need one." The warden sets the lantern down on the flagstones, unhurried, a man doing a job he has done for decades and sees no reason to rush. "Bavel said you'd come. Said not to stop you."

Aldric's mouth is open for several seconds before anything useful comes out of it. "He said what."

"Word for word, if you want it. 'The Vane boy'll be in the yard some night this week. Let him be.'" The warden looks at him with an expression that is not unkind and is also not remotely surprised by anything it is looking at. "So I'm not stopping you. I am, however, going to sit on that bench and watch you do it, because I'm not paid to pretend I didn't see a first-year out past block curfew, whatever an instructor tells me about it privately."

"That's — " Aldric considers several words. "Efficient."

"He's an efficient man. Four words is practically a speech from him. I've had entire conversations with Bavel that were shorter than the one we just weren't having about you." The warden settles his coat around himself against the cold with the air of someone arranging his own comfort as a form of protest. "For what it's worth, Vane, in thirty-one years on this gate I have caught exactly one other student out here at this hour who wasn't drunk, lost, or meeting somebody they shouldn't have been meeting. Wasn't as polite as you when I found him, either."

"It's a small academy full of people who think they're the only ones being careful," the warden says, sitting, exactly as promised. "Get on with it. I've got the whole night, and I'd rather you finish before my knees do."

Aldric gets on with it, though *it* refuses to cooperate any better with an audience than it did without one. He tries the shell again. He tries standing perfectly still and thinking about nothing at all, which fails immediately and completely, because a person who has just been told an instructor arranged for his curfew violation to go unpunished cannot, in fact, think about nothing at all. He tries anger, which is easy to find and useless once found. He tries fear, briefly, manufacturing it out of nothing, and it doesn't work either — his body, it seems, has opinions about which kinds of fear are load-bearing and which kinds are performance, and it will not be fooled by the second kind.

Somewhere past the middle of the night the warden clears his throat.

"Bavel say why?" Aldric asks, before he can decide whether he wants to know.

"Bavel," the warden says, with the particular patience of a man who has known Bavel for longer than Aldric has been alive, "does not explain himself to me. He tells me things I need to know to do my job, which tonight was exactly four words long, and not one syllable more."

"That sounds like him."

"It generally is." The warden studies Aldric for a moment in a way that makes him feel, not for the first time this term, exactly measured. "You're not the only name I've had this week, if it helps."

Aldric goes very still. "What does that mean."

"Means what it means," the warden says, and does not elaborate, and Aldric understands, with the particular clarity of someone who has spent a great deal of his life reading what people choose not to say, that this is as far as the sentence is ever going to go.

He gives it another twenty minutes for form's sake, gets nothing for his trouble but sore arms and sandy boots, and calls it a night before the warden's knees can outvote him. He is at the gate, the warden already turning back toward his bench and his unlit lantern, when Aldric looks up out of pure habit — the same habit that maps every room he has ever stood in — and finds the theory wing across the courtyard almost entirely dark.

Almost. One window, third floor, is lit.

It is late enough that the light should not be there. It is precisely, unmistakably, the kind of late Doven jokes about and Bavel would call efficient and nobody with anywhere better to be would choose on purpose. Aldric stands at the gate a moment longer than he needs to, watching it, and for just a second — he will not be able to say afterward whether he imagined this, and it will bother him for exactly that reason — something in the window that might have been a shape shifts, very slightly, as if whoever is standing behind it has just decided not to be seen standing there.

*You're not the only name I've had this week.*

Aldric does not know who else. He does not know why a window in the theory wing is burning oil at an hour reserved for people who are either desperate or careful, and he has learned enough about this academy in fifteen weeks to know those are frequently the same people. He carries it back with him anyway — the light, the shape, the four words the warden wouldn't finish — adding it to a private ledger of his own that is getting far too full for one person to keep, and walks to his dormitory without looking up again, because looking up again will not tell him anything looking up once didn't.

Somewhere behind that window, someone is not asleep either, and Aldric would very much like to know why.
