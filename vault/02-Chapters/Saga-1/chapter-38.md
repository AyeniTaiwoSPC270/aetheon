# Chapter 38: Round One

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


Corwin Bale turned out to be the answer to a question Aldric hadn't known he'd been asking, which was: what does it look like when someone's spent an entire year training specifically to beat you, without you noticing they existed?

"He requested this ring," Solen said, low, as they walked out. "Not this match — this *ring.* Third one from the left. Same one you fought in yesterday."

"Why does that matter?"

"I don't know yet. I don't like not knowing things a day before they matter."

Rynn, jogging to keep up, had opinions about this. Rynn had opinions about most things, but this one arrived with unusual speed. "Maybe he likes the acoustics."

"Nobody requests a ring for the acoustics."

"I would. If I ever won anything, I'd want people to hear it properly." Rynn considered this further. "Actually, no, forget the ring, what I'd really want is a small bell. Rung once. Just so there's a *sound* attached to it afterward."

"That's not a prize, that's a cowbell."

"It's dignity, Solen. You wouldn't understand it, you've never wanted anything that loudly." Rynn peeled off toward the stands with a last, "Don't die, I've got money on you with Davan," delivered at a volume that suggested several people three rows back now also knew about the bet.

"You bet on me?"

"I bet on you *winning,* which is different from betting on you generally, which would be reckless." Rynn was already gone, swallowed by the crowd filling in around the third ring, which had, Aldric noticed once he actually looked at it properly, filled in considerably faster than yesterday's rings had. Word had gotten around about yesterday's match. People wanted to see whether it had been an accident.

Bale was already waiting when they reached the ring — tall, unhurried, House colors Aldric didn't recognize stitched small and deliberately understated at the collar, the kind of understatement that cost more than the alternative. He didn't look at the crowd. He looked at Aldric, once, with an expression that wasn't hostile so much as *satisfied,* like a man confirming a measurement he'd already taken from a distance.

"Vane," he said. Not a question.

"That's me."

"I know." Bale's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've watched you drill. Three sessions, from the upper gallery, where nobody thinks to look. You favor your right side by about a half-step when you're tired, and you always, always reset your stance the same way after a Mana Bolt — same foot, same beat, every time." A pause, unhurried. "I've had a year to plan around a half-step."

The judge called the ring open before Aldric could decide whether that was meant to unsettle him. It worked anyway.

The first exchange went the way the man in expensive grey would have expected it to, if he'd bothered to actually watch instead of reading a number off a sheet. Bale's Mana Bolt came in tight and precise, exactly where Aldric's stance would have put him a half-second earlier — except Aldric had heard the man say it out loud, which meant the half-step was no longer information Bale actually had, only information he thought he had, and the difference between those two things put four clear feet of open sand between the bolt and Aldric's shoulder.

"Lucky," Bale said, resetting.

"You told me your plan. That's not luck, that's just—" Aldric caught his shell up a beat late, felt it crack along one seam, absorbed the second bolt with his forearm instead and hissed through it. "—a mistake. Yours, not mine."

"One mistake." Bale didn't sound rattled. He sounded like a man crossing a single line off a much longer list. "I've got several more to try."

He wasn't lying. Over the next several exchanges Bale worked through what was, unmistakably, an actual campaign — testing the shell's timing, testing Aldric's guard when he cast one-handed versus two, testing whether tiredness changed his footwork the way a year of upper-gallery notes said it should. Each test cost Aldric something small. None of them, individually, cost him the match. All of them together were wearing him down faster than a straightforward beating would have, because a straightforward beating he knew how to read, and this was something else — patient, cumulative, designed.

"You're not even trying to finish it," Aldric said, between exchanges, mostly to buy himself a second to breathe. "You're just — collecting data."

"I collected the data a year ago." Bale reset his stance, unhurried as ever. "Today I'm spending it."

"That's a very calm way to describe hitting someone."

"I find calm is cheaper than the alternative. Costs less mana. Lasts longer." Another bolt, testing the same seam a third time, patient as a man checking whether a lock had changed since he'd last picked it. "You talk during fights. Is that a habit, or a delaying tactic?"

"Bit of both. Mostly I'm hoping you'll get distracted and explain your whole plan out loud."

"I already did."

"I noticed. Thank you for that."

Bale's mouth twitched — not quite a smile, closer to a man recalculating a variable he hadn't accounted for — and the next bolt came in a half-beat faster than the pattern had promised, testing whether talking slowed Aldric's own reactions the way it apparently slowed nothing else about him. It didn't. Aldric's shell caught it clean, first time all match, and something in Bale's shoulders went very slightly rigid, the way a man's does when a plan he trusted develops its first crack.

By the fourth minute his shell was thinner than he liked showing anyone, and the crowd noise had gone from background to a genuine pressure behind his ears, and somewhere in that pressure the *other* pressure — the one with no name he was allowed to say out loud, the one that had cracked a yard in half and put a mark on a ceiling that never asked permission — started, very quietly, to test its own weight against the inside of his chest.

Aldric noticed it the way you notice a held breath. Not as a thought. As a fact about his body that arrived slightly ahead of any thought about it.

*Not now.*

He didn't say it aloud. He didn't need to. What he did instead was the same thing he'd been doing since Ashford, since the drawer, since every time something in him arrived without asking — he set it down, deliberately, with both hands, the way you set down something heavy you can't afford to drop in front of people. It cost him a half-second of total stillness in the middle of a live match, which was either the single worst timing of his life or exactly correct, because Bale, expecting motion, threw his next bolt at where Aldric *would* have been moving, and hit air.

"That's new," Bale said, and for the first time, something in his patience actually flickered.

"I'm full of surprises," Aldric said. "Mostly to myself."

He didn't win by being stronger. He'd known, walking in, that he probably couldn't — not against a year of specific preparation. He won the way Lirien had once, flatly, told him he ought to think about winning: not by fighting the pattern, but by noticing it was a pattern at all, and refusing to be predictable inside it. Bale's fourth test assumed Aldric would guard high after two low bolts, because that was what the notes said tired opponents did. Aldric guarded low instead, on nothing more than a guess and the growing, uncomfortable certainty that being unreadable to a man who'd spent a year reading him was the only weapon he actually had left in the ring. The guess held. The bolt that would have ended it went wide by inches, and Aldric's answering shot — nothing clever, just a plain Mana Bolt, point and push, exactly as Bavel had taught it two years ago in a yard that felt very far away right now — caught Bale flat in the chest and put him down onto the sand hard enough that the judge called it before he finished sitting up.

The crowd noise, when it came, arrived like something physical.

Aldric didn't look for the man in expensive grey. He told himself that. He looked for him anyway, out of the corner of one eye, and found him not smiling, not frowning, just — watching, with the flat attention of someone recalculating.

*Good,* Aldric thought, and was surprised by how much he meant it.

Solen met him at the ring's edge before the judge had even finished the count, already talking, already past the part where anyone else would have said *well done.*

"That cost you something," Solen said. Not a question either.

"It was a hard match."

"That's not what I mean." Solen's eyes did the thing they did when he was deciding how much of an observation to actually say out loud. "You went still. Mid-fight. For about a second and a half. I timed it." A pause. "That's not a tactic I've seen you use before."

"It worked."

"It worked," Solen agreed. "That's not the part I'm asking about."

Aldric didn't answer that directly, because the honest answer was a thing he still didn't have words for — that the stillness hadn't been a tactic at all, that it had been the only thing standing between a won match and whatever the Ch. 26 ceiling-crack had been, small and unprovoked and entirely his own, arriving again in a stadium full of people who had come specifically to watch him. He'd held it. This time. By a margin he didn't especially want to measure too closely.

"I'm fine," he said instead, which was, as ever, the single least true word in his vocabulary, and the one he reached for the most.

Solen looked at him for a moment longer than the sentence deserved, the way he looked at a diagram he suspected of lying to him, and then, apparently, decided to let it go — for now, his expression said, in the particular way Solen's expressions said things without using any words at all.

Rynn arrived a moment later at a dead run, already loud before he was close enough to be heard clearly, something about *did you see that, he went completely still, that was the strangest thing I've ever seen you do and I've watched you do a lot of strange things* — and then, arriving at the actual point with the specific triumphant urgency of a man who has been vindicated financially, "*Also,* I have won money. I would like that noted somewhere official."

"You bet on me winning. That's not a prediction, that's just having met me."

"It's *risk,* Aldric. You could have lost. You didn't. I am now four coppers richer and considerably more insufferable." Rynn beamed at him like this was, itself, a form of congratulations. "Davan's furious. He bet on the *other* half-step guy specifically because he liked the stitching on his collar."

"That's not a strategy."

"It's Davan's strategy. It's mostly always been Davan's strategy." Rynn's grin faltered for half a second, replaced by something closer to actual attention. "You alright, though? You went still. Right in the middle. I've never seen you do that."

"I'm fine," Aldric said, and let Rynn's noise carry him past the question, the way it always did, the way he'd learned to let it — and let the noise of that carry him away from the quieter, colder fact sitting underneath it: that the thing from Ashford hadn't gone anywhere in two years. It had only gotten better, this whole time, at waiting for exactly the moment he was least prepared to notice it arrive.
