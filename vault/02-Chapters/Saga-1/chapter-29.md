# Chapter 29: Torval and Rynn

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"I volunteered," Rynn said, with the specific pride of a man who had just done something he was already regretting. "Nobody made me. I want that on the record."

"There's no record," Solen said. "There's a sign-up sheet with your name on it in handwriting that gets worse every letter, which suggests you wrote it faster than you thought about it."

"I thought about it for a full second."

"That explains the handwriting."

Bavel had started posting sparring bouts twice a week — supervised, controlled, first-years only, a chance to put the drills to use against something that hit back — and the board by the gate had filled up fast with names next to names, most of them chosen the way people chose things they wanted witnesses for. Rynn's name sat across from Torval's in chalk that did, in fact, get worse toward the end, as though his hand had started arguing with the rest of him halfway through.

"You could still scratch it," Aldric said.

"I'm not scratching it."

"Nobody would think less of you."

"*I'd* think less of me," Rynn said, which was, Aldric had learned, the only argument that had ever reliably worked on him. "Six weeks ago some idiot in a corridor made a joke about me being a punch waiting for an excuse, and I've spent every week since being careful. Careful got Crest's attention off me and onto you-know-what, and careful is *fine*, but I didn't come to this academy to be careful for the rest of my life. I came to hit things. Correctly. In a supervised setting, with witnesses, so nobody can call it a hearing afterward."

"That's oddly reasoned," Solen said.

"Thank you."

"I didn't say it was good reasoning. I said it was oddly reasoned. Those are different compliments."

Torval didn't say anything at all, which was, so far, the entirety of what Aldric knew about him. He was enormous in the specific way that made rooms rearrange themselves without being asked — not tall so much as *wide*, shoulders that looked like they'd been built for a larger person and then handed down early — and he moved through the yard with the unhurried caution of someone who had spent years being told, gently and less gently, to mind his own size around smaller things. He'd been in Aldric's year since the first week and had produced, in all that time, perhaps a dozen words that anyone could remember, none of them memorable enough to repeat.

Nobody had a read on him. That was the interesting part, and it was the part that bothered Aldric more than Rynn's odds did. Aldric had spent his whole time at this academy cataloguing people the way he catalogued rooms — a habit that had never once failed him twice on the same person — and Torval was the single largest gap left in the whole ledger. Crest had never bothered targeting him — too large to threaten, too quiet to embarrass — and Torval had simply existed at the edge of every social calculation in the year without anyone ever quite deciding what to do with him. Aldric intended, today, finally, to close that gap, whatever it ended up costing Rynn to provide the occasion.

"What's his affinity?" Rynn asked, watching Torval cross the yard with the particular caution of a man measuring a doorway he suspected might be too small for him.

"Earth," Solen said. "Working assumption. Nobody's seen him cast anything above a first-week drill. He answers questions in class the way you'd expect a very large, very patient boulder to answer questions, if boulders were occasionally required to."

"That's not reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be reassuring. It was meant to be accurate. Those keep turning out to be different things, around you specifically."

"He's going to fold you in half," Rynn's own voice supplied, unhelpfully, the night before the bout, lying in the dark with the specific honesty people only managed after the lamp was out. "I've seen him lift a training dummy over his head. One-handed. Like it insulted him."

"You'll be fine," Aldric said, which was not a promise he could actually back, and they both knew it.

"I know I'll be *fine*. I might also be *folded*. Those aren't mutually exclusive."

---

The yard was fuller than usual when the day came — word had gotten round, the way it always did, that Rynn Vane's-friend was matched against the enormous quiet noble nobody could get a read on, and half the first year had found reasons to be near the gate at the right hour. Crest stood with his usual cluster, arms folded, wearing the specific expression of a man expecting to enjoy himself and not especially hiding it.

Bavel called them to the ring — a chalked circle in the sand, wider than the drill posts, the closest thing to open ground the yard offered — and gave the rules in four sentences that took less time than most people's throat-clearing. "Curriculum spells only. Nothing above what you've been taught. First to yield, first knocked outside the ring, or first I stop it. Nobody's proving anything today that's worth a broken bone. Go."

Rynn went in loud, which surprised nobody. He'd never once approached a fight quietly in his life, and this one started with a shout and a lunge that would have worked, Aldric thought, against almost anyone his own size.

Torval simply stepped sideways and let him go past.

It wasn't cruelty. It was economy — the specific, unbothered economy of someone who had learned, the hard way and probably early, that his own size did most of his fighting for him if he simply declined to waste it. Rynn recovered, turned, and threw a Mana Bolt that Torval took square on a raised forearm without so much as flinching, the print smearing harmlessly against skin that had clearly weathered worse.

"That's not fair," Rynn said, mostly to himself, already circling for another approach.

"He's not casting," Solen observed, from the edge of the ring, in the tone of a man noting something for later use. "Not once. He's just standing there being difficult to move."

Rynn tried Ignis Lance next — the first time Aldric had ever seen him use it on the page, a thin, fast line of white-orange fire that had clearly cost him more effort to hold steady than the bolt had, his hand shaking faintly with the strain of a beam he could just barely sustain for its full three seconds. It caught Torval's shoulder, close enough to singe cloth, and for the first time in the bout, Torval actually moved — not away, but forward, closing the distance the fire had bought Rynn no benefit from at all, because a beam that held a man at range was useless the instant the man simply walked through where it used to be.

"That's the worst possible use of that spell," Solen said, wincing slightly. "He needed the range. He just gave it away."

"He's tired," Aldric said. "He's been holding a beam. Tired people make short-sighted choices. It's practically a rule."

Torval reached him a half-second later, and the bout, for one heart-stopping moment, looked like exactly what Rynn had predicted in the dark the night before — folded, or close to it, one large hand closing on Rynn's collar with the calm inevitability of a door closing.

Then Torval didn't throw him.

He held him there instead, at arm's length, off-balance and unable to do much of anything about it, and looked at him for a long moment with an expression Aldric couldn't immediately place. It wasn't triumph. It wasn't even particularly interested.

"You're going to lose your footing," Torval said — the first full sentence Aldric had ever heard him produce, low and slow and entirely without malice. "You keep leading with your weight before your feet are set. You did it with the bolt. You did it with the fire. You'll do it again in about four seconds if I let go."

He let go.

Rynn, predictably, lost his footing.

He didn't fall out of the ring — caught himself on one knee at the very edge of the chalk, close enough that the whole yard drew a breath at once — but he stayed down a moment longer than the fall required, looking up at Torval with an expression that had nothing to do with the loss and everything to do with genuine, startled confusion.

"You could have thrown me," Rynn said. "You had me."

"I know," Torval said.

"Why didn't you?"

Torval considered the question with the same unhurried care he gave everything, as though answers, like his own strength, were things not to be wasted on situations that didn't need them.

"Because you'd have gotten up and done the same thing again," he said, "and I'd rather tell you why it isn't working than watch you do it four more times. Throwing you teaches you I'm strong. You already knew that. It doesn't teach you anything you didn't have this morning."

Bavel, watching from the edge with his slate against his chest, said nothing at all — which was, Aldric had learned to recognise, its own kind of approval.

Rynn got to his feet slowly, brushing sand off his knees, and looked at Torval the way a man looks at a bill he hadn't expected and found, on inspection, entirely fair.

"That's the strangest way anyone's ever beaten me," he said.

"I didn't beat you," Torval said. "You're still in the ring."

"I'm on one knee at the *edge* of the ring."

"The edge is still the ring."

There was a pause while the whole cohort worked out whether that was a joke, and Torval's face, entirely without change, gave no hint either way — which, Aldric thought, was itself the funniest part.

"I want a rematch," Rynn said, "purely so I can lose to you again with better footing."

"That's a reasonable thing to want," Torval said, and something in his voice suggested this was, for him, roughly the emotional equivalent of a full grin.

---

Crest's cluster had gone quiet at the edge of the yard, the specific quiet of a group that had come expecting one story and left holding a different one entirely. Milo said something low that Aldric didn't catch. Seya didn't say anything at all — she was watching Torval with the same measuring look she'd given the ring after the burst, adding a line, Aldric suspected, to whatever long list she kept in her head about who was worth accounting for.

Crest himself said nothing, which was, for Crest, its own kind of statement.

Torval crossed the sand afterward, unhurried, and stopped in front of Rynn and Aldric both, the enormous bulk of him somehow entirely without threat now that Aldric had watched him decline three separate chances to use it.

"You're the one from the corridor," Torval said to Aldric, without preamble, in the same flat, unbothered register he'd used on Rynn. "The one Crest talks about and doesn't say anything true about."

"That's most of what he says about me."

"I've noticed." Torval considered this for a moment, the way he seemed to consider everything, at his own pace, on his own terms, indifferent to whoever was waiting on the other end of the sentence. "I don't much like people who need an audience to be cruel. He needs one. You don't seem to." He looked at Rynn, then back at Aldric, as though weighing the two of them against some private scale only he had access to. "I'd rather be near people who don't."

He walked off before either of them found an answer for that, the same unhurried pace he did everything at, and left the two of them standing in the sand with the distinct, settling sense that something in the yard's arithmetic had just quietly, permanently shifted.

"Did we just make a friend?" Rynn asked, watching him go.

"I think we just watched someone choose a side," Aldric said. "Quietly. Without being asked to."

"That's a very Solen way of putting it."

Solen joined them a moment later, unhurried as always, folding a page he hadn't needed to fold, and Aldric relayed the exchange while it was still fresh enough not to lose anything in the telling.

"For what it's worth," Solen said, once he'd heard it, "I've never once seen him cast anything. Not a bolt, not a shell, nothing above whatever the curriculum requires him to demonstrate and no more. I'd assumed it was Earth, on the strength of exactly no evidence at all, because he's large and it seemed like the sort of thing a large person ought to have."

"You guessed," Rynn said, delighted. "Solen *guessed* something."

"I proposed a working hypothesis pending better data. It is not the same as guessing."

"It's exactly the same as guessing."

"It's guessing with better posture," Solen allowed, which was, from him, practically a concession, and the three of them let the argument die there, comfortable, unresolved, the kind of small disagreement that existed only because none of them minded losing it — only the plain, unremarked fact underneath it that the yard, for the first time in weeks, had one fewer name sitting comfortably in Crest's corner than it had that morning.
