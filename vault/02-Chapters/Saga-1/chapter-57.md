# Chapter 57: What the Kingdom Filed

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"Ten stones says he reads it standing," Rynn whispered.

"He always stands," Solen whispered back.

"Then twenty says he doesn't *look* at it. He memorizes it. He's the type. He'll recite the whole thing to the back wall with his eyes shut and dare us to laugh."

"That is not a bet. That's a character assassination."

"It's both," said Rynn, with dignity.

Aldric kept his eyes on the ledger in front of him and did not join in. He did not need to. Maret had walked in with a sealed sheet of good paper in his left hand, the kind with the Crown's mark pressed into the corner in wax, and had set it on the lectern without a word. He had opened the class register. He had taken the roll, without a single name spoken aloud. He had then stood, hands folded behind his back, and looked at the sheet for almost a full minute as though it were a diagram somebody had drawn incorrectly.

The room had gone quiet in stages, the way a room does when it can feel weather coming.

It had been some days since the term's last real event, and the academy had spent every one of them pretending, with great skill, that nothing was outstanding. The tournament had been settled. The attack had been discussed to death and then quietly buried under a rumor about a second-year's engagement. In the corridors, in the refectory, in the long, dull afternoons of the winding-down term, the general agreement was that the year had had its drama and would now be so good as to conclude.

Then the notice had arrived. Every class, the order said, in every academy of the realm, to be read aloud before noon.

Maret picked it up, and broke the seal, and did not look at any of them.

"By order of the Crown," he read, in the exact voice in which he read a mana diagram's title, "the investigation into the deliberate redirection of creatures within the southern provinces has concluded."

Rynn's elbow found Aldric's ribs. He did not turn his head.

"A single individual has been taken into custody. The Crown is satisfied that the individual responsible for the offences in question has been identified and apprehended, and that no further danger to the realm arises from them. Details of the offences, and of the proceedings, are sealed by order of the Crown and will not be released." Maret turned the page over. There was nothing on the back. "The earliest of the offences in question predates the present academic session. The Crown thanks the investigating officers and the public for their patience. The matter is closed."

Silence.

It was, Aldric thought, the least informative thing a person could say aloud in a room full of people who badly wanted information. It had a shape, and the shape was a door, being closed, quietly, by someone who knew exactly how it would sound.

"That's it?" Rynn breathed.

"That's it," Solen said, in a voice with nothing in it at all. "Two hundred and sixty-one words. I counted. Last year's harvest levy notice was four hundred."

"It's *shorter than a tax.*"

"It's shorter than a sale on wool," Nessa Croft said, from the row behind them, in the flat, practical tone of someone reading a price. Aldric hadn't heard her lean forward. "My mother's runner told me it would be. She said the Crown had a habit, when it wanted a thing to stop being discussed, of saying almost nothing in the most official possible way." She paused. "She said the shorter the notice, the bigger the file."

Nobody answered that.

A hand went up two rows ahead, small and hesitant, belonging to a first-year Aldric only half knew. "Instructor? Does that mean we're safe?"

Maret regarded the hand for a moment. "It means the Crown has stopped discussing it," he said. "That is a different claim. I do not answer questions the notice did not."

The hand went down slowly. Somewhere in the back, a very small voice said, "Thank you," and Maret, who did not respond to gratitude any more than he responded to anything else, turned a page.

Maret laid the sheet back on the lectern and squared it against the edge with two fingers. He did not speak. He did not look up, or offer the class a word of context or comfort or correction, and Aldric, who had spent the better part of a year learning to read a man whose reactions went nowhere but inward, found himself watching Maret's other hand.

It had gone to the pocket of his coat. It came out with the small black notebook.

Maret opened it. He uncapped a pen. He wrote, quickly, one line, in a hand too small and too far away to read from the third row, and then he did something Aldric had never seen him do in all the time he had watched him.

He underlined it. Twice.

Then, for perhaps a second, his eyes lifted from the page and found Aldric's.

It could not have been anything. Maret's gaze went over every student in that room at least once every lesson, checking the row for a diagram gone wrong. But it stopped. It stopped for the length of a single breath. And then it went on, unhurried, to Pell at the window, and to the wall clock, and to the ceiling, as though checking every place a wrong answer might hide.

"Return path," said Maret, and turned to the board. "Diagram seven. You have thirty minutes."

---

It was, in fairness, a very ordinary thirty minutes.

Aldric drew the return path. It came out clean, with all three branches and the small feedback loop that everyone else got wrong. He had a fairly precise sense of what he was doing with his hands and almost none of what he was doing with his mind, and this, he thought, was probably the healthiest state of affairs available to a person who had just been handed a notice with a wall built into it.

*The earliest offences predate the session.*

Which ones? There must have been several. There was a *deliberate redirection of creatures,* which meant plural, which meant a long list of unlucky places, and the southern provinces were not small. Somewhere on that list there was a farm, a road, a ford that had gone quiet after dusk. Whoever they were, they'd been told, in two hundred and sixty-one words, that it was over.

He drew the next branch a little harder than it needed.

"You're pressing," Solen murmured, at his shoulder, not looking up. "The line's gone thick. It'll show on the copy."

"I'm aware."

Something landed on Aldric's desk. It was a folded scrap of paper, of the kind Rynn believed to be undetectable by instructors, and it said, in large, hopeful capitals, *I OWE SOLEN TWELVE STONES. DO YOU HAVE ANY STONES.*

He passed it back without looking, with the words *no* and *pay him* added underneath in a very small hand.

"You've got the face," Solen said, apparently to the diagram.

"It's the same face."

"It's not." Solen made a small, exact mark in his own margin, apparently a correction. "You've been doing arithmetic all week and nobody's told you the answer. I'd like to say it gets easier, but I've no data."

That, at least, was true, and Aldric let it stand.

"If the notice were a diagram," Rynn hissed, leaning across the gap, "where's the missing return path?"

"Rynn."

"I'm being clever."

"Be clever quieter," said Solen.

"He's not wrong," said Nessa Croft, from behind them, at a volume that made all three of them stop.

Behind them Rynn had begun trying to sneak a look at Maret's notebook by means of what he clearly believed was a subtle stretch, and was being ignored by the notebook's owner with an expression of near-perfect neutrality. It was, Aldric thought, the closest thing to a duel the room had produced all term, and Rynn was losing it politely. At the front of the room Maret had returned to the lectern and taken up his ledger, and had not so much as glanced at his coat pocket since the pen went in.

*He underlined it.*

Aldric considered, in the abstract, how many things there were on a Crown notice to underline. He was not certain there were any. The notice had not been designed to be underlined. It had been designed to be read once, filed, and forgotten, and the only person in the room who had responded to it as though it might, in fact, be evidence of something was the one man who never responded to anything aloud.

Which meant either that Maret knew something about it that the notice hadn't said, or that it had said something Aldric hadn't heard.

He went back over the words, quietly, the way you go back over a tune. *Redirection of creatures.* *Southern provinces.* *Sealed.* *The earliest predates the present session.* *A single individual.*

*Redirection.* He stopped on it. It was an odd word. It wasn't *attack,* or *summoning,* or *release.* It described a thing that already existed, being turned somewhere it hadn't meant to go.

He waited, quietly, for the thought to finish. It had the feel of a sentence with the last word missing.

Nothing came.

---

Class ended the way Maret's classes always ended, on the last syllable of a diagram and not a moment after. The room broke apart into its usual currents. Rynn, halfway to the door, announced to no one in particular that he was owed twenty stones by a man who did not, technically, exist. Solen, walking beside him, corrected the figure to twelve.

Aldric was the last to leave, for no reason he could have explained.

At the door he glanced back. Maret was at the lectern, alone, the register open, the sheet from the Crown folded once and placed square beneath it. The notebook was gone. If it was in the pocket, it gave no sign.

Maret did not look up.

"Vane," he said.

Aldric stopped.

He had been prepared, on some level, for almost anything. A question. A reprimand about the thickness of his line. A sentence with a hook in it.

"Your third branch," Maret said, to the ledger. "It's correct."

That was all. It was the closest thing to praise Aldric had ever heard from him, and it landed the way a stone lands in a very still pond, without splash, and with a great deal of consequence spreading out from it in circles.

"Thank you, Instructor."

Maret turned a page. It was over.

Aldric stepped out into the corridor. The light lay across the flagstones in long bars, and somewhere down the hall Rynn was arguing with Solen about whether wool could technically be called a sale. It was an ordinary day. In an hour it would be an ordinary afternoon.

He had thought about a captured man for days. He had thought about his face, and his armored captor, and a sword that hadn't needed a spell, and a seat with a dash where a name should have been. He had thought about the shape of the world, and what it had room for, and whom.

And underneath all of it, unnoticed until now, a single word had been going round like a burr in a sock.

*Redirection.*

He had no reason for it to matter. It was a dull, official word, the sort a clerk uses when he does not want to say what happened. But it had felt familiar, standing in Maret's room with the notice in the air: a word he had heard once, a long time ago, in a language he hadn't yet learned to read. And Maret, who read everything, had read it first.

Aldric stood at the foot of the staircase for a long moment, holding a sentence with the last word missing, and could not, for the life of him, say why it frightened him.
