# Chapter 30: Davan's Incident

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"I could hit that post from here with my eyes closed," Rynn said, which was the exact sentence Aldric had been dreading since the drill began, because Rynn only ever said it right before proving he couldn't.

"You couldn't hit that post from here with your eyes *open*," said Davan, from two posts down, grinning in the specific way of a boy who had decided, for no reason anyone could name, that today was the day to find out.

Davan was new to Aldric's immediate orbit — a commoner, lightning-affiliated, with the particular restless energy of someone whose output had always run faster than his patience for aiming it — and Aldric had exchanged perhaps a dozen words with him across the whole term, most of them about whose turn it was to use a post. He seemed, on the strength of that dozen words, entirely good-natured and entirely capable of talking Rynn into something neither of them would enjoy explaining afterward.

"You could just ask Bavel who's faster," Aldric offered. "He watches everything. He'd know."

"Bavel doesn't rank people," Rynn said. "He ranks *posts*. It's very depressing, actually, when you think about it. I've spent a whole term being outperformed by furniture."

"The furniture has better follow-through," Davan said.

"The furniture," Rynn said with great dignity, "has never once had to hold a job down while also being on fire."

"Don't," Aldric said, to both of them, preemptively, on general principle.

"Don't what?" Rynn said. "I haven't done anything yet."

"You're about to. I can see it forming."

It had been eleven days since the notice with Rynn's name on it, six since Crest's very public, very gracious resolution of a crisis only two people in the academy knew the real shape of, and Aldric had spent every one of those days with a low, persistent watchfulness he hadn't quite managed to switch off — the specific vigilance of someone who has just fixed a broken thing and does not entirely trust it to stay fixed. Rynn's record was clean again. Aldric intended for it to remain that way through simple, boring effort, the kind that involved standing next to Rynn at inconvenient moments and saying *don't* until Rynn either listened or found a way around him.

"It's a friendly contest," Davan said. "Furthest hit from the same post, same distance, no cheating. Loser buys the winner's next lamp-oil ration."

"You don't even use lamp oil," Rynn said.

"I'll find a use for it. That's not the point. The point is I'm faster than you and I'd like it documented."

"You are not faster than me."

"I am *extremely* faster than you."

Bavel, twenty feet off, did not look up from his slate, which everyone in the yard had learned did not mean he wasn't listening. Bavel's attention worked the way a lot of things worked in this yard — invisibly, until it didn't.

"He can hear you," Aldric said.

"He can hear everyone," Rynn said. "That's not a deterrent, that's just Tuesday."

---

Solen arrived at Aldric's shoulder in the unhurried way he arrived everywhere, having apparently tracked the shape of the disagreement from across the yard the way he tracked everything, and delivered his assessment without being asked for it.

"He's going to overcast," Solen said, watching Davan roll his shoulders in the specific way of someone about to do more than the drill called for. "He's trying to beat a range mark he's already decided in his head, and he hasn't accounted for the fact that Mana Bolt doesn't reward more effort past a certain point. It rewards precision. He's about to give it effort instead."

"Should we say something?"

"I already tried. He informed me that arithmetic was, and I quote, 'not the point today.'" Solen's mouth did the small, flat thing that on him passed for despair. "I've decided to let arithmetic prove its own point instead. It's more efficient."

Davan raised his hand with the wide, theatrical flourish of a man who had an audience and intended to use it, and Rynn — unable, apparently, to let the theatrics go unanswered — raised his own hand at the same moment, and for one absurd second the two of them stood shoulder to shoulder, both aiming at the same distant post, both entirely convinced they were about to win a contest neither of them had agreed on the actual rules to.

"Same post?" Rynn said, suddenly uncertain. "Wait, are we — "

"Fire," said Davan.

They fired at the same time, which turned out to be the first mistake, because two bolts converging on the same target at the same instant did something neither of them had accounted for and Bavel, watching from twenty feet with the weary patience of a man who had clearly seen this exact miscalculation before under different names, did nothing at all to stop it.

Rynn's bolt went where Rynn's bolts always went — wide, honest, entirely unremarkable, the same scattered print he'd been producing since the first week.

Davan's did not.

He had put more into it than the post required, more than his own hand could comfortably steer, the specific overreach of someone trying to win an argument with his own output rather than his aim, and the bolt left his fingers with a crack louder than the drill's usual flat snap. It missed the post by a wide, embarrassed margin, curved wider than any bolt had any business curving, clipped the yard's low stone rim at a shallow angle, and skipped off it entirely — a pale streak of light that arced, absurdly, sideways across the whole width of the drill line before losing its force against the chalk square Rynn had, until roughly one second earlier, been standing directly in front of.

Rynn was not standing in front of it any longer, because Aldric — moving on a reflex he did not examine until well after the fact — had already gotten a hand on his collar and pulled him half a step sideways, which put both of them well clear and left the ricocheting bolt to spend the last of its force smearing a wide, harmless print across a target square that now belonged to nobody.

The yard went very quiet for exactly one second.

"That," Rynn said, into the silence, with real wonder, "went absolutely nowhere near where it was supposed to."

"Neither of you did," Solen said.

Davan stood with his hand still raised, staring at the empty air where his bolt had gone, wearing the specific stricken expression of a man who has just watched an argument he was winning turn, without warning, into evidence against him.

"That's not — I don't usually — " he started, and gave up on the sentence entirely, which was, Aldric thought, probably the most honest thing he could have said.

Bavel crossed the sand at his usual unhurried pace, looked at the smeared chalk square, looked at the low stone rim with its fresh pale scorch, looked at Davan, and said nothing for a long, uncomfortable moment that Aldric suspected was doing more disciplinary work than an actual sentence would have.

"Lightning," Bavel said finally, "does not need help going fast. It is already the fastest thing most of you will ever throw. What it needs is somewhere to go. You gave it effort instead of a destination, and effort with no destination goes wherever it likes." He looked at Rynn, who had the good sense to say nothing at all. "You. Post. You were standing in the one place a wild bolt was statistically unlikely to reach twice in the same afternoon. Don't test that a second time."

"Understood, sir."

"You." Bavel's eyes returned to Davan. "Same post. Same distance. This time aim at the post and not at your own pride. The post has never once insulted you."

"Yes, sir."

"Again," said Bavel, and walked away, and that — the whole of it, four sentences and a walk — was, Aldric understood, watching the rest of the yard exhale at once, the entire disciplinary consequence Davan was going to face for nearly setting a friend's face on fire out of sheer competitive enthusiasm. It was, somehow, more effective than shouting would have been. Aldric had come to recognise, over fifteen weeks, that this was simply how Bavel administered consequences: not with volume, but with a sentence precise enough that the boy carrying it would still be turning it over a week later.

"I owe you," Davan said to Rynn, once Bavel was safely out of earshot, sheepish in the specific way of someone whose pride had just been handed back to him slightly dented. "That was — I don't usually overcast. I don't know what happened."

"You got competitive," Rynn said, with the easy, immediate forgiveness of a man who had clearly done the exact same thing to someone else at some earlier point and simply hadn't been caught for it. "It happens. I once set my own sleeve on fire trying to impress a girl who wasn't looking."

"That tracks," Solen said.

"Nobody asked you."

"You told the story unprompted. I'm allowed to react to it."

---

They packed up for the day in the loose, relieved good humor of people who had narrowly avoided a story that would have needed explaining to an office, and it was Solen — because it was always, eventually, Solen — who noticed the thing nobody else had bothered to.

"That's odd," he said, crouching by the low stone rim where Davan's bolt had skipped off at its wide, embarrassed angle. He touched the scorch mark with one careful finger and drew it back a half-second later, faster than the heat alone would have warranted.

"What's odd?" Aldric asked.

"Mana Bolt doesn't burn. It's in the profile — everyone's been taught that since the first week. It's a fist, not a flame. It shouldn't leave a mark that smells like this."

Aldric crouched beside him. The stone did, faintly, unmistakably, carry the sharp, clean smell of something scorched — not the flat char of a fire, but something thinner, colder, gone almost as soon as he noticed it.

"Maybe it's the stone," Rynn offered. "Old stone. Reacts weird."

"Maybe," Solen said, in the specific tone he used for things he did not believe even slightly. He straightened, folded nothing, and looked at the mark for a moment longer than the explanation required. "I don't have a theory. I'm noting it because I don't have a theory, which is unusual for me, and I dislike things I can't immediately account for."

"You could ask Davan," Aldric said. "It was his bolt."

"I intend to. Later, when he's not still apologising to a post." Solen glanced once more at the faint, cooling mark, tucked it away wherever he kept the things he refused to explain, and turned toward the gate. "Add it to the list."

Aldric looked at the mark himself on his way out — small, pale, already fading in the afternoon light, the kind of thing that would be gone entirely by morning and remembered by no one except a boy who counted things for a living and hated not knowing why.

He didn't have a theory either. He filed it anyway, the way he filed everything, and told himself, without much conviction, that it probably meant nothing at all.

"For what it's worth," Rynn said, catching up with him at the gate, "today was still an improvement. Nobody's record got reopened. Nobody got hit. The only casualty was a rock, and the rock started it."

"The rock did not start it."

"The rock was *standing there*. That's practically provocation, in a yard like this."

"I'm going to tell Bavel you said that."

"You are not."

"I'm considering it," Aldric said, and let Rynn spend the rest of the walk back arguing, at length and with real conviction, for the structural innocence of a low stone rim that had, by any reasonable account, done nothing at all except exist in the wrong place at the wrong moment — which was, Aldric thought, turning it over once more before he let it go, roughly the story of the whole afternoon.
