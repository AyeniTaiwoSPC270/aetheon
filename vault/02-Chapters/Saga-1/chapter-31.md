# Chapter 31: Varen

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"You're doing the face," Rynn said, not looking up from the bread he was systematically dismantling. "The one where you're thinking about the window."

"I don't think about the window."

"You think about the window constantly. You've got a whole private relationship with that window. I'm frankly a little jealous of the window."

It had been eighteen days since the courtyard, since Solen had timed a stranger's attention down to the minute and told him, flatly, that seven of those eleven minutes had been spent on Aldric specifically. Eighteen days, and the window itself had gone dark most nights since, unremarkable, ordinary, exactly the kind of window that belonged to exactly the kind of second-year Aldric had no business thinking about at all. He'd told himself, several times, that he'd stopped.

He had not stopped.

"I'm working on it," Solen said, from across the table, without being asked, which was how Aldric had learned Solen answered most of the questions people were too careful to voice aloud. "The name. I told you I'd have one by the week's end. That was optimistic. Second-years don't advertise themselves to first-years who haven't earned the introduction, and asking directly would tell him I'd noticed him noticing you, which is a conversation I'd rather not start on his terms."

"How close are you?"

"Closer than I was. Further than I'd like." Solen folded a corner of his page, considered it, unfolded it again. "I know his year, his general standing — the sort of second-year other second-years defer to without quite being able to say why — and I know he isn't part of Halvon's circle, or any circle that overlaps with it. That's the part that's been slowing me down. I'd assumed, when I started asking, that whoever this was would trace back to Crest eventually. A friend of a friend. An older cousin doing reconnaissance on his behalf."

"And it doesn't."

"It doesn't. Not anywhere I've found, and I've looked in the places that sort of thing usually hides." Solen's flat register didn't change, but something in the pause before he continued told Aldric the absence of an answer bothered him more than an answer would have. "I don't like that. A watcher connected to Crest, I could file under *known problem, manageable shape*. This is someone operating entirely on his own account, for his own reasons, with no thread back to anything I already understand. That's a worse kind of not-knowing."

"You're saying it would be better if it were Crest."

"I'm saying Crest is a shape I've already measured. This isn't." Solen looked at him, level, the grey eyes doing the thing they did when he'd decided a point mattered enough to make without softening it. "You should sit with that, not with the window."

Rynn, who had abandoned the bread entirely somewhere in the last exchange, looked between the two of them with the specific discomfort of a man who had wandered into a conversation heavier than the one he'd signed up for.

"Is this still about the watching thing," he said. "Because I thought we'd decided it was probably nothing."

"We decided no such thing," Solen said. "You decided it, out loud, twice, in the hope that saying it would make it true. I never agreed."

"You could have said something."

"I did. You weren't listening. You were eating."

"I'm always eating. That's not a valid excuse for you to withhold information."

"It's the only excuse I need," Solen said, and went back to his page, and Aldric understood, watching the two of them, that this particular argument had already happened once before, somewhere he hadn't been present for, and had simply been waiting for a fresh audience to run again.

---

He'd tried, in his own limited and considerably less methodical way, to shortcut Solen's patience the week before, and the attempt had gone exactly as badly as he should have predicted.

Nessa had been the obvious first door — she seemed to know every collar colour in the year above her the way Solen knew lamp-bracket counts, an inherited fluency rather than a studied one — and he'd asked her, as lightly as he could manage, whether she recognised a second-year who kept to the dormitory windows at odd hours.

"That describes at least six people," she'd said, not unkindly, over the top of a book she had no intention of putting down for the question. "Half the second year keeps odd hours. It's the year they start actually worrying about their futures. Be specific or don't ask."

He hadn't been able to be specific without explaining the window, the eleven minutes, the whole uncomfortable shape of it, and explaining that to Nessa felt like handing a Croft a piece of information she would file, correctly and immediately, as gossip worth trading. He'd let the question die there, unspecific, unanswered, and told himself it hadn't cost him anything.

It had cost him something. Not much. Enough to notice.

Pell had been the second attempt, and had gone worse, in its own quiet way — a single raised eyebrow, a long pause, and the observation, delivered without any particular heat, that "asking a demi-human to name every noble you're curious about is an interesting choice, Vane, and I'm choosing not to be insulted by it today." Aldric had apologised, meant it, and abandoned the whole approach by the end of the week, having learned mostly that he was worse at this than Solen was, and that the badness itself was its own kind of tell — a first-year commoner asking pointed questions about a specific, unnamed second-year was exactly the sort of thing that got noticed, filed, and eventually traced back to the person it was meant to protect.

Solen hadn't made either mistake. Solen, as far as Aldric could tell, hadn't asked a single direct question of anyone.

---

The tournament notice hadn't gone up yet, but the corridor talk had started carrying its shape anyway — brackets, seeding, whose family would be watching from where, the specific electric restlessness of an academy that knew something was coming before anyone had confirmed the date. Aldric had caught himself, twice this week, doing arithmetic he hadn't consciously started: how much longer before whatever this was stopped being a shape at a window and became something with an actual name attached, something he could no longer pretend was simply weather.

He didn't like the arithmetic. It kept coming out the same way — not much longer, and not on his own timeline.

He found Solen alone two days later, in the library corner nobody used for studying, hunched over a stack of papers that had nothing to do with any module Aldric recognised, and something in the particular stillness of his posture told Aldric this was not, this time, a conversation he was walking into by accident.

"You look like you're building a case," Aldric said.

"I'm building several. It's efficient to build them at the same table." Solen didn't look up, but he shifted the topmost page a fraction, the small deliberate gesture Aldric had learned meant *come closer if you're going to hover*. "How did the direct approach go? I assume, since you're here instead of triumphant, that it went the way I told you it would."

"You didn't tell me anything. You told me you'd rather I not."

"That was the telling. You simply declined to hear it as one." Solen set down his pen. "Croft, or Pell?"

"Both. In that order."

"Croft would have wanted specifics you couldn't give her without paying for them, and Pell would have heard an accusation you didn't mean to make." Solen said this without any particular satisfaction, the way he delivered most conclusions he'd already reached well before the conversation that confirmed them. "You're not built for this part. You're built for reading a room once you're already in it. This required staying out of rooms entirely, which is a skill you don't currently have and I'd rather you not practise on people who matter."

"That's a very roundabout way of saying I made it worse."

"You made it marginally worse. I've made it very slightly better. On balance, the week is a wash." A flicker of something that wasn't quite a smile. "I'd call that progress, by this term's standards."

"For the tournament?" Aldric asked, nodding at the stack of papers. "Is that what all this is."

"For several things. The tournament is the least interesting of them." Solen set one page atop another, squared the corners with the same automatic precision he brought to everything, and finally looked up. "I did want to tell you — Nessa's mother has confirmed the noble bloc's viewing arrangements for the first round. Front terraces, house by seniority, which means House Croft will be seated closer to the arena floor than House Halvon this year, and Nessa is already composing the letter home about it. She's very pleased. It's the pettiest thing I've seen her care about, and I mean that as a genuine compliment to her range."

"That's — good for her."

"It is. It's also going to make Crest insufferable for exactly as long as it takes him to find a worse grievance to replace it with, which, knowing him, will not be long." Solen returned to his papers, sorting them with the same unhurried care, and added, almost as an afterthought, in precisely the tone he used for lamp-bracket counts and eleven-second intervals, "Varen, by the way. That's the name. Second-year, House unremarkable enough that I hadn't bothered learning it until this week, which was apparently the point."

Aldric went very still.

"Say that again."

"Varen," Solen said, and kept sorting, kept talking, already three sentences further into an entirely different subject — something about the terrace seating chart, the precise angle at which House Croft's bench would catch the morning sun, a complaint half-formed about whether that counted as an advantage worth Nessa mentioning in her letter — as though he hadn't just handed over, in the middle of a sentence about somebody else's seating arrangement, the single answer Aldric had spent eighteen days waiting for.

"Solen."

"Mm."

"You just — " Aldric stopped, started again, aware even as he said it that the sentence wasn't going to come out sounding as calm as he wanted it to. "That's the name. That's it. You just said it like it was the seating chart."

"It is, in a sense, part of the same list." Solen finally looked up again, and something in his expression suggested he'd known exactly what he was doing, had simply chosen to do it this way on purpose — dropped flatly, mid-sentence, robbed of the ceremony Aldric's whole body had apparently been braced to give it. "I could have made an occasion of it. Sat you down, said *I have news*, let you brace for something. I decided against it. You've spent eighteen days building this up into something with weight. I thought it might do you good to have it arrive weighing considerably less than you expected."

"It doesn't feel like less."

"No," Solen agreed. "I didn't think it would. But it's still just a name, Vane. A name is not an approach. A name is not a threat. It's a fact, the same as any other fact, and you're better at handling facts than you are at handling the shape of not having one." He tapped the page in front of him once, precise, final. "Varen. Second-year. Watches you specifically, for reasons neither of us has found yet, and does not appear to answer to Crest, to anyone in Crest's circle, or — as far as I can currently determine — to anyone at all."

Aldric sat with it — the name, small and unremarkable and entirely unhelpful on its own, exactly as Solen had said, and yet somehow larger for having a shape at last than it had ever been as a lit window and an eleven-minute count.

"Thank you," he said, eventually. "For finding it."

"Don't thank me yet," Solen said, already turning back to his papers, already three-quarters gone into whatever came next on his private list. "A name is a beginning, not an answer. I intend to keep looking until I have both."

He found Rynn later, in the common room, and said the name out loud once more, quietly, just to hear how it sounded outside Solen's flat delivery.

"Varen," Rynn repeated, testing it the way he tested everything, out loud and at volume, drawing a look from the card game by the window that he ignored entirely. "Doesn't sound like anything. Doesn't sound like a threat. Sounds like a name you'd give a horse."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be comforting, it was meant to be true. Also I don't think horses are named Varen, actually, now that I say it out loud. Maybe a minor river." Rynn considered this with real, misplaced seriousness. "A river's not nothing, though. Rivers flood."

"That's the least reassuring thing anyone's said to me all week, and I had a conversation about a Croft's seating arrangements earlier."

"I have many thoughts and most of them are useless," Rynn said, with real pride, and went back to his bread, satisfied, apparently, that the matter had been sufficiently addressed.

Aldric let him have the last word. He turned the name over once more instead, alone in the noise of the common room, and found that Solen had been right about one thing at least: it weighed less than eighteen days of watching a dark window had led him to expect. It weighed considerably more than nothing at all.
