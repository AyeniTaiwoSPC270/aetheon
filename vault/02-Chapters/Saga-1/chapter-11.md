# Chapter 11 — The Shape of the Place
### Saga 1, Arc 4

---

— Aldric Vane —

He was awake before the bell.

This was not a surprise. He had been awake before the bell his entire life — some part of him that had never learned to trust the morning to arrive without being watched for it. He lay on the bunk for a moment in the dark before first light, listening to the dormitory block settle around him. Through the wall: someone turning over in a bunk. Further down the corridor, a door opening carefully, the way doors open when people are trying not to be heard. The stone held sound differently than wood did — flatter, less resonant, everything arriving as if slightly further away than it was.

He dressed, laced his boots, and looked at the room.

Same dimensions as yesterday. Same window, same desk, same hook. The pack on the hook was lighter now — he had put away what could be put away. He had not brought much. He mapped the room once more by reflex, the way you test a knot you have already tested, and then moved into the corridor.

The dormitory block in early morning was a series of sounds and half-seen shapes — other students moving toward the washrooms, the common stairwell, the smell of something being burned in a lamp somewhere on the floor below. He followed the corridor to the stairwell and took it down.

He had twelve minutes before the first bell. He used them to walk the route between the dormitory block and the main building, which he had taken once yesterday in the direction of his room and not yet taken in the other direction. He counted the turns. He noted which corridor had the best light and which had none. He found a side passage that the intake card's map had marked as a secondary route and confirmed it was real and unlocked.

He arrived at the assembly hall with four minutes to spare and filed the route.

---

The first-year assembly hall was larger than the intake receiving hall and colder, in the specific way of rooms that were big enough to require more heat than they received. High windows on the east side let in the early light in long, flat angles across rows of stone benches facing a raised platform at the front. The platform held a lectern, a single chair, and a notice board mounted on the wall behind it with nothing yet posted.

Aldric entered and read the room in the first ten seconds.

The noble students had claimed the centre benches — not by instruction or discussion, but by the accumulated gravitational pull of people who had always sat at the centre of rooms and found no reason to stop. They moved through the space the way people move through spaces they have already decided belong to them. Conversations were already happening in the easy, shorthand register of students who had known each other at least by name before today. A boy in the front row with a dark blue collar and the posture of someone who had been told since childhood that posture was character caught Aldric's eye across the room and held it for two seconds before looking away. Filed.

The commoner students were distributed around the edges and toward the rear, in the way Aldric recognised immediately and without needing to think about it. Not together — apart from each other, occupying separate positions rather than clustering, because proximity to other commoners did not confer safety and sometimes confirmed a category that was better left unnamed. A girl near the back wall with her gaze fixed on the platform and her hands folded in her lap in the careful posture of someone who had learned early that stillness drew less attention than movement. Another boy near the east wall, standing rather than sitting, with a coiled quality about him that Aldric mentally tagged and moved on from.

He chose a bench on the left side of the hall, near the aisle, with a clear sightline to both the platform and the main door. He sat down.

His white hair drew looks. This was not surprising — it had drawn looks in Ashford, in Valdris Prime, in the carriage. It drew looks everywhere he went, and people interpreted it differently depending on what they needed it to mean. He noted the looks the way he noted the benches and the windows and the angle of the light, and did not respond to them.

The bell rang. The hall filled.

---

The staff member who delivered orientation was a woman Aldric would have estimated at mid-forties, with the particular efficiency of someone who had given the same information many times and had long since stopped finding it either interesting or tedious — it simply was the information, and she was delivering it, and she would continue to do so until it was delivered. She stood at the lectern without notes and spoke at the measured pace of someone who knew exactly how many words she had.

She covered the rules in a sequence that was probably traditional: curfew times by bell number, access hours for the common spaces, the prohibited areas — the faculty wing beyond the marked corridor, the principal's building without appointment, the lower levels without an upper-year supervisor. She covered conduct expectations in two sentences that contained the word *honour* and the word *distinction* in close proximity.

*The academy expects its students to conduct themselves with honour and distinction at all times*, she said, with exactly the same cadence as the curfew instructions, as though honour and distinction were administrative matters like bell times — which, in this building, Aldric suspected they were.

Then she covered the structure of the foundation year, and this part he listened to with more attention.

Four core modules, running concurrently: Mana Theory and Circuit Fundamentals with Instructor Maret. Combat Application with Instructor Bavel. Elemental Foundations with a third faculty member she did not name. Physical Conditioning integrated into the combat module. Assessment points at the end of each term, with a formal ranking review at the end of the year. Students who failed to meet minimum threshold at the year-end review would be placed on supervised remediation. Students who failed remediation would be reviewed for continued enrollment.

The academy was old and it had systems and the systems worked and they would learn them.

She finished, asked for questions, received none — at least none from Aldric's section of the room, though he heard a voice near the front offer something that was answered briefly — and directed the cohort to move freely to the common spaces until the morning's module assignment was posted.

The hall dissolved.

---

The first-year common space was off the main corridor, through a door that opened onto a room the academy had clearly furnished in the spirit of *functional adequacy* rather than any more ambitious principle. Tables. Chairs of varying age and matching solidity. A notice board on the far wall, blank except for a single sheet confirming that module assignment postings would follow. A window on the south side facing an inner courtyard, letting in light of adequate quality.

It smelled of old wood and something faintly mineral — stone dust, possibly, the particular smell of very old buildings that had absorbed a long time.

The sorting reconstituted itself immediately. The noble students entered the common space with the ease of people moving into their natural habitat — conversations resuming, chairs claimed with the proprietary confidence of students who had done this before in their minds if not in practice. The commoner students moved around the edges or found positions at the outer tables or, in the case of the girl Aldric had noted in the back row of the hall, appeared to have slipped out of the flow entirely and gone somewhere else.

Pell — the demi-human, lupine sub-race, the only one in the year — chose a chair near the window and looked out at the courtyard with the expression of someone who had made a decision about this room and was not going to revisit it. He was quietly enormous in the way of people who carry themselves as though they have been told their size was the problem and have decided it is not. He did not glance at Aldric. He watched the window.

Lira Voss moved through the common space efficiently, in a straight line from the door to a table near the notice board, and sat down with the quality of someone who had been the only elf in many rooms and had developed a system for it. She opened a small book she had been carrying and did not look up.

Aldric moved to the notice board.

The single posted sheet confirmed what had already been announced. He read it anyway, once, to confirm his understanding of the posting schedule against what had been said in the hall, and was standing there reading it when someone stopped beside him.

He registered her peripherally — noble, by the quality of the clothes, about his height, brown hair pulled back, no particular urgency in the way she had approached. She was looking at the notice board with the same practical attention he was.

"The block curfew," she said. Not *excuse me* first, not a preamble. Just the subject, directly. "Is it separate from the main curfew, or is the main bell sufficient?"

Aldric considered the question. He recalled the intake card — the map on the back, the small printed note at the bottom edge that he had read because he read everything on cards that were handed to him.

"Block-specific," he said. "Quarter-bell earlier than the main curfew. It was on the intake card."

She processed this. "Quarter-bell earlier."

"Yes."

She nodded, the nod of someone who had received the information they needed and was filing it, and returned her attention to the notice board. She did not follow it with a comment about his hair or his clothes or the obvious question of where he was from and how he had arrived here. She had a practical question, she had asked the nearest person, she had received an answer, and the transaction was complete.

She stepped away. He returned to the notice board.

He stood there for a moment longer than necessary.

The first person in this building who had spoken to him without an angle. Not warmth — he was not filing it as warmth. Neutrality was the correct word. She had treated him as a source of information rather than as a category, and the difference between those two things was significant enough to note. He noted it. He filed it.

He went to find the door to the courtyard.

---

It was a side door, half-visible, set into the wall beside the window where Pell still sat with his book and his expression. It opened without resistance onto a stone-flagged inner courtyard that the common space window had shown him only in cross-section.

The courtyard was not large. Four stone walls, the mountain visible over the northern roofline, two doors besides the one he had come through — one on the east side and one in the far corner, the far corner one slightly ajar. A path of lighter-coloured stone ran diagonally across the flagging from the east door to the north wall, worn smooth by many years of the same feet taking the same route. Late summer light came directly overhead, clean and even, the kind of light that made everything look the same age.

He walked to the centre of it and stopped.

He mapped it: four walls, three doors, one path, open sky. Dimensions: approximately twelve metres east-west, ten north-south. The mountain above the northern wall was a dark shape against the sky. The light was direct and unhurried. The academy was around him in every direction — below him, probably, in the carved-out lower levels; above him in the towers he had seen from the road; to every side in corridors and halls and rooms full of students sorting themselves into the positions they would hold for the next six years.

He was inside it.

He knew where the exits were.

This was not comfort, exactly. Comfort was a different thing — it involved familiarity in the emotional sense, the specific ease of a place that had absorbed you and you had absorbed in return. He did not have that yet, and he was not certain when he would. But orientation was not the same as comfort, and he had orientation. He had the shape of the place. He had curfew times and module names and a map of the route from his room to this courtyard, and the knowledge that the door in the far corner was ajar and led somewhere north.

He could work with that.

The light was warm on the stone. Somewhere above the north roofline, a bird crossed the sky and was gone. He stood there with no task ahead of him and the academy mapped and the morning still young, and let the place be what it was.

In the distance, through the stone, the bell rang.

Module assignment postings.

He turned and went back inside.
