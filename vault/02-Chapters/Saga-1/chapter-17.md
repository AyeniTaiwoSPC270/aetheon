# Chapter 17: Nessa Croft's Question

### Saga 1, Arc 4

---
— Aldric Vane —

The seat by the wall was taken.

Aldric stopped in the doorway with his bag half off his shoulder. He had chosen that seat from the corridor, by elimination: back row, farthest from the front, no window behind it, a clear line to every face in the room. He would have sworn it was empty.

It had a girl in it.

She didn't look up. Her hands lay folded in her lap, and she watched the bare table as if it might be about to say something. Aldric knew that posture. He'd seen it against the back wall of the assembly hall on his first day: the careful stillness of someone who had learned that stillness drew less attention than movement.

He had catalogued the posture. He had never caught the name.

That was twice now that the catalogue had been wrong about an empty seat. He was starting to feel it owed him an apology.

He took the next seat along. It was that or the floor.

The room was long and low, and it had plainly been furnished by someone who expected things to be spilled. One long table ran down the middle, with a stone trough of water at one end, a tray of sand at the other, and an unlit brazier squatting between them like a suspicious guest. A strip of paper hung from a hook over the window, turning slowly on a draught nobody had asked for. The benches climbed in three ragged rows.

Elemental Foundations. The one module he had walked into blind. Maret had his ledger. Bavel had his board. Nobody had told Aldric what Doven kept.

His plan was small. Sit where he could watch a teacher work before the teacher started watching him. Take a mark in the exact, dull middle of the page. He already had a board and a notebook with his name in them, and he did not need a third thing.

Yesterday hadn't helped. Bavel had made him fire one more after the others left, and *ragged* had turned out to be work. It had taken every scrap of concentration to make a bolt that ugly, like walking down a dark stair on purpose. Bavel had studied the smeared print for a long time.

"Better."

That was all he'd said before sending Aldric away. Aldric had spent the night working out whether it was an insult.

The room filled by degrees. Crest took the middle of the front bench as if it had been carved to his measurements, with Milo a careful half-step behind and Seya on his far side. Nessa Croft sat at the far end of the same bench, as far from Crest as the wood allowed, which wasn't as far as she'd have liked. Pell took the end of the second row by the window and looked at everyone in turn. His eyes stopped on Aldric a moment longer than on the rest. Then he looked at the brazier.

The door opened backwards.

A man came through it hip-first with a wooden crate in his arms, saying, "Sorry, sorry, sorry, the stairs in this building are a conspiracy—" and something small and loud slid in under his elbow.

"I'M SORRY," said Rynn. "I'M NOT LATE, I'M—"

"Slightly late," said the man. "As am I. Sit, Mr. Rynn, before we start a tradition."

Rynn sat. He landed in the second row beside Solen, who did not look at him and squared a page.

The man set the crate on the long table and stood getting his breath back with his whole face, not embarrassed in the least. His sleeves were shoved to the elbow. There was chalk on both hands and one cuff, and his coat did not match his trousers.

"Doven," he said. "Elemental Foundations. Apologies for the delay. There are eleven staircases between the supply room and this door, and I have been taking the wrong one for years. I've started calling it exercise."

Somebody laughed. It was a real laugh, short and surprised out of them. Aldric felt his own mouth move and stopped it.

Doven's eyes went along the benches and reached the back row, and he smiled like a man spotting friends.

"Ah. Good. The back row." He said it the way other men said *the good china.* "Best seats in the room. You can see everyone, and nobody's craning to see you. I sat there for six years."

Aldric had chosen the seat to hide in. Being told it was the best seat in the room turned out to be a remarkably efficient way to stop feeling like he was hiding.

"Roll," said Doven, pulling a sheet from his coat, "because I like to learn names before I learn anything else. Names are the one part of a person that's never wrong."

He read them with one small word for each, never the same shape twice. Aldric watched for the trick and was annoyed to find there wasn't one. The man simply liked them.

"Croft."

"Present," said Nessa.

"You've the look of someone who asks the useful question," said Doven. "I'll want those."

"Halvon."

"Here, sir," said Crest.

"I'm told you're very good at the answer, Mr. Halvon," said Doven. "I hope you'll get good at the question."

Crest's smile stayed exactly where it was. It just stopped reaching anything.

Doven read "Pell," and Pell lifted one hand. "Good," said Doven, and moved on without a single remark about anything. Aldric noticed that, and noticed Pell noticing.

"Essa."

"Here," said the girl beside Aldric, so quietly it barely made the distance.

"You sat where I'd have sat," said Doven. "Good instincts."

She nodded at her hands.

"Vane."

"Here," said Aldric, raising two fingers.

"Ashford." Doven looked at the sheet, then up. "They make a root oil there that gave my mother's knees ten good years. I've been meaning to thank someone from Ashford ever since. It seems to be you."

It was Maren's oil, on Wyla's shelf. It was the strangest thing anyone had said to him since he'd arrived, and the kindest, and he hadn't seen it coming.

"I'll pass it on," said Aldric.

"Do," said Doven, and went on down the sheet.

Aldric liked him. That was inconvenient. He had a system for teachers, and it did not have a slot for this.

"Before the bell there'll be an exercise," said Doven, putting the roll away. "It'll be in pairs, and I'll be taking no requests. But first. Nobody tell me what you are. I have that on paper. Tell me what you'd like to be able to do by winter."

Forty minutes, then. Aldric could be forgettable for forty minutes.

The front row answered like people filling in forms. "Whatever the curriculum permits, sir," said Crest.

"A fine answer for a form," said Doven. "Try it again next month."

A boy wanted to light a candle without the smoke. A girl wanted her father to stop asking. Doven wrote each answer down without comment. His pen came up the benches to the back.

"Essa, you may keep yours," said Doven, and she did. "Vane."

Aldric could have said anything. He had a dozen sensible answers, and every one of them was forgettable.

"Be average," said Aldric. "On purpose. Reliably."

The silence lasted one breath. Then Doven laughed, a short bark that made the front row jump.

"Ambitious," said Doven. "Most of this room wants to be terrifying, and half of them are lying about it. Yours is the first I believe outright." He wrote it down, still grinning. "Try it and report back."

It was the truest thing Aldric had said aloud since Ashford. He hadn't planned to say it. Doven had made it so easy.

"Talk among yourselves for a minute," said Doven, patting his coat and finding it empty in a way that visibly displeased him. "I've left my text on the far stair. I'd blame the staircases again, but I should own it."

He went out. The door closed behind him, front-first this time.

The room breathed out and began to talk. Then a bench scraped.

Nessa Croft had stood up.

She picked up her bag. She didn't look at anyone. She walked the full width of the front of the room, past the trough, past the brazier, past the middle of the front bench, and she neither hurried nor slowed. Every head in the room turned to follow her, like sunflowers if sunflowers gossiped.

"Nineteen paces," said Solen from the second row, not turning his head. "Halvon watched fourteen."

"Miss Croft," said Crest pleasantly. "The light is better at the front."

Nessa paused exactly as long as it took to answer him. "I'm not here for the light."

She climbed the aisle. Aldric watched her come the way you watch a wave from a long way off, with time to understand what it is and none to move. She sat down in the empty seat on his other side. He was now wedged between a silent girl and a Croft, in the exact centre of the room's attention.

His hiding place had become the stage.

"Vane," said Nessa, setting her bag between her feet. "How do you keep the bolt that small?"

Aldric opened his mouth.

"I've watched three times," said Nessa. "Yours, mine, yours. Mine's a splash. Yours is a stamp. I'd like to know how."

"You crossed the room in front of him," said Aldric. "You could have asked me in the corridor."

"I could have," said Nessa. "Then it would have been a favour in private, and I didn't want one of those. I wanted the answer." Her glance at the front bench was brief and had no heat in it. "That'll be in a letter to my mother by Thursday."

"What will she say?" asked Aldric.

"She'll ask whether it was worth it," said Nessa.

"Was it?" said Aldric.

"Ask me after you answer."

He looked at her. She wasn't performing anything. She wasn't taking a favour or offering one. It was a question, asked plainly, in front of an audience, by someone who had known exactly what it would cost.

*Luck,* said the useful voice in his head. *Say luck.* It was the easiest lie he owned. Crest had left it lying on his post yesterday.

He didn't. He had told the truth about a diagram, and the sky had stayed up.

"Nobody taught me to make it bigger," said Aldric. "There was one book, in a village with no teacher, and every exercise in it was thin. So I got good at one thing. I don't let it get away."

"That's all?" said Nessa.

"I think that's all. Everyone here pushes to make it more. I've only ever pushed to make it stay." He looked for the plainest way to say the rest, and said it. "Push less. Then watch where it goes."

Nessa repeated it under her breath, like a sum she was checking twice. "Push less. Watch where it goes."

"It isn't a secret," said Aldric. "It's just what I had."

"Those are the best kind," said Nessa, and turned to the front as if the matter were closed. She had paid a Croft's price for a sentence, and she seemed entirely satisfied with the rate.

Aldric became aware of the room.

Crest's back had gone very straight. Milo was studying the trough. Seya had turned all the way round on the front bench and did not pretend otherwise. She looked at Aldric, then at Nessa, then at the space between them, adding lines to a list that had just gotten longer. In the second row, Rynn had twisted so far around he looked like a corkscrew. *What did you DO?* he mouthed, with volume. By the window, Pell had turned as well. He studied the back row (Nessa, the girl by the wall, Aldric) like a man reading a notice he had no intention of signing.

Beside Aldric, the girl by the wall stirred for the first time since he had sat down.

She didn't look up. Her hands stayed folded, her eyes on the table, her voice as low as a page turning.

"Nobody here forgets a first mistake."

Aldric turned his head.

Six words. No inflection, no glance. She might have meant Nessa, who had just crossed the room. She might have meant him, who had just answered. She might have meant whoever made the day's real mistake, in a room Aldric didn't yet know how to read. She might even have meant herself, who had just spoken.

He opened his mouth. The girl's shoulders made the smallest movement, the shape of a door being pulled shut.

He closed his mouth.

The door opened backwards again.

"It was in my coat," announced Doven, holding up a thin, battered book. "It was in my coat the entire time. We will never speak of it."

His gaze went down the benches, reached the back row, paused on Nessa, and moved on. There was nothing in it but the smallest nod, the kind you give someone who has done a thing you once did yourself.

He set the book on the table and picked up the crate.

"Elemental Foundations," said Doven, "is mostly arguments. You all know the rules. Water puts out fire. Earth swallows lightning. Wind scatters smoke. Hard counters, the academies call them, and you've recited them since you could talk. Then there are the soft counters: the stories of the mage with enough skill to make the rule blink." He lifted a plain clay jar out of the crate and shook it. Small wooden tokens rattled inside. "So. Pairs. One of you argues the rule holds. One of you argues it can be beaten. Then you swap. No casting, no showing off. Only the argument, and whether you can find the crack in it."

"Sir," said Crest, with perfect courtesy. "Is there any provision for choosing?"

"There is," said Doven. "I've decided not to have it. The point is that you don't get to choose your disagreement. And before anyone panics: this exercise only. Next time, a new lot. Nobody in this room is stuck with anybody for a term. I'm not that cruel."

Aldric felt the shape of the room settle. Nessa on his left, the wall on his right. A Croft who had paid for a sentence and would now, by the plain arithmetic of the day, get to argue about it. The lot would have to be spiteful.

Doven put his hand into the jar and drew one token. He held it up to the light from the window.

"Vane."

Every head turned to Nessa. She gave Aldric half a nod, the nod of someone being sensible.

Doven's hand went back into the jar. It came out with a second token, and he read it with plain, uncomplicated pleasure.

"And Halvon."

Nobody moved.

"I'd like to appeal," said Aldric.

"Denied," said Doven, delighted. "Lots don't take appeals. It's what keeps them honest."

At the front, Crest turned all the way round on his bench. He looked at Aldric. He looked at Nessa, still seated at Aldric's side. Then he smiled, slowly and completely, the way a man smiles at a gift he intends to open in private.
