# Chapter 37: The Bracket

### Saga 1, Arc 6

---
— Aldric Vane —


The board wasn't even up yet, and people were already fighting over where to stand.

Aldric got there early on purpose — early enough, he'd told himself, to read his own name once, privately, and be finished feeling anything about it before anyone could watch him do it. He had not accounted for the fact that half of Greyveil had had the same idea, and the other half had brought their parents.

"Move," said a boy in Croft green, to a boy in nothing recognizable at all, "I can't see past you."

"You can't see past anyone. You're not tall."

Rynn arrived like a small localized weather event, already talking. "Tell me it's not up yet. Tell me I have time to eat something first."

"It's not up yet," Solen said, not looking up from whatever he was counting. "You have four minutes."

"That's not enough time to eat anything."

"It's enough time to regret not eating something earlier."

Aldric said nothing. He'd learned, across two terms, that the fastest way to end up in the middle of one of these was to say something reasonable in the first three seconds, and reasonable was the last thing either of them wanted from him this morning.

The arena rose behind the notice board like something that had been waiting a very long time to be looked at properly. Grey stone in tiers, climbing higher than the assembly hall, higher than anything else Aldric had seen at Greyveil that wasn't a mountain. Banners in a dozen House colors hung from the upper rim, not yet moving in any wind. Somewhere above the north entrance, workers were still fitting the last panel of a box big enough to seat forty people in a single unbroken curve of good stone and better cushions.

"That's the royal box," Solen said, following his eyes without being asked. "In case you were wondering why it's the only one with a roof."

"I wasn't wondering."

"You were about to."

A bell somewhere inside the arena rang twice, and the crowd did the thing crowds do — went instantly, uncannily quiet, then all talked again at once, louder, because a Records clerk had come out with a rolled paper under one arm and everyone had decided simultaneously that the wait was now, officially, unbearable.

The bracket went up.

Aldric found his own name the way he always found things in a crowded room — not by looking for it, but by noticing where the noise thickened. Seed four. His name sat above a box that connected, two lines down, to a name he didn't recognize, and he had approximately one and a half seconds to feel nothing about that before Rynn made a sound like a kettle.

"Fourth," Rynn said. "Aldric. Fourth."

"I heard."

"I'm ninth."

"Congratulations."

"Nobody's congratulating me, I'm playing Nessa Croft in the first round, she's going to turn my own shield spell back into a *lesson*—"

"You'll be fine."

"You don't know that."

"No," Aldric agreed. "But it sounded encouraging, and I'm told encouragement is a thing friends do."

Solen had already moved two steps closer to the board, reading it the way he read everything — once, completely, without moving his lips. "Torval's top seed."

"Good," Aldric said, and meant it.

"Crest's three. You're across from him if you both keep winning — not until the final, though. Different half of the bracket." A pause. "I'm eleventh."

"How do you feel about eleventh?"

"Unbothered. Eleven's a fine number. It's done nothing to hurt me personally." Solen tilted his head, the smallest fraction, at something further down the sheet. "Huh."

"What."

"Nothing. Sixteen names is a lot of names."

It was, Aldric thought, looking at the full spread properly for the first time. Sixteen. He found the others without trying very hard — Lirien, fifth, expression unreadable at a comfortable distance; Nessa Croft, eighth, arms folded, already looking less pleased than a moment ago because Rynn's name had just landed in her half; Davan somewhere near the bottom, loudly telling someone he'd drawn Seya and that this was, in his opinion, an outrage against a man who'd never done anything to anyone; Pell, quiet at the edge of the crowd the way Pell was quiet at the edge of most things, present and entirely unbothered by not being looked at. Lira Voss stood a little apart from everyone, reading the board with the specific patience of someone who had, at some point in her life, learned that reacting quickly in public rarely improved anything.

Torval wasn't there yet. Torval, Aldric had noticed, was rarely anywhere until he needed to be.

And near the top of the sheet, seed two, a name Aldric had never once heard in two terms at Greyveil: *Tavish Ardell.*

It meant nothing to him. It clearly meant something to several other people, because a ripple went through a cluster of second- and third-years near the arena steps the moment someone read it aloud, and one of them said, low, "House Ardell's actually entering someone this year?" in a tone that suggested this was itself the interesting part, more than the name.

Aldric filed it. Not urgently. Just — filed.

He was still filing it when the voice arrived from behind his left shoulder, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to and no further.

"Fourth seed." A man in expensive grey, older, the kind of well-dressed that assumed an audience by default. He wasn't speaking to Aldric. He was speaking to the woman beside him, who was nodding along in the specific way people nod when they've already agreed with something before it's finished being said. "Somebody's idea of a story, I imagine. The commoner boy, seeded above three actual noble houses." A small, dry laugh. "Charming, if it weren't going to embarrass someone in three days."

Aldric didn't turn around. He'd had two years of practice at not turning around.

Crest, elsewhere in the crowd, would have made him feel something sharp and specific — an old grievance in a familiar shape, easy to file under *known.* This wasn't that. This was a stranger who had looked at a piece of paper and decided, instantly and with total confidence, that the number next to Aldric's name was an administrative error rather than a fact about him.

It didn't sting the way Crest stung. It sat somewhere lower, and colder, and stayed.

Rynn, who had heard every word and gone very still beside him, opened his mouth. Aldric put a hand very lightly on his arm — not a stop, just a *not yet* — and Rynn, for once in his life, read the room correctly and closed his mouth again.

"He's wrong," Solen said, quietly, not looking at the man at all. "For what that's worth."

"I know he's wrong."

"I don't think you fully know it yet. I think you're currently deciding whether to know it." Solen's eyes were back on the bracket. "You'll get there. You usually do, eventually, and generally later than would be convenient."

Aldric didn't answer that, because it was true, and because the thing shifting into place in his chest right then didn't have words attached to it yet — only a decision, arriving whole and without ceremony, the way the pressure sensation used to arrive back in Ashford before he had a name for that either. He wasn't going to win this because of Crest. He wasn't going to win it to settle anything with Daran, four days and a lifetime away, training alone against a deadline nobody here knew existed. He was going to win it so that a man in expensive grey would have to stand in this same courtyard in three days and be *wrong,* out loud, in front of the woman who'd already agreed with him.

It wasn't a noble reason. It didn't need to be.

The rest of the morning went the way mornings before a tournament always went, apparently — a great deal of standing around, a great deal of noise, and then, abruptly, none of it, because someone official rang the bell a third time and announced that the Round of Sixteen would be run through the day in a rotating series of short bouts across four practice rings, results posted as they came, official matches beginning tomorrow.

Aldric's went by so fast he almost missed being nervous about it. His name was called for the third ring an hour after midday; his opponent was a broad-shouldered second-tier commoner named Jareth Combe, who fought like a man convinced that enthusiasm was a substitute for a plan, and who put himself directly in the path of a Mana Bolt he could easily have avoided within the first thirty seconds. It ended on a yield rather than a knockdown, which Aldric was grateful for, because it meant nobody had to watch his shell hold or fail under real pressure, not yet, not today.

He didn't see most of the other results. He heard them, secondhand, in fragments carried across the courtyard by people who had — Torval through in four moves nobody could quite describe afterward, Crest clean and fast and utterly without theatrics, Lirien over almost before anyone in the stands had settled, Rynn's own match not until tomorrow's proper draw, since apparently the Round of Sixteen results only mattered for seeding the eight who remained, and Aldric was one of them, and so, it turned out, were most of the names he already knew, and one or two he didn't.

Somewhere between the third and fourth ring, word went round that the King himself would not arrive until the day of the final — a scheduling matter, someone said, though nobody could say whose schedule, exactly, outranked a Grand Tournament — but that a royal envoy would be present from tomorrow onward, watching the quarterfinals in the crown's stead. This did nothing to lower the noise. If anything it raised it, because now every noble family in the stands had two audiences to perform for instead of one, and Rynn spent a full five minutes working out, aloud, exactly how badly he needed to not embarrass himself in front of an envoy whose name he didn't know and would probably never learn.

"He won't remember your name either way," Aldric pointed out.

"That's worse. That means I could disgrace an entire House and nobody official would even write it down properly."

"You're not going to disgrace a House. You don't have one."

"I'm borrowing Nessa's for the day. In my head. It felt like it needed to be somebody's."

"That's not how Houses work."

"It's how mine works. I invented it forty seconds ago and I'm already very loyal to it."

Solen, from three feet away, didn't look up from the board. "House Rynn's colors are going to be whatever's cheapest to dye."

"House Rynn's colors are going to be *dignity,*" Rynn said, with the specific wounded gravity of a man who had lost an argument he'd started and knew it.

Aldric let himself smile at that — small, private, the kind that didn't cost him anything — and thought, not for the first time, that whatever else this week turned into, at least it had started with this: two people who would stand in a crowd with him and make it smaller.

He found out who the eight were, and who he'd face next, on the way back to the dormitory that evening, from Solen, who delivered it the way he delivered everything that mattered — flatly, mid-sentence, about something else.

"You're through," Solen said. "Quarterfinal's tomorrow. You've got Corwin Bale."

Aldric turned that over and found nothing there. No weight, no history, no reason for the way three separate people within earshot went suddenly, deliberately quiet at the name, the way the second-years had gone quiet at *Ardell* that morning.

"Who's that."

Solen didn't answer immediately. When he did, it wasn't really an answer.

"You'll find out tomorrow," he said, and did not sound like a man who was looking forward to it.
