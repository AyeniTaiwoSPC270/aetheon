# Chapter 3 — What the Instrument Sees
### Saga 1, Arc 1

---

— Aldric Vane —

The guild hall's assessment room smelled like old paper and metal. That was the first thing Aldric noticed — a specific, unlived-in smell, the smell of a room that got used four times a year and stored quietly in between. The assessor had propped the door open and the morning air came through it in a thin line, not enough to help.

She was already set up when they arrived. Instruments laid out on the table in a row — a mana resonance plate, two calibration rods, a recording ledger open to a fresh page, a small glass vessel that Aldric didn't recognise and didn't ask about. Everything placed with the tidiness of someone who had unpacked and repacked the same kit in the same order for twenty years. She looked up when they came in, confirmed their names against a list, and pointed to two chairs against the wall.

"Written component first," she said. "Then practical. You'll each go individually for the practical. The other waits outside."

Daran sat immediately. Aldric took the chair beside him and told himself the smell wasn't bothering him.

The written component was a single sheet of questions. Forty items, covering the basics — mana channel theory, circuit function, elemental affinity identification, the F-to-S rank criteria. Aldric worked through it the way he worked through most things: quickly, with the low-grade suspicion that he was doing something wrong he couldn't identify yet. He finished before Daran, set the sheet face-down on the edge of the table, and had nothing to do with his hands.

He put them in his lap. He looked at the propped-open door. A cart moved past on the south road, the wheel catching on a loose stone and clattering once before the driver clicked his tongue at the horse.

Daran finished four minutes later. He set his sheet down with a precision that suggested he had placed every answer exactly where he intended to and didn't need to check.

"Practical next," the assessor said without looking up from where she was already marking the written results. "Daran. You're first."

---

Aldric waited outside on a wooden bench that sat against the guild hall's outer wall. The morning was warm. A dog was sleeping in the patch of shade under the water trough across the road. Two women from the eastern farms were talking near the grain merchant's doorway — something about a fence line, the kind of conversation that had been ongoing since before Aldric was born and would still be ongoing when he left.

He looked at his hands.

He put them flat on his thighs and looked at the road instead.

Daran came out eleven minutes later. His expression was the same as it had been going in — composed, specific, already somewhere else in his head. He sat on the bench without speaking and Aldric understood from the quality of his silence that the result had been exactly what Daran had prepared for it to be.

"Fine?" Aldric asked.

"Fine," Daran said. And then, because Daran could not go more than thirty seconds without forward motion: "If the formal exam follows the same structure, the practical component is the one to watch. The written is theory. The combat section is what they're actually—"

"I'll go," Aldric said, standing.

Daran stopped. Made a small gesture with one hand — *obviously, go* — and turned his attention to the road.

---

The room felt smaller with only one of them in it.

The assessor pointed him to the mark on the floor in front of the resonance plate — a worn circle of stone, scuffed by every pair of boots that had stood there before him. Aldric stood on it and tried to look like someone who had done this before.

"Relax your arms," she said. "You don't need to do anything. The plate reads ambient output."

He relaxed his arms. He breathed.

She watched the resonance plate.

For the first several seconds, nothing about her expression changed. She had the specific neutral face of someone whose job was to read instruments, not react to them — and Aldric had been watching faces long enough to know what genuine professional neutrality looked like versus the kind that was working to hold itself in place. This was the first kind. She was simply reading.

Then she checked the instrument a second time.

It was a small motion — precise, unhurried. She moved the calibration rod a fraction, read the plate again, made a note in the ledger. She didn't frown. She didn't look at him differently. When she looked up her expression was the same as it had been before.

"Thank you," she said. "That's everything I need."

Aldric didn't ask.

He didn't ask because the thing to do was not ask, because asking would mean he had noticed, and if he had noticed then it was real and if it was real then he would have to think about what it meant and he was not going to think about what it meant in a room that smelled like old paper and metal with a stranger writing something in her ledger that he was not allowed to read.

"The formal exam correspondence will come through the kingdom channel," she said, already turning a page. "Usually six to eight weeks. You'll receive it at your registered address."

"The Vane household," he said. "On the interior road."

"I have it." She made a small mark and closed the ledger. "You can send the other one back in."

Aldric said thank you — he heard himself say it, registered that it was the correct thing to say, and left the room.

---

The dog was still sleeping under the water trough. The two women had moved on.

Daran looked up when Aldric came out.

"Clean?" he asked.

"The other one's done," Aldric said. "She wants you to let her know you're heading off."

Daran stood, went inside for thirty seconds, came back out. "Clean?" he asked again.

"Preliminary," Aldric said. "No verdict yet. That's the whole point of preliminary."

Daran considered this for exactly as long as it took him to decide it wasn't worth pushing, then fell into step beside him on the south road back through the village. "The formal exam's three stages," he said. "Written, practical, combat. The combat section is weighted more heavily than the written — Ren Dallus said his brother told him the practical and combat together account for nearly seventy percent of the total score."

"Ren Dallus's brother failed the exam twice."

"That doesn't mean he's wrong about the structure."

"It doesn't mean he's right either."

Daran made the quiet exhale that meant Aldric was being technically correct in an unhelpful way, which was true. Aldric put his hands in his pockets and let the subject go.

The village went past them in the way it always did — slow and specific, every building exactly where it had always been, the same faces in the same doorways at the same time of the afternoon. Aldric had walked this road since he was old enough to be let out of the household alone. He knew every uneven stone, every fence post that leaned slightly left, the specific creak of the gate outside the widow Ferran's garden. The familiarity of it was usually a comfort.

Today it felt like something that was going to end.

He didn't say that. Daran was three sentences ahead, calculating something about the formal exam's combat assessment, and there was no way to say *I think this version of things is going to end* without it turning into a conversation Aldric didn't want to have yet.

Daran glanced at him once — a brief, sideways look. Whatever he saw or didn't see, he let it go. He went back to the calculation.

They separated at the usual corner. Daran raised a hand and turned east without looking back. Aldric watched him go for a moment, then turned toward home.

---

Dinner was ordinary. Josse asked how it went. Aldric said preliminary, no verdict, six to eight weeks. Josse nodded in the way that meant he was filing it for later rather than responding to it now, and passed the bread.

Maren didn't ask. She'd have heard it from Josse by morning if she wanted to.

After dinner Aldric helped clear the table, did the things that needed doing before the household settled, and went to bed.

---

He wasn't asleep when it came.

The pressure sensation arrived the way it had been arriving for weeks now — a gathering weight at the centre of his chest, moving outward through his arms in slow increments. He lay on his back and breathed and waited for it to do what it always did, which was build and then fade and then leave him alone.

It built.

And then his hand moved.

Not much. His fingers curled slightly — a small, slow motion, like the beginning of a grip — and then released. He hadn't done it. He knew with complete certainty that he hadn't done it because he had been lying still, not moving, specifically not moving, and his hand had moved anyway.

He looked at it.

His hand lay flat on the bedcover, the way he'd left it, fingers loose. It looked the same as it always looked. The pressure sensation was already receding, the way it always did, pulling back from his chest with the slow unhurry of a tide going out.

The assessor checking her instrument a second time.

His hand moving without him.

He put them together — both things, briefly, in the same thought — and then he did the thing he had been doing with the pressure sensation since the first time it appeared: he set it aside. He put it in the same place he put everything he didn't have language for yet, the place that held all the things he was going to deal with when he had more information, when he was somewhere he could do something about them, when the right moment existed.

*Not yet.*

The village was quiet. Through the wall he could hear the household breathing — the specific sound of people asleep, people who trusted the night to be what it was.

Aldric closed his eyes.

He went to sleep.
