# Chapter 35: What the Arc Has Built

### Saga 1, Arc 5

---
— Aldric Vane —

"They're posting it today," Rynn announced, dropping onto the bench across from Aldric with the specific breathless urgency of a man who had run somewhere he didn't need to run, "and I would like it on record that I am handling this with total composure."

"You sprinted here."

"I *jogged* here. Briskly. Under duress." Rynn stole a piece of bread off Aldric's plate without asking, which was its own kind of composure, Aldric supposed. "The board goes up at noon. Full bracket. Names, seeding, everything. Solen says the noble families start arriving tomorrow to watch the draw ceremony, which is apparently a whole separate thing from the actual matches, because nobles cannot simply attend an event, they must attend the *anticipation* of the event first."

"That does sound like them," Aldric said.

"I've decided I'm not nervous," Rynn said, in the tone of a man laying a foundation stone for a building he had already privately conceded would collapse. "I've decided that today. This morning. I woke up and chose it."

"How's it going?"

"Terribly. But I've chosen it, and I intend to keep choosing it, right up until the moment I throw up."

Solen arrived without comment, sat, and unfolded a page he had clearly already read twice, judging by the crispness of its corners. "The draw ceremony is at second bell tomorrow," he said. "I've confirmed the alternative-assessment track doesn't require attendance, which means I will not be there, which means somebody will have to describe it to me afterward in enough detail that I don't have to have watched it."

"I could exaggerate," Rynn offered.

"I'd prefer accuracy."

"I could exaggerate *accurately.*"

Aldric let the two of them run that particular argument into the ground — it wasn't really an argument, more a shape the three of them had learned to occupy together over the last several weeks, comfortable the way an old chair was comfortable — and found his attention drifting the way it had been drifting for days now, toward the accumulating weight of things he hadn't quite finished thinking about.

He hadn't spoken to Lirien since the drill a few weeks back where she'd told him, without softening a word of it, exactly what was wrong with his control. He still had the number she'd given him — eleven seconds — sitting somewhere behind his ribs, unresolved, the way a fact sat when you didn't yet know what shelf it belonged on. He caught himself watching for her sometimes across the yard or the lecture hall, not for any reason he could have defended out loud, and each time she was doing something entirely ordinary — reading, walking, once simply standing very still near the gate in a way that made him think, uselessly, of an old burst and a look she'd given the gate instead of the ring.

Torval nodded to him now, crossing the common room toward his own bench, an economical acknowledgment that had become, somehow, a fixture of most days without either of them ever agreeing it should be. Crest's cluster had gone quiet around Torval's defection in a way that told Aldric more about the yard's actual arithmetic than any board score could have.

And underneath all of it, two threads that hadn't resolved into anything yet and showed no particular interest in doing so: a name — *Varen* — that had cost eighteen days to learn and had done nothing since but sit there, unclaimed, attached to a person who hadn't shown his face again since the window; and a warning from a girl he'd met exactly once, behind the library, that the name came with a cost she couldn't yet describe. He had told Rynn and Solen the smallest possible version of the second one and neither had pushed. He appreciated that more than he'd said.

"You're doing the arithmetic face," Rynn said, apropos of nothing, which meant he'd been watching for it. "Right now. This exact second."

"I have a lot of arithmetic."

"You have a *suspicious amount* of arithmetic for someone whose actual life, on paper, is going quite well. Friends. A rival who's stopped being funny about it. A girl who insulted your control so precisely you've been thinking about it for weeks like it was a compliment." Rynn ticked these off on his fingers with visible relish. "If I had your term I'd be insufferable."

"You are insufferable regardless of whose term you're having."

"That's fair. That's very fair, actually." Rynn considered this with the air of a man conceding a genuinely good point. "Still. Objectively. Good term."

Aldric didn't answer that directly, because the honest answer — that a good term, viewed from close enough, looked exactly like a stack of things he hadn't finished dealing with, arranged so that none of them was urgent enough on its own to demand attention — wasn't the kind of thing that improved a shared breakfast. He ate instead, and let the noise of the common room fill in around the silence, and tried not to think too hard about the fact that somewhere in the yard, this week, Davan's overcast bolt had left a scorch mark on stone that shouldn't have burned at all, and that nobody — not Solen, who noticed everything, not Aldric, who'd been standing close enough to smell it — had come up with so much as a guess.

It was, altogether, an uncomfortable amount of things to be carrying quietly into a public event.

He caught sight of Lirien once on the walk to the assembly hall, threading through the corridor at her usual unhurried pace, entirely apart from the current of noise building around the tournament. She didn't look at him. She rarely did, in any way he could point to and call proof of anything. But something in the set of her shoulders, the specific economy of a girl who never wasted a movement on anything that hadn't already earned it, made him think — not for the first time, and with no better evidence than the thought itself — that whatever was about to happen in that hall, she had already worked out the shape of it well before the rest of them arrived to be surprised.

He filed the thought the way he filed most things lately, without deciding what to do with it, and kept walking.

Davan fell into step beside him near the doors, apologizing for something Aldric hadn't asked about yet. "I still don't know what that smell was," he said, without preamble, which told Aldric it had been sitting on him the same way the eleven-second number sat on Aldric. "I've asked around. Nobody's got a theory. I keep thinking somebody's going to tell me it means something and I'd almost rather they did, at this point, than nothing at all."

"Solen doesn't have a theory either," Aldric said. "For what that's worth."

"That's worth quite a lot, actually. Slightly worrying, but a lot." Davan managed something like a laugh, thin but real. "I'd rather be part of an unsolved mystery than the answer to an embarrassing one."

"You might be both."

"Don't," Davan said, "say that out loud again. Ever."

The assembly hall filled by noon the way it always filled for anything official — not eagerly, exactly, but completely, the whole first year folding itself into the benches with the specific compressed patience of people who understood that whatever was about to happen would happen regardless of how they felt about waiting for it.

Aldric had expected, in some unexamined corner of himself, that the announcement would feel like something. A drumroll. A moment. The tournament had loomed over the back half of the term the way distant weather loomed — always mentioned, never quite arrived — and its arrival, he'd assumed, would land with a weight to match the waiting.

It didn't. A senior administrator he didn't recognize read the schedule aloud in the flattest possible register — dates, venue, the order of elimination rounds, a reminder about conduct expectations during matches that seemed aimed at nobody in particular and everybody in general — and then said thank you, and left, and that was, procedurally, the entire event. No fanfare. No names read aloud. Just a schedule, delivered the way a schedule was always delivered, by someone for whom this was one administrative task among several that day.

The real event happened after, in the slow crush of bodies moving toward the corridor, when someone near the doors said the board was already up, and the crush became a current.

The notice board stood in its usual place outside the administrative wing, except that today it had been given an entire new section, framed in fresh wood that still smelled faintly of resin, with the full bracket posted under glass so that no one could tear a name down out of spite or hope. Thirty-two names. Seeded, paired, dated. Aldric found his own before he found anyone else's — a habit he hadn't chosen so much as inherited, the same reflex that mapped a room's exits before he'd decided he needed to know them — and stood there a moment doing the small, cold arithmetic of who he'd meet first, and when, and in front of how many people.

Rynn's name sat comfortably mid-bracket. Crest's, near the top, seeded high enough to suggest the faculty rated him more highly than his classroom composure this term might have suggested. Torval's name carried a small mark beside it that Solen, arriving at Aldric's shoulder a moment later, translated without being asked: "Dark horse notation. Somebody on staff has been watching him more closely than he's let on."

"You came," Aldric said. "I thought the alternative track excused you from all of this."

"It excuses me from *fighting* in it. Nothing excuses me from *watching* other people find out how they feel about a piece of paper." Solen studied the board a moment longer, his eyes moving with the particular efficiency of someone counting something Aldric couldn't see. "You're seeded fourth."

"Is that good?"

"It's high enough that the people arriving tomorrow to watch the draw ceremony will already know your name before the ceremony tells them it." Solen said this the way he said most things — flatly, as fact rather than opinion — and it took Aldric a moment to feel the actual weight underneath it.

He looked at the board again, at his own name sitting in black ink inside a numbered bracket that noble families he had never met would study before they ever laid eyes on him, and understood, in a way the flat administrative reading of the schedule hadn't managed to convey at all, that the term's quiet accumulation of things — the friendships, the enemy who'd stopped smiling, the ability that arrived without his permission and left without explaining itself, the name he still hadn't attached to a face, the warning he still hadn't found a shelf for — had just been made public and permanent in a single unglamorous stroke, framed under glass where no one could take it back down.

Somewhere behind him, in the press of bodies still filtering past the board, he heard Rynn make a sound that was either delight or nausea and hadn't yet decided which. He didn't turn to check.

He was still looking at his own name, seeded fourth, dated, official, when he noticed the second thing — a cluster near the far end of the corridor, second-years mostly, arranged the specific way people arranged themselves when they'd come to look at something rather than read it. He recognized none of them individually. He recognized, all the same, the particular quality of attention they were spending on the board, and understood, with the same cold arithmetic that had found his own name first, that at least some of it wasn't aimed at the bracket at all.

It was aimed at whichever name in it belonged to him.

He thought, unbidden, of Sable's flat voice behind the library — *his interest isn't nothing* — and of a name he still hadn't managed to attach to a face since a dormitory window, weeks back now. Varen wasn't in that cluster, as far as he could tell from here; he didn't yet have a face to check against. But it didn't take a face to make the coincidence sit badly. A public bracket, a seeded name, and a knot of second-years he didn't recognize studying it with more attention than a piece of paper reasonably deserved — it was, he thought, exactly the kind of thing a person with an unexplained interest in him would find useful to attend.

"Noble families start arriving tonight," Solen said, apparently reading some fraction of this off Aldric's face, though he had the grace not to say so directly. "Tomorrow they'll watch the draw ceremony from the terraces, and after that they'll watch everything. I'd get used to being looked at, if I were you. It doesn't appear to be stopping on its own."

"That's not comforting."

"It wasn't meant to be. I try to reserve comfort for things I actually believe."
