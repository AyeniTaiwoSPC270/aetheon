# Chapter 51: What Doesn't Add Up

### Saga 1, Arc 7

---

— Aldric Vane —

"Say it again," Rynn said, "but slower, so I can be offended properly."

"You lost a wager to Torval about a goat that doesn't exist."

"The goat exists. The goat is *hypothetical.* There's a difference."

"There is not a difference."

"There is an *enormous* difference, and I refuse to be lectured on it by a man who once told an entire yard he had several faces and meant it as a defense."

It was, by any reasonable measure, an ordinary morning — the kind Aldric had learned, across two years, not to trust precisely because it felt so completely unremarkable while it was happening. Rynn was mid-recovery from a wager he refused to explain in full, Solen was three steps behind them doing the thing he did with his eyes when he was counting something nobody had asked him to count, and the corridor toward Mana Theory was exactly as crowded and exactly as loud as it had been every morning since the term's last real disruption, two weeks and one goat-related incident ago.

Principal Solm crossed their path outside the east stair.

It shouldn't have registered as anything. Solm crossed a great many students' paths on a great many mornings — Greyveil was not so large an institution that its head could avoid the corridors entirely, and he had never, in two years, treated a hallway as beneath him the way some administrators did. He nodded to Rynn. He said something brief and warm to a pair of second-years arguing about a missed curfew that made them both laugh despite themselves. He was, in every visible respect, exactly the man Aldric had watched shake the King's hand a fortnight ago and exactly the man who had told him, in a courtyard full of overturned tables, *later, Vane, not tonight.*

Then he reached Aldric, and something in the ordinary rhythm of it changed by a degree so small that Aldric spent the rest of the corridor trying to decide whether he'd imagined it.

"Vane." A nod, precisely the same weight he'd given the second-years. "I trust the last fortnight's been kinder to you than the one before it."

"Considerably kinder. Nobody's tried to kill anyone in front of me since."

"I should hope not. We've a reputation to consider." It was the right joke, delivered at the right speed, and Solm's face did the correct things around it — the small, dry warmth Aldric had come to associate with him specifically, the same private crease at the corner of his mouth that had once found Aldric's eyes across a ceremony and held there for two full seconds before letting go.

And yet.

Aldric could not, afterward, have named the exact mechanism of what was wrong with it. Solm hadn't lingered. He hadn't looked away too fast, or too carefully avoided looking away, or done any of the specific, catalogable things Aldric had spent two years training himself to notice in other people's faces. If anything, he had been almost too correct — every beat landing exactly where a warm, busy headmaster's greeting should land, nothing missing, nothing added, the whole exchange finished and moving on again inside perhaps four seconds.

That was the wrongness, Aldric decided, turning it over as Solm's robes disappeared around the corner toward the administrative wing. Not what Solm had said. How carefully he'd said it — the specific, deliberate evenness of a man reading from a script he'd rehearsed rather than one he was making up as he went, the same evenness Aldric himself used on strangers who asked about his hair.

"You've got the face," Solen said.

"I don't have a face."

"You have several, and that one's new. What just happened?"

"Nothing. Solm said good morning."

"He said good morning to Rynn as well, and Rynn's face didn't do that."

"My face doesn't do anything," Rynn said, wounded. "My face is an open book."

"Your face is a book someone's reading aloud in a language you don't speak. It's very expressive. It simply isn't private." Solen hadn't taken his eyes off Aldric. "What did he actually say?"

"That the fortnight's been kinder than the one before it. That we've a reputation to consider." Aldric heard himself repeating it and, on the second pass, heard nothing in it worth the attention he was giving it. "It was a joke. It was a perfectly good joke."

"I believe you that it was a joke," Solen said. "I don't believe you that it was nothing."

Aldric didn't have an answer for that, mostly because he didn't have one for himself. He let it go for the length of the walk to Mana Theory, and for most of the hour after — Maret's diagram was a genuinely interesting failure this time, a three-branch loop that looked sound until you traced the return path all the way home, and it held his attention with the specific, welcome thoroughness of a problem that had nothing to do with him personally. By the time the bell released them he had almost convinced himself the corridor had been exactly what it looked like.

Almost.

He tested it at lunch, the way he tested most things he couldn't quite name — obliquely, low-stakes, in a shape small enough to survive being wrong about. Solm kept an open hour on Tuesdays for students with genuine administrative business, a fact Aldric had never once used in two years because he had never once had administrative business that warranted it. He invented some, in the loosest possible sense — a question, entirely real as questions went, about whether the tournament's unused visitor tickets from Ch. 43's clerk had ever been sorted out, a matter of no consequence to anyone, least of all him.

Solm's office door was open. He looked up from a stack of correspondence with the same unhurried warmth he gave every visitor, gestured Aldric in, and answered the tickets question — correctly, in full, with a small aside that the waiting list's current occupant had written three separate letters of increasingly baroque complaint about her position on it, "which I've shelved, appropriately, under matters no institution has ever successfully solved" — inside perhaps ninety seconds.

"You could simply give her the tickets," Aldric said.

"I could. Then I'd have to find four new tickets for whoever she displaced, and their letters would begin the cycle again at a slightly higher register of outrage. Some problems aren't solved so much as inherited by whoever's foolish enough to sit at this desk next." He said it lightly, with the specific dry economy Aldric had come to expect from him, and for exactly as long as the joke lasted, the office felt like every other conversation they'd had — unremarkable, warm, entirely without weight.

It was, by every measure Aldric could name, a completely normal conversation. Solm asked, in passing, how his friends were settling into the end-of-term rhythm. He asked, with what read as genuine interest, whether Aldric had given any thought to his second-year modules. He was, in short, exactly as warm and exactly as present as he had been in every other conversation Aldric could remember having with him.

And when Aldric rose to leave, thanking him, Solm's eyes followed him to the door with an expression Aldric couldn't fully catalogue even after turning it over three separate times that afternoon — not cold, not distant, nothing so simple as either of those. Careful. The specific, deliberate care of a man closing a door slowly enough that it wouldn't make a sound, rather than one throwing it open.

"Was that useful?" Solen asked, waiting for him in the corridor with the particular patience of a man who'd guessed exactly what the errand had really been for.

"He answered the question."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." Aldric turned it over one more time and found, at the bottom of it, nothing more solid than he'd started with — a warmth that had been entirely genuine and entirely, precisely managed, both at once, in a way he couldn't fully reconcile no matter how many times he ran the conversation back. "He was exactly as warm as always. It still felt like a door easing shut instead of opening."

"That's a specific thing to feel about a conversation regarding *tickets.*"

"I'm aware of how it sounds."

"I didn't say it sounded foolish. I said it was specific." Solen fell into step beside him, quiet for the length of the corridor, the particular quiet that meant he was weighing something before committing to it aloud. "For what it's worth, I've noticed nothing. Not in his face at the ceremony, not since. If there's a pattern, I haven't caught its edge."

"Maybe there isn't one. Maybe I'm inventing a mystery because the last fortnight's had an unusually large hole where the mysteries used to be."

"That's possible." Solen didn't sound convinced by it, and didn't pretend otherwise. "It's also possible you're the only person in this corridor who's spent two years getting unnaturally good at reading rooms, and you just read one correctly and don't like what it told you."

Aldric didn't have an answer for that either, and the two of them walked the rest of the way to the common room in the kind of silence that wasn't quite companionable and wasn't quite anything else, just full — the specific fullness of a question with nowhere obvious to go.

He thought about raising it with Rynn, once they'd rejoined him, and decided against it within the length of a single breath. Rynn would worry, loudly and at volume, about a thing that had no shape yet, no evidence beyond a feeling Aldric couldn't even fully justify to himself, and worrying at volume about nothing was precisely the kind of energy Rynn least needed spent on his behalf this close to the end of a term that had already cost him more than his share of peace. Solen, at least, could hold an unresolved thing without needing to do anything about it. Rynn held nothing quietly; it wasn't a flaw, exactly, just a fact, the same way water wasn't flawed for finding the lowest point in a room.

Rynn, predictably, wanted the whole story the moment Aldric rejoined them in the common room, and predictably found the tickets themselves far more interesting than anything Aldric was actually turning over. "Three letters of *increasingly baroque complaint,*" he repeated, delighted. "I want to meet this woman. I want her to write letters about my life."

"You'd hate it within a week."

"I'd hate it within a *day,* but it'd be a good day. That's the secret nobody tells you about complaining professionally — it's exhausting, but it's exhausting on *purpose,* which is a completely different feeling from the exhaustion you get for free." He considered this with the specific gravity he brought to observations he'd clearly only just invented. "I should write a strongly worded letter about something. Establish myself early."

"About what?"

"I don't know yet. That's the beauty of it. The grievance comes later. First you commit to the register."

Solen, unmoved, hadn't stopped watching Aldric since he'd sat down. "You still haven't told me what he actually said in there."

"I told you. Tickets. A joke about inherited problems."

"And the careful part. Was it still there."

Aldric considered lying, briefly, and discarded it — not out of any particular virtue, but because Solen would know within a sentence and the correction would cost more than the honesty. "Yes."

So he let it sit. He had done this before — filed a wrongness away under *not yet, not enough to act on*, and told himself, the way he always told himself, that he'd know when it mattered. It had worked, more or less, for two years. It had worked right up until the moment it hadn't, in an Ashford guild hall and a training yard and a courtyard full of overturned tables, and some small, honest part of him understood, letting this particular wrongness go the way he let all the others go, that the pattern of *not yet* eventually resolving itself into *too late* was not actually a pattern he had ever once successfully broken.

He let it go anyway. It was, he told himself, the only sensible thing to do with a feeling this small and this unprovable — and telling himself that cost him something on the way down, a small, specific weight settling somewhere he'd have to carry it, whether or not he ever worked out what it actually was.
