# CHAPTER 54 — "WHAT HE CARRIES BACK"

## Plan

**Recap (Ch. 53):** Orin Vael, POV, read the capture and Voss's inexplicable control, noted a white-haired boy on a rise and judged it meaningless, and signed the investigation closed without believing it.
**Position:** The evening after Aldric's hillside errand (Ch. 52). Aftermath chapter plus the Lirien thread's single small increment (`arc7-replan.md`).
**Characters:** Aldric, Rynn, Solen, Lirien.
**Rule 9:** Want: process what he saw without letting it show. Obstacle: nobody to tell (Rynn and Solen would want a story with an ending). Outside pressure: ordinary academy life carries on around him. Mid-chapter reversal: in the library corner he finds Lirien already there and recognizes it isn't the first time. Turn: he notices the pattern consciously; neither asks nor explains.
**Ending hook (Reversal):** what he came to carry alone quietly becomes something he isn't carrying alone. Hook ledger: follows Question (53).
**Spell Gate:** none. **New canon:** none.

## Draft

— Aldric Vane —

"You went to the post house," Rynn said, "and came back looking like the sky had insulted you personally. I need details."

"I posted a letter."

"That's not details. That's a receipt." Rynn dropped onto the dormitory step beside him with the total confidence of a man who had never once been refused a seat. "Did the clerk say something? Was there a line? Did you cry over the letter? You can tell me. Solen's not here, so it's only me who'll judge you."

"I'm here," Solen said, from two steps above, without looking up from whatever he was counting. "I'm judging in advance. It saves time."

Aldric looked at the two of them and felt the story sit in his mouth like a stone he'd swallowed the wrong way.

There was a version he could tell. A man in irons, a hillside, some soldiers, a very upright person in white armor. He could feel exactly how it would go, the way you can feel a joke landing before the punchline is out. Rynn would say *and then what*. Solen would say *and then who*. And Aldric would say *and then it was over*, and they would both look at him with the same expression, waiting for the rest of it, because a story was supposed to have a rest of it.

He didn't have a rest of it. He had a face.

"It was a long letter," he said. "I was very moving."

"Liar," Rynn said, delighted.

"It's efficient," Aldric said, and stood up before either of them found the seam.

---

Dinner was loud, which helped, and then dinner was over, which didn't.

He had a plan for the evening. It was a short plan: walk somewhere quiet, sit down, think about a man's face until it either meant something or stopped. Aldric had made worse plans. He'd made several in the last month that involved running at things.

The trouble was that the academy had no interest in his plan.

A second-year with a stack of ledgers walked straight through his path and apologized to a pillar. Two first-years argued about whether the tournament bracket had been rigged, in the loud, careful voices of people who hoped to be overheard. A notice on the east board announced, in Bavel's handwriting, that the yard would be closed for resurfacing on the following morning and that anyone found in it would be resurfaced with it. Somebody's kettle was screaming in a stairwell. Life at Greyveil went on, thorough and cheerful and indifferent, exactly as if a man hadn't been walked out of a valley at dawn in front of a single, silent witness on a hill.

He found, halfway across the lower court, that he'd stopped being angry about it. It wasn't the academy's fault. Nobody here knew. That was the whole problem, tidily stated, and it didn't feel any better for being tidy.

*Quiet,* he thought. *Somewhere with a wall.*

His feet, which had opinions of their own, took him to the library.

He made it as far as the foot of the gallery stairs before Solen caught up with him. Solen never ran. He simply arrived, the way weather did.

"You ate four bites," Solen said.

"I wasn't hungry."

"You're always hungry. It's a structural feature of you." He fell into step, hands behind his back, eyes on the floor a few paces ahead as though it might try something. "Rynn thinks you're being mysterious for fun. I've told him it isn't fun. He's decided that means it's worse mysterious, which is an improvement on his previous position."

"Solen."

"I'm not asking." He said it flatly, like a man reading a notice. "I want that noted. I've asked you a great many things this term and been told 'fine' by a person who couldn't have been less fine. I've retired the question. I'm just pointing out that I noticed, and that it's on the record, and that if anything you saw today has a shape you can say aloud, I'll be somewhere with a door that closes."

It was, Aldric thought, the kindest thing anyone had said to him all week, delivered in the tone of a man reporting a broken hinge.

"There isn't a shape yet," he said. "There's just a face."

"Then keep the face," Solen said, "until it grows a shape. I'll count in the meantime." He stopped at the stairs. "The arithmetic one. You're doing it again."

"I have several faces."

"You have one. It's just very good at looking like several." And he was gone before Aldric could produce a rebuttal, which was, Aldric considered, exactly the sort of exit a person built for himself when he wanted to leave a kindness where it had landed.


---

The corner was where it always was: the far end of the second gallery, behind the shelves nobody read because nobody was assigned them, where the ceiling dropped and the lamp had been put by someone who understood that people who came here wanted to be found only on purpose. Two chairs. A table with a ring burned into it from some long-dead student's mistake. A window the color of slate.

He had already stepped past the last shelf before he registered that the lamp was lit.

Lirien sat in the far chair, a book open in front of her, one hand resting flat on the page as if she were holding it down against a draft. She did not look up.

Aldric stopped.

Every instinct in him produced the same sensible instruction, which was to turn around. He had done it before, in doorways and on staircases, the small, dignified retreat of a person who has walked into someone else's silence. He was good at it. He had a whole technique.

He stayed where he was, and something odd happened in the back of his mind. It was the sensation of a drawer sliding open on its own.

*Not the first time.*

He tried, for a moment, to be reasonable about it. There were, after all, only so many quiet corners in a library, and Lirien was a person who valued quiet with the enthusiasm other people reserved for money. A pattern of two was a coincidence. A pattern of three was a habit. A pattern of more than that was something he did not currently have the vocabulary to call by name, and he had a very good vocabulary.

He tried to remember the last time he'd come to this corner and found it empty. He went back through the term, through the evenings he'd carried a problem up the stairs like a sack of flour, and the lamp was always already lit. He'd assumed the lamp was a feature of the room. He had, he realized, spent the better part of a year mapping every exit, every angle, every second-year who might be watching from a window, and had never once noticed that the one person in the school he couldn't read kept turning up in the place he went to be alone.

It was, he thought, an extraordinary failure of tradecraft. It would have gotten him laughed out of a village he no longer entirely had the right to miss.

Lirien turned a page.

He could ask. *Do you come here often* was, at least, the right kind of stupid. *Did you know I'd be here* was worse, because it had an answer, and he wasn't sure he wanted it.

"I can go," he said instead.

She didn't answer. She didn't look at him. Her left foot, under the table, moved out a few inches, and the far chair, the empty one, the one that had been tucked neatly in, slid back from the table with a small, dry sound against the stone.

Then the foot went back where it had been, and Lirien went on reading as though nothing at all had happened, which was the kind of thing she did so well that it was practically a signature.

Aldric looked at the chair.

He was aware that this was not a large gesture. In another person, in another room, it would have barely counted. It was a chair, moved four inches, by a girl who had never once, in a year of watching, said a thing she didn't mean.

He sat.

The library breathed around them: a shelf settling, a far-off cough, the small industrious noise of a lamp doing its best. He put his hands on the table and looked at the burn ring for a while, and for the first time all evening he did not think about the face. He waited for it to arrive, the way it had in the post house and on the road and all through dinner, and it didn't come.

That was strange. He tested it, carefully, the way you test a tooth. The face was still there. He could reach it if he wanted to: a tired, ordinary, relieved man, looking at a lantern in the way people look at a thing that has finally stopped being a threat. It hadn't gone anywhere.

It was just that, for the moment, he wasn't holding it alone.

"It's a very large amount of nothing you're not saying," Lirien said.

She still hadn't looked up. Her voice was as level as the frost she cast, and every bit as exact.

"I wasn't aware I was saying it loudly."

"You weren't. That's what makes it loud."

He should have had something dry ready. He had a great many dry things, kept for exactly such emergencies. What he had, at present, was a stone in his throat and a very small, very unhelpful urge to laugh.

"I saw something today," he said. It came out more honestly than he'd planned. "I don't know what it was. I don't think it was about me. I keep thinking it was about me."

Lirien turned another page.

"Do you want an assessment," she said, "or do you want me to keep reading?"

He thought about it. It was, all things considered, the most generous question anyone had asked him in a month.

"Keep reading," Aldric said.

"Good."

She did. He reached behind him for the nearest book, purely so his hands would have an alibi, and opened it somewhere near the back with the air of a man who had been meaning to read this for weeks.

It was a survey of municipal drainage in the southern provinces.

He read the first paragraph with great seriousness. He read the second with slightly less. Somewhere in the third, a sentence about the gradient of culverts made him think, without any warning whatsoever, of a very tired man in the back of a cart, and he turned the page a little too quickly.

"It's a good book," Lirien said, not looking up. "Sluices are underrated."

"I was just about to say that."

"You were on page two hundred and eleven. It's the appendix."

He closed the book and set it very gently on the table, as one might set down something that had betrayed him. It was the closest thing to a laugh he'd managed all day, and it came out through his nose, and Lirien, without a flicker, turned another page as though she had not heard it, which was how he knew she had.

Neither of them said anything for what might have been an hour. Somewhere in that hour the lamp guttered and steadied, and he understood that he would remember this evening, and that he would not be able to explain to anyone, Rynn least of all, exactly why.

When he finally stood to go, she didn't look up, and she didn't say anything. The far chair stayed exactly where she had moved it. She didn't push it back.

He noticed that on the stairs, and noticed himself noticing. He had walked into that corner carrying a thing he meant to turn over alone, in the dark, the way he turned over everything. He was walking out of it with the same thing, exactly as heavy, and the strangest sensation that somebody had been holding the other end.

Nobody had asked him a single question. It was the most thorough interrogation he had ever survived, and he had absolutely no idea what he'd confessed.

# CHAPTER 55 — "TWO THINGS"

## Plan

**Recap (Ch. 54):** In the library corner Aldric noticed that Lirien is always already there, said nothing, and left carrying the same weight with the sense someone had held the other end.
**Position:** Dawn after Ch. 54. The internal reckoning chapter: the captured man's face and the armored figure.
**Characters:** Aldric, Bavel (opening), Rynn.
**Rule 9:** Want: make sense of what he saw well enough to set it down. Obstacle: it won't resolve. ## Draft

— Aldric Vane —

"You look like a man with something to think about," Bavel said. He held out a rake. "Think it with this."

Aldric looked at the rake. It was a very good rake, iron-toothed and long-handled, and it had the air of an instrument that had ended more than one difficult conversation.

"Instructor, I don't recall volunteering."

"You didn't. The yard's closed for resurfacing. Somebody has to pull up the old sand before the crew comes at noon, and you were standing in my doorway at first light looking for a place nobody would find you." Bavel's face gave away nothing at all. "This is a place nobody will find you. Congratulations."

"That's very generous."

"It's very practical. Rake it into rows. Keep the rows straight. Don't leave anything under the surface I'd have to explain to the crew." He was already walking away, wiping his hands once on his rag, which Aldric noticed and chose not to think about. "Noon, Vane."

The yard was empty and enormous and smelled of cold stone and old scorch. Aldric took the rake, and the sand began to argue with him.

He raked for a while in respectable silence. The sand had been walked into that yard by a great many frightened first-years and had absorbed, in return, a great many frightened first-year secrets. It gave none of them back. It was, he decided, the most discreet thing at Greyveil, and he'd have hired it.

---

It turned out that raking was very good for thinking, in the same way that being hit by a cart was very good for clarifying your priorities.

The trouble was the pattern of it. Pull, step, pull, step, a long grey ridge of sand curling up behind the tines, and the yard laid itself out in front of him in orderly rows while his mind, unattended, went straight back to the hill.

*Two things,* he thought. It was a habit he'd learned from Maret, over a diagram with a missing return path: when a mess wouldn't resolve, stop looking at the mess. Find the two smallest pieces and set them side by side. See which one refuses to fit.

He had two pieces.

The first was the man. Not his crime, or his name, which Aldric didn't have. Just his face, kneeling in the trampled grass. Tired, and thin in the specific way of someone who has been walking for too long, and looking at the soldiers with something that was not defiance and had stopped, somewhere underneath, being fear. It had taken Aldric most of a day to put a word to it. *Relieved.* The man had looked like someone who had finally been allowed to put a heavy thing down.

The second was the armor.

White, with a grey trim the color of a blade left out in the rain. A figure standing apart from the rest, not calling orders, not moving, and yet somehow the axis on which the whole valley turned. A ring of soldiers, trained and armed and plainly dangerous, and every one of them had kept a little of their attention pointed at him, the way iron filings keep a little of themselves pointed at a lodestone.

He hadn't cast anything. Aldric had watched for it, out of habit and something like hunger. Not a flicker. He had simply stood there with a plain sword on his hip, wearing the kind of authority that didn't need a spell to hold it up.

Pull, step. The rake caught on something and he freed it. A ridge of sand, straight as a ruler.

He set the two pieces side by side, as Maret would have. And he waited, with the specific patience of a person who has been told that the answer will come eventually and suspects it will arrive at the worst possible time.

---

"You're doing it wrong," Rynn said.

Aldric didn't jump. He'd been aware of Rynn for the last forty paces, in the way you're aware of a wagon rattling up a hill. "You can't see the rows from there."

"I can see that they're tidy. It's suspicious. Nobody rakes that tidily when they're happy." Rynn perched on the low wall that ringed the yard, holding two rolls in a cloth, one of which he was clearly planning to eat regardless of what happened next. "Solen told me not to ask. I want it on record I'm not asking. I'm bringing bread. It's a completely different act."

"That's very legalistic."

"I'm learning from the best." He tossed a roll. Aldric caught it left-handed without breaking the rhythm of his rake, and Rynn made a sound of profound wounded pride. "Do you know how irritating you are? I brought you *food* and you've made me feel like the clumsy one."

"You are the clumsy one."

"I'm the *loud* one. It's a completely different qualification." Rynn bit into his roll, chewed, swallowed, and said, much more quietly than usual, "Whatever it is, you don't have to have it worked out before you say it. I'd like you to know that. I'm not smart, but I've got a very large pair of ears and nothing much in them."

Aldric stopped raking.

It was such a Rynn thing to say that for a moment he simply stood there, the rake propped in the sand, holding a roll and a stone in his chest that had suddenly gotten a great deal lighter and a great deal heavier at once.

"I know," he said. "Thank you."

"Good. Enough emotion. I have to go be somewhere else." Rynn slid off the wall. "Bavel's crew is coming at noon and I heard they've got a *cart.* I want to see the cart." He went, loudly, in the wrong direction, and corrected himself two turns later without any sign of embarrassment.

Aldric picked the rake back up.

Pull, step.

---

The answer came without any drama, the way a splinter finally works itself out of a thumb.

It was not that the two pieces didn't fit. It was that they fit *backwards*. Each one pointed the opposite way from the other, and he had spent a day and most of a night trying to make them point in the same direction, out of a stubborn conviction that pieces of the same event were supposed to agree.

The man's face was a warning.

He could see it now, quite plainly, laid out like a diagram. A man with a gift that answered to somebody else's will. A man who had been used and used, and had walked into a valley and felt, when it was over, the first honest thing in months. Nobody had asked him to. Nobody had needed to. The world was simply a place where a person could be turned into a tool, and hollowed out, and put down when he stopped being useful, and the only thing anyone would say about it afterward was that the investigation had concluded.

That was the shape of the thing. A face at the end of a rope somebody else was holding.

And the armor was a permission.

He stopped raking. This one arrived slowly, like a light being brought into a room, and it took him a moment to recognize what it was he was looking at.

The figure in white had not needed a spell. That didn't prove he couldn't cast. But he hadn't needed to, in a valley full of people who could, and that was the part that mattered. Aldric had spent a year assuming that the world ran on power in the way he understood it: circuits, channels, output, the hair-thin threads he'd been drilled to hold. And the world had shown him, from a hillside, at a very great distance, that it didn't only run on that. It had room in it for other kinds of strength. It had a place, a very large one, for the person who stood up straight with a plain blade and let the work speak.

*Daran.*

He said it in his head, carefully, the way you set down something you've been afraid to drop.

Daran had stood in a hall in Valdris Prime and heard a list read that did not include him, and had walked out before Aldric could turn around. Aldric had spent every day since carrying that as a debt. He'd rehearsed a dozen versions of it. *I should have looked for him. I should have said something. I got the door, and he got the wall, and I let it happen.* He'd assumed, in the flat, unexamined way you assume the ground is solid, that Daran had walked into a dead end, and that every day since had only made the end longer.

But Daran had always, ever since they were children, given his wanting one shape. A blade. A yard. A person who stood up straight and let the work speak. If he was anywhere, Aldric would have bet the last of his coin on steel in his hands, becoming the sort of person the world made room for.

It wasn't a dead end. It had never been a dead end. It was just a different road, and Aldric had been so busy feeling guilty about the fork that he'd never once looked to see whether the other branch went anywhere.

He breathed out. It felt as if he'd been holding it since the exam.

*He's all right,* he thought, and did not know whether he believed it or only wanted to. *He's not all right, probably. But there's a place for him. There was always going to be a place.*

He didn't examine the relief. He'd learned, this year, that examining a good feeling too closely was an excellent way to lose it, and he had very few of them.

Pull, step. Sand curled behind the tines. Somewhere behind the stable wall a bell was ringing the hour, and a small, dry, entirely reasonable voice in the back of his head observed that he was two-thirds done and that the crew would be here at noon, and he set his shoulders and picked up the pace.

---

He was nearly at the far wall when the other half of the thought caught up with him.

It came from the same direction as the good one. That was the worst of it. It arrived on the heels of the relief, the way the second bolt of lightning follows the first, and it was made out of exactly the same material.

*The world has room for people who can't cast.*

*The world has room for people who can be used.*

The same fact. The same door. He had just spent an hour convincing himself that there was a wide, generous space out there for a boy with the wrong kind of hands, and it hadn't occurred to him until this moment that a space that size had to be shaped by something. That the room the world made for Daran was made, in some very real way, by the same world that had walked a man into a valley and hollowed out his choices. That wide roads had wide ditches. That any place with room for a Daran also had room for a Drel Voss, whoever he'd been, and a shelf for the people nobody had looked for afterward.

And Aldric had been placed, on the day of the exam, in a very particular slot in that machinery. The examiners had doubled back. Maret had written something in a notebook. Somebody had sent five people in tournament uniforms to a courtyard, and one of them had looked at him on the way out.

He had assumed, all year, that he was on the outside of the thing, looking in. A witness on a hill.

It occurred to him, standing in a half-raked yard with an iron rake in his hand and the sun just clearing the wall, that he had absolutely no way of knowing how close to the machinery he already was. Or whether he'd been standing inside it since before he could walk.

At the gate, the crew's cart rattled into view, loud and cheerful and entirely unaware of him.

Aldric finished the last row. It was perfectly straight.

It didn't help at all.

# CHAPTER 56 — "THE SEAT WITH NO NAME"

## Plan

**Recap (Ch. 55):** Raking the yard, Aldric decided the man's face was a warning and the armor a permission, and ended with the chill that the same world that made room for Daran had room to use and discard people.

## Draft

— Aldric Vane —

"You're late," said the clerk.

"The notice said by the end of the week."

"The notice said *by the end of the week*," the clerk agreed, in the voice of a man who had been waiting to be contradicted since breakfast, "and it is, as you'll observe, the end of the week." He pointed at the clock with a stamp. It was the only weapon he seemed to own, and he held it like a man who knew what it could do. "Sixteen minutes. I've had to enter it as a *tardiness.*"

"Is that a real category?"

"It is now."

He took out a fresh ledger sheet, ruled a line across it with a steel edge, and wrote the word *TARDINESS* at the top in careful capitals. Aldric had the distinct impression of watching a man found a small nation.

"Do I get a certificate?"

"You get a *record,*" the clerk said, with something close to pride. "Certificates are for people who do things right."

Aldric set the box on the counter. It held everything the Grand Tournament had lent him and now wanted back: a competitor's sash, a badge with a number on it that had, at one point, been the most important number in his life, a pouch of unused visitor tickets nobody had ever come to claim, and a sheaf of printed sheets with his own name on them, in several typefaces, none of which he'd been asked about. He'd been told to return it to the records office by noon. He had, in fact, been told this on three separate occasions, by three separate people, each of whom had used a slightly different tone of voice to imply that he would forget.

He had forgotten. Ordinarily this would have bothered him.

This morning, with the yard raked and the sand gone and a face he still couldn't put down, it felt like a mercy. A small, dull errand. A counter, a clerk, a stamp. Nothing on the other side of it that could possibly turn out to matter.

"Sash," the clerk said, and took it. "Badge." He weighed it in one palm like a man testing fruit. "Tickets. All four unused?" A pause, and a quick, involuntary look up. "Nobody came?"

"It was a short guest list."

"Mm." The clerk wrote something. It was probably not unkind. "Sheets. I'll need you to check these against the master before I can sign you off. Standard procedure, in case the printers made an error." He slid the sheaf back across the counter and flipped open a great slab of a ledger with the other hand. "Read them. Tell me if anything's wrong. If nothing's wrong, say *nothing's wrong* and sign. If something's wrong, say *something's wrong,* and I will be forced to think about it."

"I see the appeal of the first option."

"Everyone does."

"Is there a third option?"

"There is no third option. I've checked. I checked at length. It's why the chairs are hard."

---

There were eleven sheets. Aldric went through them at the speed of a man who wants to be finished: his bracket, his match order, his badge number, the competitor list with his name lodged awkwardly among a great many better-bred ones, a sheet for the Final that had the wrong hour on it in one corner and had been corrected in ink by someone with a very steady hand. Everything was in order. He was reaching for the pen when his eye slid off the bottom of the ninth sheet and caught on a table he hadn't expected.

*NOBLE COUNCIL TIER: SEATING (SEMIFINAL).*

It had ended up in his set by accident, he assumed. A stray. Someone had filed the wrong tier's chart in with a competitor's packet, and it was going to become somebody's entire afternoon.

He should have turned the page. It was, quite precisely, none of his business. He held it, for a moment, in the deliberate, careful way of a person handling something they had already decided not to read.

Then he read it.

It was tidy work. A grid, five rows deep, the seats numbered left to right across the curve of the tier. Beside every number, a name and a House in the same square, upright print. *A delegate of the river trade. The steward's deputy and guest. The envoy's secretary.* Every seat a person, every person a seat, the whole tier laid out like a very expensive game of draughts.

He was not looking for anything. He wanted that clear, later, when he tried to explain it to himself. He was doing what he always did with any room: counting. Rows, seats, exits, who sat beside whom. It was a nervous habit, the sort of thing a man did with his hands. And at some point during the counting, quite without permission, his eyes went to the third row back from the envoy's box.

He had spent two matches looking at that row. He knew, without needing to check, which seat it was: the ninth from the aisle, three rows behind the box. He'd learned it the way you learn the location of a sore tooth.

He found the ninth seat on the chart.

There was a number. There was a small square beside it.

The square was empty.

Not blank, in the way a printer's error is blank, a gap or a smudge or a place where the ink ran dry. It had a border, like every other square. Inside it, set in the same neat type as everything around it, was a single dash. A small, deliberate horizontal line. The kind of mark a person makes when they mean *nothing goes here*, and want everyone to know that they knew.

Aldric read it again.

He turned to the tenth sheet. It was the Final's chart, printed on the same grid, in the same hand. The ninth seat, third row back, carried the same border and the same dash.

*Clerical,* he told himself. *A seat that was cancelled. A guest who never came.*

But a cancelled seat would have said so. It would have said *withdrawn*, or *released*, or carried a line through a name. There was no name to strike. This seat had not been given up, or reassigned, or lost. It had been printed, twice, on the official record of an event a king had attended, and both times it had been very carefully, very officially, given to no one.

And someone had sat in it.

He had seen it. A man in expensive grey, unhurried and unreadable, watching the yard the way no one else in that tier was watching it. Watching *him.* Aldric had felt the attention like a hand between his shoulder blades. He had looked up in the semifinal and found the seat occupied, and looked up in the Final and found it occupied, until it wasn't, and in every case been so thoroughly convinced of the seat's reality that it hadn't occurred to him to wonder whether it existed.

There was one more detail, and it arrived a moment late, the way the last stair in a dark house does. Every other seat in that tier had carried a cushion. Council blue, tasseled, rather too plump for a stone bench. He'd noticed them in passing during the semifinal and thought only that the nobility took its comfort seriously. He had never once noticed that the ninth seat, three rows back, had not had one.

The man in grey had sat on bare stone, perfectly upright, for the length of a match. And Aldric hadn't seen it, because the whole of his attention had been on the man's face.

*Ask,* said a small, cold voice at the back of his mind, and it sounded suspiciously like Solen.

*Don't,* said another, and that was his own.

"Something wrong?" the clerk said, without looking up.

"I don't think so." He was pleased to find his voice entirely steady. "There's a chart in here that belongs to the Council tier. I don't think it's meant for me."

"Ah." The clerk held out his hand for it, then paused, and squinted, and took the top sheet back from Aldric with a small sigh. "Yes, the printers do that. The Steward's office sends everything in one lump, and they feed it through as one lump, and I have been *telling* them." He glanced at the chart, briefly and without interest, in the manner of a man who has looked at a great many charts. "Seating's issued against slips. Every seat. If no slip, no name, and if no name, the seat doesn't print. Steward's rule. Otherwise the cushions aren't paid for."

"The cushions."

"They're expensive cushions. It's a Council tier." He tapped the chart, above the ninth seat, without seeming to see it. "Nothing to it. Every square's a slip."

"And a square with nothing in it?"

"That would be a slip that was never written." The clerk looked at him, as though only now noticing he was there. "Which can't happen. Nobody sits without a slip. If anyone had been sitting there they'd have been turned away. I'd have heard about it. The Steward's office reports every seat problem to me, in writing, at length, three times."

"Of course," Aldric said. "That's very thorough."

"Who signs off on those? The Steward's office?"

"A deputy. A very thorough man. Do not go asking the Steward's office things, Vane." The clerk lowered his voice, as though the wall might be taking notes. "They'll send you a form to ask the question. Then they'll send you another form for the answer. I once waited a full term for a receipt for a receipt."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It's a nightmare." The clerk stamped the ninth sheet with something like tenderness. "There. That's you finished. You may be released into the world."

Aldric looked at the stamp, and then at the small initials beside the dash, which he had not noticed before. There were two of them, in a firm, unremarkable hand: not the printer's, and not the clerk's.

The Steward's office had reviewed the seating chart. It had approved the empty square.

He signed the receipt. He said *thank you*. He walked out of the records office, and down a flight of stairs, and across a lower court, at the pace of a person with nowhere in particular to be, and only when he had come to the end of the colonnade, where the light fell across the flagstones in long, ordinary bars, did he stop.

"You've got a face," Solen said.

Aldric didn't jump. He'd been aware of Solen for the last several paces, in the way you're aware of a clock. Solen was standing in the shade at the end of the colonnade with a stack of returned library slips, looking as if he'd happened to be there for some time.

"It's paperwork," Aldric said.

"The face for paperwork is different. It has more contempt in it." Solen tilted his head, a fraction. "That one's for something with a name."

"It hasn't got a name yet."

"Then I'll be somewhere with a door that closes." He shifted the slips to his other arm and went on past, unhurried, and did not look back, which was the kindest thing he could have done.

Aldric watched him go. He would tell him. He was nearly certain he would. But not yet, not until the thing had a shape that would hold, and he had, at present, a dash in a square and a stack of cushions to go on.

He was thinking, quite calmly, about the cushions.

Somebody had sat in a seat the academy had never issued. Somebody had watched him across two matches from a place that did not exist on paper, and the paper, when asked, had been very careful to say that nobody had.

It hadn't said *I don't know who that was.*

It had said, in a firm and unremarkable hand, *there was no one there.*

The attack had been discussed to death and then quietly buried under a rumor about a second-year's engagement. In the corridors, in the refectory, in the long, dull afternoons of the winding-down term, the general agreement was that the year had had its drama and would now be so good as to conclude.

Silence.

"That's it?" Rynn breathed.

"That's it," Solen said, in a voice with nothing in it at all. "Two hundred and sixty-one words. I counted. Last year's harvest levy notice was four hundred."

"She said the shorter the notice, the bigger the file."

Nobody answered that.

---

It was, in fairness, a very ordinary thirty minutes.

Aldric drew the return path. It came out clean, with all three branches and the small feedback loop that everyone else got wrong. He had a fairly precise sense of what he was doing with his hands and almost none of what he was doing with his mind, and this, he thought, was probably the healthiest state of affairs available to a person who had just been handed a notice with a wall built into it.

*The earliest offences predate the session.*

Which ones? There must have been several. There was a *deliberate redirection of creatures,* which meant plural, which meant a long list of unlucky places, and the southern provinces were not small. Somewhere on that list there was a farm, a road, a ford that had gone quiet after dusk. Whoever they were, they'd been told, in two hundred and sixty-one words, that it was over.

He drew the next branch a little harder than it needed.

"You're pressing," Solen murmured, at his shoulder, not looking up. "The line's gone thick. It'll show on the copy."

"I'm aware."

He passed it back without looking, with the words *no* and *pay him* added underneath in a very small hand.

"You've got the face," Solen said, apparently to the diagram.

"It's the same face."

"It's not." Solen made a small, exact mark in his own margin, apparently a correction. "You've been doing arithmetic all week and nobody's told you the answer. I'd like to say it gets easier, but I've no data."

That, at least, was true, and Aldric let it stand.

"If the notice were a diagram," Rynn hissed, leaning across the gap, "where's the missing return path?"

"Rynn."

"I'm being clever."

"Be clever quieter," said Solen.

"He's not wrong," said Nessa Croft, from behind them, at a volume that made all three of them stop.

Behind them Rynn had begun trying to sneak a look at Maret's notebook by means of what he clearly believed was a subtle stretch, and was being ignored by the notebook's owner with an expression of near-perfect neutrality. It was, Aldric thought, the closest thing to a duel the room had produced all term, and Rynn was losing it politely. At the front of the room Maret had returned to the lectern and taken up his ledger, and had not so much as glanced at his coat pocket since the pen went in.

*He underlined it.*

Aldric considered, in the abstract, how many things there were on a Crown notice to underline. He was not certain there were any. The notice had not been designed to be underlined. It had been designed to be read once, filed, and forgotten, and the only person in the room who had responded to it as though it might, in fact, be evidence of something was the one man who never responded to anything aloud.

Which meant either that Maret knew something about it that the notice hadn't said, or that it had said something Aldric hadn't heard.

He went back over the words, quietly, the way you go back over a tune. *Redirection of creatures.* *Southern provinces.* *Sealed.* *The earliest predates the present session.* *A single individual.*

*Redirection.* He stopped on it. It was an odd word. It wasn't *attack,* or *summoning,* or *release.* It described a thing that already existed, being turned somewhere it hadn't meant to go.

He waited, quietly, for the thought to finish. It had the feel of a sentence with the last word missing.

Nothing came.

---

Class ended the way Maret's classes always ended, on the last syllable of a diagram and not a moment after. The room broke apart into its usual currents. Rynn, halfway to the door, announced to no one in particular that he was owed twenty stones by a man who did not, technically, exist. Solen, walking beside him, corrected the figure to twelve.

Aldric was the last to leave, for no reason he could have explained.

At the door he glanced back. Maret was at the lectern, alone, the register open, the sheet from the Crown folded once and placed square beneath it. The notebook was gone. If it was in the pocket, it gave no sign.

Maret did not look up.

"Vane," he said.

Aldric stopped.

He had been prepared, on some level, for almost anything. A question. A reprimand about the thickness of his line. A sentence with a hook in it.

"Your third branch," Maret said, to the ledger. "It's correct."

That was all. It was the closest thing to praise Aldric had ever heard from him, and it landed the way a stone lands in a very still pond, without splash, and with a great deal of consequence spreading out from it in circles.

"Thank you, Instructor."

Maret turned a page. It was over.

Aldric stepped out into the corridor. The light lay across the flagstones in long bars, and somewhere down the hall Rynn was arguing with Solen about whether wool could technically be called a sale. It was an ordinary day. In an hour it would be an ordinary afternoon.

He had thought about a captured man for days. He had thought about his face, and his armored captor, and a sword that hadn't needed a spell, and a seat with a dash where a name should have been. He had thought about the shape of the world, and what it had room for, and whom.

And underneath all of it, unnoticed until now, a single word had been going round like a burr in a sock.

*Redirection.*

He had no reason for it to matter. It was a dull, official word, the sort a clerk uses when he does not want to say what happened. But it had felt familiar, standing in Maret's room with the notice in the air: a word he had heard once, a long time ago, in a language he hadn't yet learned to read. And Maret, who read everything, had read it first.

Aldric stood at the foot of the staircase for a long moment, holding a sentence with the last word missing, and could not, for the life of him, say why it frightened him.

Rynn took the second, sprawled, one boot on the stone and the other on Aldric's shin, entirely without apology. Solen sat above them both with the exact economy of a man who had decided where he would be and was now merely completing the paperwork.

Rynn had brought a cake. It was a small, dense, slightly lopsided cake, and he refused to say where he'd found it. "It's a gift," he said, "from an anonymous friend. Or the kitchen. The distinction is legally murky."

"You stole it."

"I was *given* it, in a manner that should not be examined."

They ate. They talked about nothing. It was, Aldric thought, a very difficult thing to do properly, and the three of them were terrible at it: forty minutes of reasoned argument about whether wool could technically count as a sale, a heated exchange about the exact height of the ceiling in the refectory, and a long, patient explanation from Solen of why a goat that did not exist could nevertheless be said to have lost a wager.

"It's hypothetical," said Solen.

"It's *mine,*" said Rynn.

"You can't own a hypothetical goat."

"I'd like to see you take it from me."

"I could take it from you in a sentence."

"Try."

He tried. He was correct. Rynn took it with the wounded dignity of a man who had been robbed of something very small and very dear. Aldric laughed until his ribs hurt, and then, because it seemed to be the evening for it, laughed some more.

Torval came by around the fourth bell, carrying a wardrobe on his back. It was a whole wardrobe: oak, tall, the kind of thing two grown men would consider an afternoon's work. He did not seem to be in any difficulty. He set it down on the flagstones, nodded to the three of them with a civil gravity, and said, "It belongs to my cousin. He asked me to move it," in the tone of a man reporting the weather.

"Where's it going?" Rynn asked.

"Up," said Torval. He paused. "I thought I'd tell you. My House wants me home a week early." He looked, briefly, at Aldric. "It was good to fight you."

"It was," Aldric said. "I'm still sore."

"Good. It's meant to be." And he picked up the wardrobe again, and went up the stair with it, as if it were a satchel, and none of them said anything for a moment.

"That," Rynn said at last, "was the most Torval thing that has ever happened."

---

It was Rynn who suggested the awards.

He announced it sometime after the cake was gone. It was a *ceremony,* he said. A small one, held on the steps. Each of them would give out a prize, in a category of his own devising, to whichever of the other two most deserved it. There would be no speeches. There would be one speech. It would be his.

"Most Improved Vocabulary," he said, presenting Solen with an imaginary medal. "For a man who has gone from *no* to *I would prefer that you didn't.*"

"Thank you," said Solen. "I accept this on behalf of all my previous refusals."

"Loudest Person In A Room Where I Was Trying To Be Quiet," Solen said, and Rynn took a bow. "Most Likely To Say *Fine* When He Means Something Else," Solen went on, and Aldric raised a hand and was applauded, ironically, by two people who knew exactly how deserved it was.

"Most Likely," said Rynn, "To Run *At* Something."

The step went quiet.

It was not a large quiet. It came in the space of a breath, the way a shadow slides across a floor when a cloud passes the sun. Solen's hand, which had been half-raised in mock-applause, stopped where it was. Rynn's grin stayed on his face for exactly one heartbeat longer than it should have, and then, quite slowly, ceased to be a grin at all.

Nobody said the word. Nobody said *the attack,* or *the courtyard,* or *Solm,* or *five people in tournament uniforms.* But it was there in the air like a smell. A year of it, the tournament and the burst and the look on a stranger's face as he fled. It had been building, quietly, in three people who had all been very careful not to mention it, and it had found the shape of a joke to slip through, and was now standing on the step in front of them, waiting to be spoken to.

Aldric felt his mouth start to move.

He didn't know what he was going to say. Something true, probably. It was that sort of evening, and he was very tired of *fine.*

"—which," said Rynn, loudly, "brings me to the winner. Because the winner, and I want this on record, is the *goat.*"

Solen blinked.

"The goat," Rynn continued, standing, one hand pressed over his heart. "The hypothetical goat. Who has been *through* things this term. Who has lost a wager to a man who does not exist, been denied by a legal argument, been tried in absentia, and has *never once complained.* Ladies and gentlemen. The goat."

A pause.

Aldric started laughing. He couldn't help it. It came up from his chest, from somewhere underneath the shaking that he had been carrying since the night of the attack, and it wasn't at the joke. It was at the *timing.* At a boy who could not keep a secret for the life of him and had just kept the most important one of the year, in the space of one sentence, by being ridiculous.

Solen was laughing too, in his own way: a single, dry, helpless exhale through the nose, and then a long silence in which he did not look at either of them. "That was an extremely bad speech," he said, finally.

"I know," said Rynn, sitting down, and Aldric saw that his hands were not entirely steady. "I've been working on it for hours."

Nobody said thank you. It didn't need to be said. It hung there, on the step, exactly as heavy as it should be.

---

It was quite late when they went in.

Aldric stayed a minute after the others, the way he always did, for no reason but that it seemed rude to leave a room without looking at it. The steps were cold. The courtyard was black and silent. The dark window across the way was still dark, and he didn't wait for anything to move in it.

*Something sturdier,* he thought. It arrived without ceremony. He'd once told them that some people called them a group, in a courtyard, on these steps, and had not meant it as a compliment. He had not meant it as anything. It had simply been a word for three people who kept sitting in the same place.

But that wasn't what this was anymore. Nobody had decided it. Nobody had said a word. Somewhere between the bracket and the Final and a courtyard full of screaming, the three of them had stopped being a thing that happened to be true and had become a thing that was simply so, the way a house becomes home without anybody noticing the day the furniture stopped being borrowed.

It was, he thought, the best thing that had happened to him all year, and he had not been looking.

He stood, and stretched, and went up.

In his room the trunk lay open on the floor where he'd left it that morning, empty except for the half-packed edge of a winter coat. He looked at it for a long time. The lamp was low. Through the wall he could hear Rynn's voice, muffled and cheerful, arguing with someone about the last of the cake.

It occurred to him, standing there, with a coat in one hand and nothing at all to do with the other, that the evening was over.

That was all. It had been a good one. It had gone, in every way that counted, exactly right. And it was over, and the week was nearly over, and the year was nearly over, and there was no way to keep a night like that except to have had it.

He folded the coat. It took much longer than it should have.

# CHAPTER 59 — "WHAT THE YEAR MADE OF HIM"

## Plan

## Draft

— Aldric Vane —

"Closed," said the man at the arena arch, without looking up from the broom he was leaning on.

"I know."

"Then why are you standing there?"

"Because I'm hoping you'll say *open* if I stand here long enough," Aldric said. "It's a technique. It doesn't work very often."

The man looked up. He was a stonemason, by the dust on his sleeves, sixty or thereabouts, with the ruined patience of a person who had spent forty years being asked to move things that were already exactly where they belonged. He gave Aldric a slow, unimpressed once-over that began at the white hair and finished, without hurry, at the boots.

"You're the boy who won it."

"I'm afraid so."

"Hm." The broom shifted from one hand to the other. "I've got an hour before I lock up. Bunting's coming down tomorrow and I'll not have you fiddling with it. Don't touch the ropes, don't touch the boxes, and if you fall off something I never saw you."

"That's very generous."

"It's very insured. There's a difference." He went back to sweeping. "Mind the sand on the east side. It's got opinions, and no one to share them with but me."

Aldric went in.

It was the last evening of the term. Tomorrow the carts came, and the trunks would go down the hill like a slow, cheerful avalanche, and the academy would empty itself into the roads and villages and great houses of the kingdom until the bell called it back. A letter was on its way to Ashford. Whatever came back would find him changed, in some way he could not yet describe, from the person who had written it.

He had an hour. He wanted to spend it honestly, which was a slightly alarming thing to want, since it meant he would have to start by finding out what that looked like.

---

The Grand Arena, empty, was much smaller than it had been.

He hadn't expected that. It had been a mountain, all spring. It had loomed over the whole of his year, in a shape that seemed to grow every time he looked at it, and now, at dusk, with the crowd gone and the banners stripped from the rim and only a few sagging ropes of bunting left to stir in the wind, it was just tiers of stone around a yard of trodden sand. A very good yard. A large one. But a place. Something you could walk across in a minute and a half.

He walked across it anyway. The sand gave slightly under his boots, and on the east side, as promised, it had opinions.

He stopped near the center, where he had stood for the last exchange of the Final, and looked up.

*All right,* he thought. *Let's do this properly.*

He had a list. He had had one for months, in the way a person has a room in his house he doesn't go into: not written down, never once opened, but known in every detail by the way he walked around it. He had been so careful about it. Every time it came near he had picked up something else, a class or a rumor or a bolt drill or a friend's joke, and filed the whole business under *later.* He had been very good at *later.* It was the best skill he'd brought to Greyveil, and he'd sharpened it every day.

He opened the door.

*The promise.*

That one came first. It always did. *We'll both get in.* A boy in a field at dusk, saying it like a fact. And a hall, and a list read out, and another boy walking out before it was finished. He looked at it. It hurt. It was supposed to. But it didn't hurt in the old way, in the flat, sour, guilty way it had when he was a first-year with something to prove. It was more like a bruise: a real thing, in a real place, with edges he could feel and a size he could measure. *I made a promise and it broke.* It was true. It didn't need to be anything else.

*Ashford.*

The place, and the sensation, and the thing in the road. He let it come up. He didn't deflect it or make a joke. A village he loved, a night he couldn't explain, a pressure at the center of his chest that had answered him when nobody had asked it to. He had spent a year assuming it was a mistake to be managed. He turned it over, slowly, and for the first time it did not look like a mistake. It looked like a fact. A large, strange, unpleasant fact, that would have to be understood someday, and that wasn't going away because he'd stopped looking at it.

*What he was.*

He didn't have a word for that one. He didn't know what would go there. White hair. Red eyes. A thing that woke when he stopped holding it down, and had, in front of half the kingdom and a king, done something Tavish Ardell had called *not a spell.* He had no name for it. He was not sure anyone did.

*What they thought he was.*

*Just showing up looking like a question nobody could answer.*

It was a good line. Essa had given it to him without meaning to, and it was correct, and he had been carrying it around all term like a coin he couldn't spend. They had thought he was a pamphlet, and then a fluke, and then a threat, and then a story. They had decided what he was in the first week and had spent every week since revising the verdict. He'd been so tired of it. He had thought that if he could only be *forgettable,* the whole apparatus would just wind down, like a clock nobody had the heart to fix.

It hadn't. It wasn't going to.

There was a fifth thing on the list that he hadn't put there.

It came up unbidden, a little embarrassed by its own presence, like a guest arriving at the wrong door. Rynn's very large ears and nothing much in them. Solen at the foot of the stairs, reporting a broken hinge in the tone of a man handing over a key. A chair in a library corner, moved four inches by a person who had never once said a thing she didn't mean. None of them was a problem. None of them belonged with the others. He had no idea where to put them, and he suspected that was the entire point.

He decided, for once, not to put them anywhere.

He stood in the middle of the sand with the whole list open in front of him, all doors wide, and waited for the floor to go out from under him.

---

It didn't.

He waited a good while, to be certain. The wind moved the bunting. Somewhere beyond the wall, a horse was being led to a stable. A single bell rang the third quarter of the hour and stopped.

*Huh,* Aldric thought.

It seemed, if he was honest, slightly rude of the world not to have made a bigger occasion of it. He had prepared a whole face for the moment. He put it away again, unused, with the faint disappointment of a man who has dressed for a storm and been handed a pleasant afternoon.

He'd been so sure. He'd braced for it all year. He had expected the honest accounting to be like opening a cellar and finding a flood: cold, black, and rising fast. Instead it was quiet. It was just a list. Four things, with edges, that he'd finally looked at squarely, and none of them, now that they were in the open, was as large as the thing he'd built to keep them out.

That was the strange part. It wasn't that anything had been solved. The promise was still broken, and Ashford was still a question, and what he was remained a blank and a rumor. It was only that the *fear* of them had gone. He'd spent so long bracing for the impact that he'd forgotten the impact was a thing that happened once, and could be borne.

It wasn't peace, exactly. It was closer to what a person feels when a tooth that has ached for a year finally comes out. A gap. A raw place. And an unexpected, almost embarrassing lightness.

He laughed, once, out loud, in the empty arena, and the echo came back to him from the stone with a slight delay, as though the building were checking his reaction.

*The boy who left Ashford,* he thought, quite calmly, *is gone.*

It wasn't dramatic. It didn't hurt, either. It was simply a fact, the way a bridge you've already crossed is a fact. He had come into this place in a borrowed coat with a letter in his pocket and a plan to be unnoticeable. He had wanted, above all things, to blend in. And that boy, with his careful diagrams and his careful filing and his careful, cowardly *fine,* had not survived the year. Not because anything had killed him. He had just turned into somebody else a little at a time, like water finding its way under a door. It would have been rude to mourn him. He had done his job.

Aldric stood there a while longer, and let himself be a person who had arrived.

---

The stonemason's hour had nearly run down when he climbed to the tier.

He went up without a plan, taking the stairs two at a time, as far as the Council rows, and the wind found him there, cool and clean, the way it does in high places. The rows were empty. The blue cushions were gone. The whole curve of the tier lay bare and pale in the dusk, a great stone smile with the teeth taken out.

He counted the rows, and then the seats, without meaning to, and stopped in front of the ninth.

It was bare stone. It looked like every other bare stone seat in the tier, now that the cushions had come off, which was precisely the thing about it. No mark. No line through a name. Just a seat, and a view.

He sat.

It was colder than he'd expected. He put his hands on his knees and looked down, at the yard, at the place where he'd stood, and then out, over the wall, at the whole of the academy lying spread out in the last of the light: the towers, the dormitory roofs, the long grey ridge of the east wing, and, small and perfectly clear, a window on the third floor where a lamp had just come on. His.

It was a very good seat. From here, a person could see everything.

He'd been so afraid, all year, of being seen. He thought of it now, from the watcher's chair, with a curious calm. It seemed to him that being watched was no longer the worst thing that could happen. It was just a condition, like the weather. Let them look. Let them draw whatever conclusions they liked. He had a list, and he'd read it, and it hadn't destroyed him. If there was a man in expensive grey who had sat here and thought of him, well, that man could think what he pleased.

He was, for the first time since he had left the village, not afraid of anything at all.

He sat until the light went, and then stood, and stretched, and went down the long stair to the sand. He nodded to the stonemason on his way out. The stonemason, sweeping, did not look up. It was the friendliest thing he could have done.

Aldric walked back to the dormitory in the last of the dusk, lighter than he'd been all year, with the peculiar bright certainty of a man who had finally looked at every threat he could name.

It did not occur to him that the ones worth fearing were the ones he hadn't yet learned to name.

It would be some time before it did.

## Draft

— Daran —

Hask said a full sentence.

It happened a little after the sun cleared the stable roof, in the ordinary middle of the ordinary morning, with the slops bucket in one hand and two fingers half-lifted for the usual greeting. Daran had already begun the second form. He had his back to the yard and his weight on the wrong foot, and for a moment he honestly believed he'd misheard.

"Cousin's cart goes south Thursday," Hask said.

The two fingers came down. The bucket swung. Hask, having discharged the whole of his correspondence for the year, continued across the yard toward the hogs at the same pace, without a glance back, and Daran stood with the blade still extended, completing a turn that had gone entirely out of his head.

Five words, about a cart. He had known this man the better part of a year, and had never once heard him string more than three together in the presence of a living creature.

*He's been counting too,* Daran thought. It was an uncomfortable idea. It suggested there had been an audience.

He reset. Did the turn again. It came out fine.

He did not say *thank you.* Hask would have hated it. He lifted two fingers instead, at the man's back, and the man, without turning, lifted his own in reply, and that was the whole of the conversation, and it was, Daran reflected, the most generous thing anyone had done for him since a village he no longer named.

---

He had woken without the number.

That was the odd part. Every morning of the last year, in the grey before the light was worth anything, it had been there behind his eyes like a coin held under the tongue. It had told him how far he'd come, and how far he had left, and had never once asked his opinion. He had never said it aloud. He had promised himself, somewhere around the fortieth day, that he'd never say it aloud, and he never had, not to Hask, not to a horse, not to the ceiling.

This morning it hadn't been there.

He had lain on the cot, listening to the stable shift and breathe below him, and waited for it. It didn't come. Not a hollow, not an ache where it had been. It was simply gone, the way a sound is gone when you notice, only afterward, that the clock has stopped.

*Ah,* he'd thought.

That was all. *Ah.* He'd rolled out of bed and pulled on his boots and gone down to the yard, and it was only when Hask had said his sentence that he'd understood the arithmetic. The count had run out. Sometime in the night, while he slept, the last day of a year he'd built out of nothing but stubbornness had quietly turned over and become the first day of whatever came next.

It hadn't felt like anything. That was what he'd been braced for, all those mornings: some kind of bell. And there was no bell. There was a yard, and cold hands, and a cheap sword, and a Wednesday.

He was, on balance, fine with that.

Before going down he had taken stock, the way a man does before a journey, sitting on the edge of the cot with his boots half laced. A cheap sword. A hilt wrapped twice more than it needed. A coat with a repair he'd done himself, badly, and a second he'd had done better. The last of his coin under the tack-room board. The cot itself, which he'd been given for nothing and had never once been asked to explain. It was not a great deal. It was, by his own careful accounting, exactly enough.

---

The forms went well, and then they didn't, in the manner of forms.

He worked through the first six the way he always did, before the day had time to give him reasons not to. They ran without asking permission. His feet knew the ground. The wrap on the hilt, which he'd redone twice more than the job required, sat in his palm like a hand he'd been shaking for months. Somewhere in the fourth, a horse behind the stable wall blew out through its nose at something only it could smell, and it sounded so much like disgust that Daran nodded at it, one professional to another.

Then the seventh, and the seventh did what it had done every day for the better part of a year.

It was a low, awkward thing, a reverse cut out of a crouch, meant to open a man's guard from below while the wrist did most of the lying. The book had a diagram for it, with the weight marked in tidy percentages. He had followed them for two months and fallen over on schedule. He had burned the diagram, metaphorically, and gone on falling over off the page. He'd come, somewhere around the middle of spring, to think of it less as a form than as a person who disliked him.

*Good morning,* he thought, dropping into the crouch. *Shall we?*

The cut came out an inch shy of the line. The weight went wrong in the follow-through. His left knee took the blame, as it always did.

He reset. He did it again.

It was wrong, and a little different from yesterday's wrong. He noted it. That had been the whole method, in the end: get up, be wrong, get up, be wrong more interestingly. Nobody had ever shown up at the edge of the yard to tell him he was doing it right. He had stopped waiting for the man some time in the summer. He had decided that the grizzled figure who spoke nine words and changed a life was a story told by people who'd never had to do it alone, and that the real instructor was just the morning, over and over, which was patient in a way no human being had ever managed.

*I trained myself into a sword school,* he thought, rising out of the crouch. *The alternative was waiting for one to be handed to me.* It was still a good joke. He had nobody to tell it to, but it kept well, like a coin in a boot.

He did the seventh again. And again. And again.

---

The afternoon was long.

Hask came out twice more, once for the well, once for a horse with a stone in its shoe, and neither time said anything at all, which Daran found he was grateful for. The light moved across the yard, and the shadows of the stable roof crept slowly toward the well and away from it. His shoulders began to complain at a reasonable hour. His hands, which had thickened and broken and reset themselves so many times this year that he thought of them as a work in progress, did what they were told.

He did not think about the road east.

He noticed, somewhere in the fifth hour, that he hadn't. It was a strange thing to notice: an absence, sharp as a missing tooth. For months the road had been a presence, a place his eyes did not go, and the not-going had cost something every time. Now it just lay there, past the tree line, running toward a hall and a city and the boy he had once promised to follow into both. It wasn't guarded. It wasn't avoided. It was only a road, going somewhere he wasn't.

*A field at dusk,* he thought, testing it the way you test a bruise. *We'll both get in.*

It was a memory. It was a good one. It didn't hurt at all.

That was the thing that surprised him, in the end. Not that it was gone, but that it was *finished.* The way a good tool is finished: complete, useful, and no longer required for the job at hand. He set it down carefully, the way you set down something that has been carried a long way, and did not pick it up again.

He drew the sword. The seventh, once more, before the light went.

---

It came in the last hour, and it came so quietly that he almost missed it.

He dropped into the crouch. He set his weight, his hand, his eyes. The cut went out low and fast, on a line he could not have drawn if you'd paid him, and the wrist did its lie, and the follow-through carried the blade up through the imagined guard, and his weight came round behind it in a single, seamless motion that had no seam in it anywhere.

It landed.

He stayed exactly where he was, in the crouch, sword high, waiting for the feeling.

Nothing arrived.

He'd expected as much. He had learned, in the stable yard on a grey morning long ago, what the day a thing finally works looks like: the flat, unremarkable click of a bolt sliding home. There was no light. No music. Just the plain, complete sense of a cup set down, and a body that would now simply expect this of itself, forever, without ceremony. The floor had risen an inch. Tomorrow he would stand on it and not notice.

He rose slowly out of the crouch, and he waited for the old unease. The dread of the new floor. The knowledge that he would have to find a new failure to measure himself against, or the whole apparatus of getting up would stop making sense.

It didn't come, either.

*Oh,* Daran thought. *I've stopped needing one.*

He stood there a while, in the long gold light, with the sword in his hand and the horse behind the wall blowing gently through its nose, and turned it over. It was such a small thing. A year ago he would have said that finishing the seventh was the whole point. It wasn't. It had never been the point. The point was that a man could get up every morning and be wrong until it worked, and that he had done that, and would go on doing it, whether or not anyone was watching or a hall had a name on a list.

He sheathed the sword. It took a moment to find the mouth of the scabbard. His hands were tired, and quite steady.

---

The sun went down behind the stable, and the yard filled slowly with blue.

He stood at the well and drank, and washed his face, and looked at his hands in the last light: the thickened palm, the two knuckles that no longer closed, the scar along the thumb. They were not a boy's hands. He had a vague, distant sense that he ought to feel something about that, and found he didn't, particularly. They were good hands. They would do.

He was not thinking about the academy. He was not thinking about the promise, or the hall, or the field, or anyone in either place. He noticed that he was not, and did not take it for a victory. It was only a fact, like the weather.

He was thinking about tomorrow.

The cart went south on Thursday, Hask had said. Two days. He would need boots that would last, and a coat, and the last of the coin under the loose board in the tack room, and something to say at the door of a yard three towns away, in front of a man who would look at him once and decide. He'd worked on the something for months. It was still not good. He would work on it some more.

He looked south.

Past the tree line, past the last of the blue, the road bent out of sight toward a place he had never seen. He didn't know what the man there would ask him first. He didn't know what he'd say. He didn't know if he was good enough, or whether good enough was even the question, or what a person did the day after he'd finally, quietly, stopped counting.

He found, standing there in the dark, with a stable at his back and a road ahead of him, that he was curious to hear it.
