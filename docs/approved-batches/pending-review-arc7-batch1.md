## Draft

The goat was the first thing to notice the riders, which meant Drel noticed them roughly four seconds later, which was still — by the standards of the last six weeks — a personal best.

He was elbow-deep in a lambing pen that wasn't his, on a farm whose owner believed his name was Corran, holding a breech kid the wrong way round and not caring, because the ewe was frightened and Drel's hands had that effect on frightened things whether he asked them to or not. The kid stopped struggling the moment he touched it. It always did. That was the whole trouble with him, distilled into one small, wet, ungrateful animal: things stopped struggling around Drel Voss whether they should or not.

The goat, tethered by the fence line, had gone rigid three fields off, ears forward, and Drel had learned six weeks ago to trust a goat over his own ears every single time.

He finished the birth. That part, at least, was still entirely his — hands, breath, the particular patience it took to turn a stuck kid without breaking its neck or the ewe's. Nobody had ever needed to make him good at this. He'd been good at it since before he had a word for what he was, back when it was just a boy in a village who could calm a kicking mule with a look, and the village had called it luck, and later a gift, and later still — once the wrong people noticed — an asset.

He wiped his hands on straw, told the boy who owned the farm that the ewe would live and the kid would too, collected the two coins he was owed and didn't especially want, and walked, without hurrying, toward the tree line the goat had already told him about.

Not hurrying was its own kind of discipline. Six weeks ago he'd have run. He didn't run anymore, and it had taken him most of those six weeks to understand why, which was the part he genuinely couldn't tell anyone, not because he was being close-mouthed about it, but because he didn't have the words either.

He walked into the trees and kept walking until the farm was gone behind him, and only then let his knees remember they were allowed to shake.

*They're close,* he thought, and felt the thought arrive with its usual companion — a second, colder awareness sliding in half a step behind the first, checking it, weighing it, deciding what to do with it before Drel himself got a vote. He had stopped trying to name that second awareness months ago. Naming it made it sound like a person, and he did not want to spend what might be his last free hour being polite to the thing that had been wearing his hands like a glove.

He kept walking. East, toward denser country, away from the farm and the boy and the goat that had, in its blunt animal way, just saved his life for one more afternoon.

The trouble with running for six weeks on an ability like his was that the ability didn't stop working just because he needed it to be quiet. He had learned, from a bitter distance, exactly which of the local farms had a sick mule, a colicking horse, a ewe in trouble — learned it the way he learned everything now, as a low, constant hum at the edge of every field he crossed, animals turning their heads to watch him pass whether he wanted them to or not. Trying not to be noticed, with a gift built entirely out of being noticed by every living thing within half a mile, was a joke so thorough he'd stopped finding it funny sometime around week three. Somewhere around week five he'd started finding it funny again, in the specific, brittle way a man finds anything funny once he's run out of other things to feel about it.

He crested a low rise and saw the smoke before he saw the village — not fire-smoke, just the ordinary grey thread of a dozen hearths going about a dozen ordinary evenings, and something in his chest did the thing it always did at a sight like that, which was want, with an intensity that had nothing to do with hunger or shelter, to walk down into it and tell someone.

*Don't.*

He didn't know if the word came from him or from the other thing, and that not-knowing was, more than the running, more than the six weeks, more than anything else that had happened to him since Ashford, the actual wound. He used to be able to tell. In the beginning — and he made himself think the word *beginning* precisely, because pretending it had always been this way was its own kind of surrender — there had been a clean line between what Drel Voss wanted and what Drel Voss did, the same line every honest man walks around inside every day without noticing it's there. Somewhere in the months after that, the line had stopped being reliable. He would reach for a thought and find it already half-formed by someone else's hand. He would open his mouth to say a true thing and find the true thing rerouted, on its way out, into something smaller and safer and utterly beside the point.

He stood at the top of the rise, looking down at the smoke, and thought — deliberately, testing it the way a man tests a loose tooth with his tongue — *I should walk down there and tell them a search party is three fields behind me, so they lock their doors and keep their children in tonight, because whatever comes to collect me may not be gentle about how it does it.*

It was a good thought. It was, he was fairly sure, entirely his own — decent, practical, the kind of thing the man he used to be would simply have done without weighing it first.

His feet did not move toward the village. His mouth did not open. Something in his chest closed instead, smooth and total and entirely without malice, the way a door closes when a draft finds it rather than when a hand pushes it, and the good, decent, practical thought simply stopped being a thing he was doing anything about.

He stood there a while longer, not moving, testing the door the way he tested it most days now, out of something between curiosity and grief. It held. It always held. He had never once, in six weeks, gotten a warning past it to anyone who might have used it — not the boy with the ewe, not the innkeeper who'd fed him on credit outside Corvane, not, worst of all and longest ago, anyone in a village called Ashford that he had never set foot in and had ruined all the same, from a comfortable distance, with a creature that had never once, in its miserable animal life, wanted to hurt anyone until something reached into it and made it want to.

He had not chosen that either. He wanted, badly, for that to matter to somebody besides himself.

Gallows humor, he'd found, was the only kind that survived the trip. *A monster tamer,* he thought, starting back down the far side of the rise, angling away from the smoke, *who cannot warn a single living soul about anything, including the monster currently wearing him.* He had told that joke to precisely no one, for the excellent reason that there was no one left to tell it to, and it was, he privately thought, wasted on an audience of one goat and a lambing pen, but a man kept the muscle exercised regardless, in case anyone was ever again in a position to laugh at it with him.

Night found him in a dry culvert half a mile on, chewing the last of the bread he'd been given instead of proper payment, listening to the particular quiet that meant the countryside around him had noticed something and gone still about it. He did not need the goat this time. He could feel it the way he felt everything now — a spreading awareness of two dozen small, wary creatures further out doing exactly what the goat had done that afternoon, ears up, backs to the wind, watching something approach that they didn't have a name for either.

He thought, with the flat clarity of a man who has done the arithmetic too many times to flinch from the answer, *tonight, then.*

He did not run.

He had asked himself why, more than once, in the six weeks it had taken him to arrive at this culvert with soldiers somewhere in the dark beyond it, and the honest answer — the one that belonged entirely to him, untouched by any other hand — was that running had stopped being about escape somewhere around week four and had started being, quietly, unbearably, about how many more villages he would pass through before whatever was inside him decided to use him again. Every day he stayed free was a day closer to some farmer's ewe, some child's pony, some stranger's dog turning traitor in his hands the way the Greyback had, because whoever held the leash had shown, once, exactly what they were willing to spend him on, and Drel Voss did not believe for one instant that it had been a single, isolated expense.

He set the bread down. He did not hide, particularly. He sat in the culvert with his back against the cool stone and let his hands go still, for the first time in six weeks, entirely on their own instruction rather than anyone else's.

The soldiers found him within the hour — quiet, professional, more of them than the occasion strictly required, which told him something about how the kingdom had come to weigh the danger of one tired man who talked to animals. He did not resist. He did not call anything to his defense, though he could feel, distantly, half a dozen creatures in the surrounding dark straining toward the sound of trouble, ready without being asked, and it took every remaining scrap of will he owned to hold that door shut himself, on purpose, the one time in six weeks he actually wanted it closed.

A young officer said something to him that he didn't fully hear, about rights and procedure and a name he no longer used. He looked past her, at the treeline, at the ordinary dark that held two dozen animals he had just spent the last effort of his freedom keeping out of a fight that wasn't theirs, and felt something move through his chest that was not the smooth, total closing-off he'd grown used to, but something else — older, plainer, entirely his own.

Relief did not feel the way he'd expected, when it finally arrived. It felt like the first honest thing he'd been allowed to feel in six weeks, sitting quietly beside the much larger, much colder feeling of knowing exactly how little of what came next would be his to choose either.

They put him in irons that did nothing at all to the part of him that actually mattered, because the part of him that actually mattered had never once, in six weeks, needed his hands free to be dangerous, and somewhere behind his sternum, patient and total and entirely without a name he could give anyone, the thing that had been wearing him went very quiet, as if it, too, understood that its work here was finished for now.

He did not know who it was. He never had. That was the part he would carry into whatever came next more heavily than the irons — not fear of what they'd do to him, and not even the weight of Ashford, though that would never entirely leave him either, but the specific, grinding indignity of having been used by something he couldn't even properly hate, because hating a thing requires knowing its shape, and this had only ever shown him its hand.

He looked up at the officer, at the torches, at the ordinary tired soldiers doing an ordinary tired job, and said the only true thing he had left that hadn't been touched by anyone else's hand.

"I'm glad it's over."

She misheard it as confession. He let her.

"I'm just saying," Rynn said, "that if you'd told me in the first week that I'd be *nostalgic* for the day Crest called you a pamphlet, I'd have had you looked at."

"You did have me looked at. You threw a punch on my behalf without asking my opinion first."

"That's different. That's supportive."

"It cost you two weeks of lamp brackets."

"Character building," Rynn said, with the specific, aggressive cheer of a man determined to enjoy the last ordinary corridor of his morning before Mana Theory ruined it. "I've got excellent character now. Ask anyone. Ask Solen."

"He has excellent character," Solen said, not looking up from the folded page he was squaring the corners of, a habit Aldric had stopped finding strange sometime in the last two years and started, instead, finding load-bearing — Solen composed meant Solen thinking, and Solen thinking usually meant something worth hearing arrived within the next thirty seconds. "It's mostly composed of volume."

"See, that's support with a knife in it. That's your whole personality."

"It's efficient," Aldric said.

"Don't you start."

The corridor outside the east stair had its usual mid-morning texture — students moving in loose, unhurried clusters toward whatever rotation claimed them next, someone's satchel strap failing spectacularly near the noticeboard, the particular institutional hum of a term winding down without anyone having formally announced that it was. Aldric had walked this stretch of corridor perhaps four hundred times since his own first, disoriented morning in it, and had long since stopped mapping it consciously — which was, he understood now, its own small victory, one he hadn't noticed happening until it had already happened, the way most of the good things at Greyveil seemed to arrive.

He nearly missed the voices for exactly that reason. He had stopped listening for trouble in this corridor months ago, on the reasonable theory that a hallway this well-lit and this full of first-years arguing about lamp brackets was not, on balance, where trouble tended to live.

It was Bavel's voice that caught him first — not the words, just the register, pitched lower and flatter than his ordinary classroom clip, the specific tone of a man who has decided a conversation isn't for the corridor and is having it in the corridor anyway because there wasn't a better room available on short notice. Aldric slowed without deciding to. Rynn, mid-sentence about something involving a goat and a wager he'd apparently lost to Torval, didn't notice. Solen did, and went quiet in the particular way that meant he'd clocked it too.

Bavel was three doors down, half-turned into an alcove with a records clerk Aldric didn't recognize — an older man, ledger under one arm, the specific weary posture of someone who'd been fetching files since before dawn and expected to keep fetching them past dusk. Their voices didn't carry whole sentences, only fragments, the kind a corridor gives up freely and takes back just as fast.

"—capture operation, outside Valdris Prime—" the clerk said, and something in Aldric's chest did the small, involuntary thing it always did at a phrase like that, an old reflex from a life he mostly didn't think about anymore, sharpening without his permission.

"—confirmed?" Bavel, flat.

"—Crown's calling it concluded. Officially. As of this morning."

"And the—"

"No," Nessa agreed. "It's the shape of one. I'm told that's occasionally useful." "That went well," Solen observed, arriving at his shoulder with the specific flat cheer he reserved for other people's failed information-gathering.

"She told me nothing."

"She told you her mother's runner came at dawn instead of the usual hour, which is not nothing, it's simply nothing *phrased* as nothing. You should be more suspicious of people who are good at that. I'd know."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm informative. There's an overlap, but they're not the same word." Solen fell into step beside him as the corridor refilled, unhurried, cataloguing. "For what it's worth, I don't think it's nothing either. I just don't have anything to put in the gap yet."

He had learned, across two years at Greyveil, a fairly reliable rule about mornings like this one: most of what unsettled him for an hour turned out, by evening, to have unsettled him for no better reason than an idle mind with too little to do and too much practice noticing things. He told himself that now, walking toward Elemental Foundations with Rynn's voice rising and falling beside him and Solen's silence doing its own quieter work on the other side.

He had been wrong about that particular reassurance before. He had simply never yet had cause, in the moment itself, to notice.

That was the story he'd been telling himself, at any rate. The truer version, which he examined only briefly and only once, was that a letter home meant deciding how much of the last fortnight to actually put in it, and he had not yet worked out an honest answer that wouldn't frighten them both considerably more than the truth deserved.

He finished it that morning, in the end, with the specific stubborn discipline of a man who has decided a thing will get done today or continue not getting done indefinitely. *We won the tournament,* he wrote, and then crossed out *we* and wrote *I* instead, because Rynn and Solen would have opinions about being erased from the sentence and Maren would notice the plural regardless. *There was some trouble afterward. Nobody I care about was hurt, including me, and I mean that plainly rather than as a thing people say. I think about the two of you more than I say out loud, which isn't news to either of you, but I wanted it written down somewhere for once instead of just carried.*

He read it back twice, decided it was as honest as he was currently capable of being on paper, and folded it away before Rynn could ask to read it over his shoulder, which he had already tried twice that morning with the subtlety of a man attempting to pick a lock in full view of its owner.

"You could just tell me what it says," Rynn pointed out, entirely unashamed. "I'd nod at the appropriate places. I'm an excellent audience."

"It's addressed to my parents, not a performance review."

"Some of us contain multitudes." Rynn considered the sealed letter with the frank, undisguised interest of a man who had never once in his life kept a secret successfully and saw no reason to start respecting other people's. "Tell them I said hello. Tell them the goat story. It reflects well on me."

"There is no version of the goat story that reflects well on you."

"Then tell them your version. I trust your judgment completely, right up until it involves my reputation."

Solen, from the window seat where he'd been squaring the corners of something for the better part of ten minutes without once looking up from it, offered only: "Give my regards to whoever's shelf that root oil is currently sitting on." It took Aldric a beat longer than it should have to place the reference — Doven's mother, the ten good years, a line dropped so casually two years ago that Aldric had half-forgotten Solen had been in the room to hear it — and by the time he'd placed it, Solen had already gone back to his page, entirely satisfied with having landed it.

He tucked the letter into his coat, safely out of Rynn's reach, and went to request the leave to send it.

Personal correspondence leave was, by Greyveil's own unglamorous standards, one of the easier permissions a first-year could obtain — a short form, a modest fee for the courier's book, and a walk down to the post house in the town below the academy grounds, a route students took often enough that the guards at the lower gate barely glanced up from their dice game when Aldric presented his slip. He had made the walk exactly twice before in two years, both times in his first term, both times before the distance from Ashford had settled into something he'd stopped measuring daily. It felt strange, walking it again now — not sad, exactly, just strange, the way any road felt strange once enough time had passed that you noticed yourself noticing it.

The morning was bright and unseasonably warm, the kind of day that made the end of a term feel closer than the calendar actually placed it, and Aldric let himself enjoy the walk in the uncomplicated way he rarely allowed himself to enjoy anything — no errand attached beyond the letter in his pocket, no faculty conversation to decode, no fragment of overheard sentence sitting at the back of his thinking demanding attention it hadn't earned. The road wound down through open pasture and scattered treeline before it reached the town proper, the academy's towers receding behind him at an angle that still, occasionally, caught him by surprise with how large they'd become in his life without his fully noticing.

He was perhaps two-thirds of the way down when he saw the riders.

There were a great many of them — more than any ordinary patrol, moving with the particular disciplined quiet of soldiers who had somewhere specific to be and no interest in announcing it. They crossed the road below him at a diagonal, cutting off toward a stand of trees at the valley's edge that Aldric had walked past twice before without ever once wondering what lay beyond it. He slowed without deciding to, the way he'd slowed in a corridor two days ago at the sound of Bavel's voice gone flat and careful, some old, unnamed part of him recognizing the shape of something before the rest of him had caught up to why.

He told himself, for exactly as long as it took to keep walking another twenty paces, that it was none of his business, that the kingdom's soldiers went about the kingdom's business every day without requiring a first-year's commentary on it, and that the sensible thing — the thing Rynn would have told him to do, the thing his own better judgment was telling him to do — was to continue down to the post house, send his letter, and be back inside the walls well before curfew.

He stopped walking instead, at the edge of a low rise that gave an unobstructed view down into the valley's shallow bowl, and watched.

It took him a moment to understand what he was looking at. The soldiers had ringed a stretch of open ground near the treeline, loose but complete, the particular formation of people who expected trouble and had planned for it rather than people simply passing through. At the center of the ring, kneeling in the trampled grass with his hands bound in front of him, was a man.

Aldric had expected, without quite admitting he'd expected anything, something closer to a monster — some proof, however small, that the effort arrayed against it was proportionate to the danger it represented. What he saw instead was ordinary in a way that unsettled him considerably more than a monster would have. A lean man, weathered, dark-skinned, dressed in the practical layers of someone who worked outdoors for a living, kneeling with the specific stillness of a man who had already decided not to fight and was now simply waiting to see what came next. He did not look dangerous. He looked tired, and something else besides — an expression Aldric couldn't fully place from this distance, but which read, unmistakably, as relief.

He was still turning that over when he saw the second figure, and it was the second figure that held his attention considerably longer than the first.

The man stood apart from the ring of soldiers, near enough to be plainly in command and far enough to suggest he didn't especially need the proximity to prove it. He wore armor unlike anything Aldric had seen on the academy grounds or in any of the Grand Arena's ceremonial parades — white, unornamented, with a trim of dark iron-grey that caught none of the morning light the way gold or silver would have, as though the metal itself had made a deliberate choice not to be noticed. He carried a sword at his hip that looked, even from this distance, wrong in some way Aldric couldn't name — too plain for its evident importance, the specific unshowy plainness of a thing that had never once needed to prove what it was.

He did nothing dramatic. He simply stood, watching the kneeling man with an expression Aldric couldn't read at this range, radiating an authority that needed no gesture to announce itself — the stillness of someone who had done this exact thing enough times before that it no longer required performance.

Aldric watched, unmoving, for longer than he could later account for.

He understood, distantly, that he was seeing something he wasn't meant to see — not forbidden, exactly, nothing about the scene suggested secrecy so much as simple distance, the ordinary privacy of an operation that had never expected an audience of one first-year on an errand. He understood, with the same distant clarity, that whatever this was connected, somehow, to the fragment he'd caught in a corridor two days ago — *capture operation, outside Valdris Prime, concluded as of this morning* — though the connection sat just beyond where he could actually reach it, a shape glimpsed through fog rather than a fact he could hold.

What he understood most clearly, and what he would turn over considerably longer than either of the other two things, was the man's face. Not the armored figure's — the kneeling man's. Ordinary. Frightened, in the specific, worn-down way of someone who had been frightened for a long time rather than newly, and underneath the fear, unmistakably, that same relief Aldric had clocked from the first moment — the relief of a man for whom capture was not the worst thing that could have happened, but very possibly the best available ending to whatever he'd been living through.

Aldric did not know his name. He would not learn it for some time yet. But he understood, watching from a hillside he had no business watching from, that whatever the kingdom believed it had just caught, it was not the kind of thing that belonged in the same category as a Greyback, or the vague, monstrous shape his own imagination had supplied before he'd actually seen it. It was a man. A frightened, ordinary, relieved man, kneeling in trampled grass while soldiers who did not seem to hate him did their duty regardless.

The operation concluded with no more drama than it had shown throughout. The soldiers formed up around the kneeling man and began the walk back toward wherever they'd come from; the armored figure watched them go for a long moment before turning, once, in a slow and unhurried arc that swept the treeline, the road, the rise where Aldric stood.

Aldric did not move. He was too far to be truly seen, he told himself — a single figure on a hillside, indistinguishable at this range from any other student out walking the lower road on a bright morning. The armored man's gaze passed over his position and did not stop there, continuing its slow sweep before the man turned away entirely and followed his soldiers into the trees, unhurried, exactly as unshowy in his leaving as he had been in everything else.

Aldric let out a breath he hadn't noticed holding.

He stood on the rise for a while longer, after the valley had emptied back into its ordinary morning quiet, turning over what he'd seen with the specific, unproductive thoroughness of a mind that knew it didn't yet have enough pieces to make anything of them. He thought of Daran, unbidden — of a promise made at a village crossroads two years and a lifetime ago, of a sword path that had once looked, from where Aldric stood, like the lesser of two roads. He thought that the man in white and iron-grey had not needed magic to make an entire valley go quiet around him, and that this, whatever else it meant, meant something about the shape of the world that Aldric hadn't fully accounted for before this morning.

He did not have the rest of the thought yet. He suspected, standing there, that he wouldn't have it for some time.

Eventually he continued down the road, because the letter in his pocket had not stopped being real just because the morning had gotten considerably larger than he'd planned for, and because Maren and Josse were owed their news regardless of what else had happened on the way to delivering it. The post house was exactly as unremarkable as he remembered it — a narrow, crowded room smelling of wax and old paper, a clerk who took his coin and his letter without any particular interest in either, a courier's book that would carry his few honest sentences toward Ashford at whatever pace the kingdom's roads allowed.

He watched the clerk seal the letter into the outgoing pouch, and felt, for one unguarded moment, the specific relief of having finally said a true thing to the two people who'd earned the truth more than anyone else alive.

Then he turned back toward the road, and the academy, and the walk uphill that would take considerably longer than the walk down had, and found that the valley behind him — empty now, ordinary, giving away nothing of what it had held an hour before — had left him with something he hadn't expected to carry home: the specific, settled certainty that whatever he'd just watched from a hillside was not a story that had finished. It was a story that had simply moved somewhere he couldn't currently follow it, and would, sooner or later, come looking for him instead.

## Draft

— [Orin Vael] —

The prisoner didn't fight, which was, in Orin's considerable experience, the detail that told you more than any amount of fighting would have.

He stood at the treeline's edge while the Stormwake detachment finished securing the man — competent work, economical, the kind of clean operation that would read, in tomorrow's report, as though it had never been in any doubt. It had been in doubt. Orin had spent enough years reading the difference between an operation that had gone well and an operation that had simply avoided going badly to know exactly which kind this one was, and the man kneeling in the trampled grass, hands bound, shoulders loose with something that looked considerably more like relief than defeat, was the reason it had landed on the better side of that line.

"Sir." The young captain who'd led the detachment — Aris, competent, ambitious in the specific way that made her useful rather than tiresome — arrived at his shoulder with the particular brisk energy of someone who'd done her job well and wanted it noted. "Secured without incident. He surrendered before we'd finished forming the ring."

"I saw."

"He hasn't said a word. Not his name, not a denial, nothing." She said it the way soldiers said most things that puzzled them — as a fact first, an opinion only if pressed. "I've handled men who talked too much and men who talked too little. I've never handled one who looked that pleased about being caught."

"You will again, eventually. It's not as rare as you think." Orin didn't elaborate, because elaborating would have meant explaining twelve years of his own history in a sentence too short to do it justice, and Aris hadn't asked for the sentence, only the observation. A man who'd been carrying something heavier than a crime for long enough sometimes greeted the end of it like a mercy, whatever waited on the other side. Orin had felt something close to it himself, once, with an arrow in his shoulder and four dead mages behind him and the king's own hand gripping his good arm hard enough to bruise. Relief didn't always require a happy ending. It only required the thing stopping.

He crossed the clearing at his own pace, unhurried, and crouched in front of the prisoner at a distance that put them roughly eye to eye.

"Drel Voss," he said, not a question. The Crown's investigators had matched the name to a face three weeks ago, from a description a farmer near Corvane had given after a sick mule recovered too fast and too completely to be anything but unnatural. It had taken this long to close the distance, which told its own story about how carefully the man had been running, and how little he'd actually wanted to be caught, right up until — evidently — this morning.

The man looked up. His eyes were an odd pale amber, striking even reddened with exhaustion, and Orin understood, in the way he understood most things about people within the first few seconds of actually looking at them, that whatever this man had done, he had not enjoyed doing it.

"I'm glad it's over," Voss said, and said nothing else, not then and not for the better part of an hour afterward, no matter what Orin asked him.

That was, in its own way, the entire case in miniature. Orin had read the file three times on the ride out — the Ashford incident two years past, a Greyback redirected with a precision that suggested intent rather than accident; two further sightings since, smaller, quieter, monsters nudged toward trouble they wouldn't otherwise have found; a pattern that had taken the kingdom's investigators the better part of two years to assemble into something they could finally put a name to. What the file did not contain, and what Orin had privately suspected it would never contain, was any explanation of *why* — why a man with Voss's evident discomfort, a man who by every account had spent his life gentling frightened animals rather than weaponizing them, would suddenly begin doing exactly that, repeatedly, at exactly the moments it caused the most damage.

He asked, eventually, the only question that actually mattered. "Who told you to do it?"

Voss's face did something complicated — not evasion, Orin thought, watching closely, but something closer to genuine grief. "I don't know."

"You don't know, or you won't say."

"I don't know." A long pause, the specific weighted silence of a man deciding whether a sentence was worth the effort of saying it to someone who'd probably never believe it regardless. "I never saw a face. Never heard a name. I'd feel it arrive, and then I'd feel myself do the thing, and there was no one standing there to blame for it, which I'm told doesn't sound like an excuse, and I'm not currently in a position to argue that it is one."

Orin had interrogated enough men over enough years to have a fairly reliable sense of which ones were lying to him, and Voss did not read as a man lying. He read as a man who had turned the same question over in his own head more times than Orin ever would, and had arrived, each time, at the same unsatisfying place. That unsettled Orin considerably more than a clean confession would have. A named enemy could be hunted. A hand with no face attached to it was a different kind of problem entirely, and Orin had a soldier's plain, unsentimental respect for the difference.

"You'll be asked that question a great many more times before this is finished," he said, rising.

"I know." Voss didn't look particularly troubled by the prospect. "I'll give the same answer every time. It has the considerable advantage of being true." A faint, exhausted ghost of a smile crossed his face. "You'll forgive me if I don't apologize for how unsatisfying that must be to write in a report."

"I've written less satisfying reports than this one will be."

"Now that," Voss said, "I believe without reservation."

It was, Orin reflected, walking back toward the treeline, almost a joke — the specific, exhausted gallows humor of a man who'd run out of better registers to speak in. He found, somewhat to his own surprise, that he didn't dislike Voss for it. Twenty years in the kingdom's service had taught him that villains came in considerably fewer varieties than victims did, and that the line between the two was, more often than the Council's official language liked to admit, a matter of which hand had reached in first.

He was midway back to where his own escort waited, boots crossing the ordinary trampled grass of an ordinary valley, when something at the edge of his vision resolved into a shape that didn't belong to the operation.

A figure, alone, on the low rise at the valley's western edge, well beyond the perimeter his soldiers had bothered to secure. Too far for detail. White hair, unmistakable even at this distance, catching the morning light in a way no ordinary color would have. A student, by the look of the uniform, standing exactly still in the particular way of someone who understood he shouldn't be watching and had decided to watch anyway.

Orin studied him for the length of perhaps four breaths — long enough to note the stillness, the distance, the fact that the boy hadn't run at being noticed, which told him something small and not entirely unadmirable about the boy's nerve. Greyveil sat close enough to this stretch of road that a student on some errand or another wasn't, on its own, anything worth remarking. Boys wandered. Boys watched things they had no business watching. It was, on the balance of twenty years' experience reading exactly this kind of unremarkable curiosity, nothing.

He turned back toward his escort and did not think of it again.

The ride back to the garrison outside Valdris Prime took the better part of the afternoon, and Orin spent most of it in the particular unproductive silence of a man turning a problem over without expecting the turning to produce anything new. Aris rode beside him for a stretch, tactful enough not to fill the quiet with questions he hadn't invited, and it was nearly dusk before either of them spoke again.

"The Crown will want this closed quickly," she said, eventually. "Clean capture. Confession, more or less. It reads well."

"It reads well," Orin agreed, "because it's being read by people who want it to."

"You don't think it's closed."

"I think we've caught the hand and not the arm." He said it flatly, the way he said most things he'd already turned over enough times to stop dressing up for an audience. "A man who can't tell you who controlled him isn't a resolution. He's a loose end that happens to be standing still long enough to look tied off."

Aris didn't argue the point, which he took as her own quiet agreement rather than deference — she was too good an officer to simply defer, and he'd have thought less of her if she had. "The Council will want you at the reading tomorrow. In the armor. They like the armor."

"They like the armor because it photographs well and doesn't ask them follow-up questions." He said it without particular bitterness — twelve years had worn the bitterness down to something closer to professional observation. "I've never once been invited to a Council session for my conversation."

"You could try improving it."

"I've considered it. The armor's cheaper." It wasn't much of a joke, and he knew it, but Aris huffed something close to a laugh anyway, which he counted as a fair exchange for the effort. He had never been a man who needed his humor to land hard to be worth spending; a captain's small, tired laugh at the end of a long ride was its own kind of currency, and he'd learned, over two decades in rooms full of people who addressed him with considerably more formality than warmth, not to spend it carelessly.

"The report will say closed regardless."

"The report will say a great many things this year that aren't precisely true. It's not the first, and I doubt it'll be the last." He didn't say it bitterly. Twelve years at the Council's uncomfortable center had taught him the specific difference between an institution lying and an institution simply preferring a tidy sentence to an honest paragraph, and he had made a private peace, years ago, with which of the two he was usually asked to help write.

He signed the closure himself that evening, in a garrison office too small for the number of candles it required, because someone with sufficient rank had to and because he trusted his own signature on the document considerably more than he trusted whichever clerk might otherwise have been handed it. *Investigation concluded. Subject in custody. No further threat identified at this time.* It was, every word of it, accurate. It was also, in the specific way only a soldier who'd spent a career reading between exactly these kinds of lines could recognize, not remotely the same thing as true.

He thought, setting his pen down, of the boy on the rise — an idle thought, unconnected to anything useful, the kind his mind occasionally produced at the end of a long day the way a hearth produces the last stray spark after the fire's mostly banked. White hair. Standing his ground rather than running. It meant nothing. He was fairly certain it meant nothing, and gave it no more weight than a long career had taught him most such things deserved — noted, unremarked, gone from his thinking by the time his boots found the road.

Somewhere beneath that certainty, in the part of him that had learned, twelve years ago in a palace corridor with an arrow in his shoulder, that the moments which mattered most rarely announced themselves as mattering at the time they happened, something declined to fully agree.

The investigation was closed. He had signed it himself. He did not, sealing the report and setting it in the outgoing tray for the morning courier, believe for one moment that it was.
